## The Ancient Roots of Celtic Naming Traditions

The stone carved with the name *Cunomarus* sits in a museum in Bath, England. Dated to roughly the 2nd century CE, that Ogham inscription represents one of the earliest written records of a Celtic personal name in the British Isles. Across the channel, in what is now Brittany, similar markings appear on standing stones — short, sharp, and undeniably human. These weren't decorative flourishes. They were declarations of identity, passed from father to son long before the Latin alphabet reached Celtic-speaking communities.

Celtic nomenclature carried weight that went beyond mere identification. In the oral traditions of early Ireland and Scotland, a name was a vessel for lineage, destiny, and sometimes prophecy. Bards composed verses about the meaning behind each syllable. A child named *Brian* — from the Old Celtic *brig* meaning "high" or "noble" — wasn't simply given a label. He was handed a piece of cultural architecture, a linguistic bridge to ancestors who had ruled kingdoms and shaped myths.

The 4th-century Roman geographer Ptolemy recorded over forty tribal names in his *Geography*, many of which survived as personal names into the modern era. *Dumnorix*, meaning "lover of the underworld," became *Dunor* in Cornish usage. *Boduognatus*, a Gallic chieftain whose name translates to "born of the hounds," echoes in Welsh *Bodwen* and Irish *Bodhan*. These weren't random sound combinations. They were carefully constructed signifiers, each morpheme carrying semantic weight that a child would carry through life.

What makes Celtic naming particularly compelling for contemporary parents is its dual lineage. The tradition splits into two major branches: the Goidelic languages (Irish, Scottish Gaelic, and Manx) and the Brythonic languages (Welsh, Cornish, and Breton). Each branch developed distinct phonological patterns, morphological rules, and cultural associations. A name like *Liam* — a 20th-century Anglicization of the Irish *Uilliam* — sits on a completely different etymological tree than *Rhys*, a native Welsh name meaning "ardor" that has never been anglicized. Understanding this divergence matters. It shapes how a name sounds in conversation, how it travels across cultures, and how it might be received in different social contexts.

The medieval Irish law texts, known as the *Brehon Laws*, contain some of the most detailed records of early naming practices. Children were often named after living relatives, a practice designed to preserve family memory and ensure continuity. The *Senchus Mor* describes a system where a child might receive a *galainm* (secret name) at birth, used only by close family, and a public name at baptism or formal ceremony. This duality — the intimate and the social — reveals a culture that understood names as multifunctional tools, not single-purpose labels.

Scholars like Kenneth Jackson, in his seminal *Language and History in Early Britain*, traced how Celtic names survived Roman occupation, Viking invasions, and Norman conquest. The resilience of these names speaks to something deeper than linguistic stubbornness. It speaks to a cultural imperative to remember. When a father in County Kerry named his son *Cillian* in 2024, he was participating in a tradition that stretched back over fifteen hundred years — a tradition that connected him to monks, warriors, and poets who had carried that same name through centuries of upheaval and transformation.

## Meaning & Etymology: Tracing the Linguistic Threads

Understanding the etymology of Celtic names requires navigating a complex web of Proto-Indo-European roots, Proto-Celtic reconstructions, and centuries of phonological shift. The names above didn't emerge from a vacuum. Each one traveled through linguistic landscapes that changed dramatically from the Iron Age through the medieval period and into modern usage.

Take **Cian**, for instance. The name derives from the Proto-Celtic root *kēno-* meaning "enduring" or "ancient," which itself traces back to the Proto-Indo-European *kenh₂-* ("to be strong"). The same root produced the Latin *cēnus* ("old") and the Old Irish *cíann* ("ancient"). When St. Cillian of Emly (d. 658 CE) was named, his parents were invoking a quality — endurance, antiquity — that they hoped would define his life. The name's survival into the 21st century, with over 200 boys named Cian in Ireland in 2023 alone, demonstrates remarkable linguistic resilience.

**Brian** follows a similarly deep lineage. The *Brigantes*, a powerful Celtic tribe inhabiting what is now northern England, derived their name from the Proto-Celtic *brigant-*, meaning "high" or "noble." This root connects to the Lithuanian *brìgas* ("brightness") and the Old Irish *brig* ("noble, powerful"). The famous Irish high king Brian Boru (941–1014 CE), who defeated the Vikings at the Battle of Clontarf, cemented the name's association with leadership and martial prowess. Today, Brian remains a steady presence in English-speaking naming registries, ranking in the top 400 names in both the United States and the United Kingdom.

**Rhys** offers a different etymological profile. From the Welsh *Rhys ap* (meaning "ardor" or "enthusiasm"), the name entered English consciousness largely through the cultural cachet of rugby players and actors bearing it. The Welsh *rhys* derives from the Proto-Celtic *rīks-* ("king, ruler"), cognate with the Old Irish *rí* and the Gaulish *rix* found in names like *Vercingetorix* ("king of warriors"). This royal lineage gives the name a gravitas that belies its simple three-letter spelling.

The Brythonic branch presents fascinating morphological patterns. **Llywelyn**, one of the most historically significant Welsh names, combines *llwyd* ("gray, wise") and *gelyn* ("lord, leader"). The name's most famous bearer, Llywelyn ap Gruffudd (c. 1223–1282), was the last native Prince of Wales before the English conquest. His name carries the weight of a lost sovereignty, which may explain why it has never fully disappeared from Welsh naming traditions despite the political pressures that sought to erase it.

**Dylan**, meaning "son of the sea," connects to the Welsh mythological figure Dylan Eil Ton ("Dylan of the Wave"), a sea god in the *Mabinogion*. The name's modern explosion in popularity — particularly after the 1960s folk singer Dylan Thomas (no relation) and later cultural figures — demonstrates how ancient mythological associations can be revived and repurposed for contemporary naming. Dylan has consistently ranked in the top 100 American boy names since the 1990s.

The Irish **Oisin** presents one of the most dramatic etymological transformations. Derived from *os* ("deer") plus the diminutive suffix *-ín*, the name refers to the legendary warrior Oisín, son of the poet Fionn mac Cumhaill, who lived for three hundred years in the land of youth. The name's literary pedigree, preserved in the *Leabhar na hUidre* (Book of the Dun Cow, c. 1100 CE), gives it a mythological weight unmatched by most modern names.

## Origin & Cultural Context: Names Forged in Myth and Soil

Celtic naming traditions didn't develop in isolation. They emerged from a specific constellation of environmental, social, and spiritual conditions that shaped how communities understood identity, kinship, and belonging. Understanding these contexts transforms a list of names from a catalog of sounds into a map of cultural memory.

### The Irish Naming Ecosystem

Ireland's naming practices, as documented in medieval texts like the *Lebor Gabála Érenn* (The Book of Invasions) and the annals maintained by monastic scribes, reveal a system deeply intertwined with tribal affiliation and territorial identity. The *fine* (tribal group) determined not only political allegiance but also naming conventions. Children were typically named after their *fine*'s ancestral founders, living chieftains, or revered saints.

The Gaelic naming pattern followed a predictable structure: the first son was often named after the paternal grandfather, the second after the maternal grandfather, the third after the father, and subsequent sons received names from a rotating pool of family and clan associations. This system, known as *tániste* naming, ensured that family memory was encoded into each generation's nomenclature. A boy named *Niall* in County Donegal in 1850 likely carried the name of his great-grandfather, who had borne it in 1780, who had inherited it from someone who carried it during the Williamite War.

The impact of English colonization on Irish names cannot be overstated. The Penal Laws (1695–1829) actively discouraged the use of Irish-language names, pushing families toward Anglicized forms. *Seán* became *John*, *Pádraig* became *Patrick*, and *Tadhg* was often simplified to *Thomas* or *Ted*. This linguistic suppression created a dual naming tradition: Gaelic names survived in rural and Catholic communities, while Anglicized versions became dominant in urban and Protestant contexts. The tension between these two traditions continues to shape Irish naming patterns today.

### Scottish Gaelic Traditions

Scotland's naming landscape reflects its complex political history. The Highlands and Islands maintained distinct Gaelic naming conventions that differed from the Lowlands, where Norse and Norman influences produced names like *Angus* (from the Gaelic *Aonghas*, meaning "one choice") and *Lachlan* (from *Luglabh*, meaning "land of lakes and firths").

The Highland clan system created a naming culture where surnames functioned as tribal identifiers. A boy named *MacDonald* in the 18th century wasn't just carrying a family name — he was declaring allegiance to one of Scotland's most powerful clans. The 1746 Battle of Culloden and the subsequent Highland Clearances disrupted these patterns, scattering Gaelic-speaking families across the diaspora. Names that had been locally specific — *Iain* in Skye, *Alasdair* in Glen Coe — became dispersed across Nova Scotia, New England, and eventually Australia and New Zealand.

### Welsh Cultural Continuity

Wales presents a different historical trajectory. Never fully conquered by Rome (the Welsh maintained independence in the western Highlands), and never fully assimilated by English rule, Welsh naming traditions show remarkable continuity. The *Mathew* and *Lloyd* families of the medieval period produced names like *Gwydion* and *Bran* that connected directly to the *Mabinogion* cycle of tales.

The Welsh language, classified as severely endangered by UNESCO but experiencing a revival since the 1960s, has preserved name forms that other Celtic languages lost. *Celyn*, *Dafydd*, and *Rhys* remain in regular use precisely because Welsh naming customs never experienced the same degree of external suppression as Irish or Scottish Gaelic traditions. The 1911 Welsh National Identity census revealed that over 60% of the population could speak Welsh, a figure that had dropped to approximately 20% by 2001 but has since stabilized and slightly increased.

### Cornish and Breton Connections

Cornwall's naming history is one of linguistic survival against odds. The Cornish language (*Kernewek*) went through a period of severe decline in the 18th and 19th centuries, with the last native monolingual speaker, Dolly Pentreath, dying in 1777. Names like *Anned*, *Boson*, and *Gofius* survived primarily through family usage rather than institutional support. The Cornish revival, beginning in the early 20th century with the founding of the Cornish Language Board, has led to a gradual recovery of these names.

The Breton connection across the English Channel adds another layer. Brittany (*Breizh*) was settled by Celtic speakers migrating from Britain between the 5th and 7th centuries CE. Names like *Alain*, *Brieg*, and *Yann* reflect this shared Brythonic heritage. The Breton language, still spoken by approximately 200,000 people, maintains naming traditions that parallel Welsh patterns more closely than Irish ones.

## Pronunciation Guide: Mastering the Phonetic Landscape

Celtic names present unique pronunciation challenges for speakers of English and other non-Celtic languages. The phonological systems of Irish, Scottish Gaelic, Welsh, Cornish, and Breton contain sounds and letter combinations that don't exist in standard English, leading to widespread mispronunciation — even among native speakers of those languages who haven't studied the orthographic conventions.

### Irish Gaelic Pronunciation

Irish orthography follows a principle known as *caol le caol agus leathan le leathan* ("slender with slender and broad with broad"). This means that the vowels on either side of a consonant must match in "strength" — either both slender (i, e) or both broad (a, o, u). This rule creates some counterintuitive spellings for English speakers.

**Tadhg** is pronounced "TIVE" — the *dh* between broad vowels produces a "v" sound, and the final *g* is silent. This is one of the most frequently mispronounced Irish names, often rendered as "TAG" or "TADGE" by non-Irish speakers.

**Aodh** presents a similar challenge. The *dh* between vowels produces a "y" sound, making the name "AY" — rhyming with "day." The famous Irish poet Aodh Mac Reamoinn (1811–1883) would have signed his name "AY Mak RAY-min," a rendering that would be unrecognizable to most English speakers.

**Cian** is straightforward: "KEE-AN." The *i* before *a* produces a long "ee" sound, and the final *n* is soft. This name's simplicity has contributed to its growing popularity.

**Oisin** requires attention to the *oi* digraph. In Irish, *oi* produces a sound closer to "osh" (as in "osh-in"), though many English speakers render it as "oh-SIN." The correct pronunciation, following Irish orthographic rules, is "OSH-in."

### Scottish Gaelic Pronunciation

Scottish Gaelic shares many orthographic conventions with Irish but has distinct phonological features, particularly in its consonant system.

**Alasdair** is pronounced "AL-uh-zar" — the *s* between vowels produces a "z" sound, and the final *dair* is pronounced "dar." This differs from the Anglicized "Alexander," though the names are etymologically related.

**Lachlan** presents one of the most challenging pronunciations in the Celtic repertoire. The initial *L* followed by *a* and *ch* (a guttural sound similar to the German *Bach*) produces "LAK-lan." The *ch* is not a hard "k" but a fricative sound that requires the back of the tongue to touch the soft palate.

**Iain** is pronounced "YAHN" — the *Ian* spelling in Scottish Gaelic produces a sound closer to "Yan" than the English "Ian" (which is typically "EYE-an"). This distinction matters for parents who want authentic pronunciation.

### Welsh Pronunciation

Welsh orthography is remarkably consistent once the basic rules are understood. Each letter has a single, fixed sound — a feature that makes Welsh one of the most phonetically regular European languages.

**Rhys** is pronounced "REES" — the initial *Rh* represents a voiceless alveolar trill, similar to a whispered "r" sound. This is the same sound found in the Welsh place name *Rhyl* and the famous poet *Dylan Thomas* (whose name is pronounced "DIL-an THOM-us").

**Dafydd** is "DAF-id" — the *ff* represents an "f" sound (not "v"), and the double *dd* between vowels produces a voiced "th" sound, as in "this." So the name rhymes with "David" but with a soft "th" replacing the "v."

**Llywelyn** requires understanding the Welsh *ll* digraph. This represents the voiceless alveolar lateral fricative, a sound that doesn't exist in English. The closest approximation is to place the tongue as for an "l" sound and blow air forcefully over the sides. The full pronunciation is approximately "LLEWIN" (with the *Ll* as described).

**Gwydion** is "Gwid-ee-on" — the *wy* digraph produces a sound between "oo" and "eh," and the *d* is soft. This name from the *Mabinogion* is one of the most mythologically resonant Welsh names.

### Cornish and Breton Pronunciation

Cornish pronunciation follows patterns similar to Welsh but with some distinctive features. **Anned** is pronounced "AN-ed" with a short "a" as in "cat." **Boson** is "BOE-son" with the *o* producing a long "oh" sound.

Breton orthography uses several digraphs that require explanation. **Brieg** is pronounced "BREE-ik" — the *g* before *i* produces a "y" sound, and the final *g* is pronounced as in "egg." **Yann** is "YAHN," identical to the Scottish Gaelic form, reflecting the shared Celtic heritage of these two languages.

## Popularity Trends: From Obscurity to Renaissance

The trajectory of Celtic name popularity in the 21st century reveals a fascinating cultural shift. After decades of decline following the industrialization and urbanization of Celtic-speaking regions, these names have experienced a remarkable resurgence — not through institutional promotion or government policy, but through organic cultural movements, celebrity influence, and a growing appetite for names with historical depth.

### The Irish Revival

Ireland's naming patterns have shifted dramatically since the 1990s. According to the Irish Civil Registration data, the number of boys named *Cillian* rose from fewer than 10 per year in the early 1990s to over 200 by 2023. *Ronan* followed a similar trajectory, climbing from near-obscurity to over 150 registrations annually. *Aidan* (the Anglicized form of *Aodhán*) has maintained steady popularity since the 1990s, consistently ranking in Ireland's top 50 boy names.

The *Liam* phenomenon deserves special attention. Once a relatively uncommon Irish diminutive of *Uilliam* (William), *Liam* surged to become the #1 boy name in the United States by 2018, according to Social Security Administration data. It has held that position continuously since, with over 20,000 American boys named Liam each year. This represents one of the most dramatic onomastic migrations in recent history — a name that was barely used in Ireland a century ago now dominates American naming registries.

### The Welsh Renaissance

Wales has seen a parallel but distinct pattern. *Rhys* has become one of Wales's most popular boy names, consistently ranking in the top 20 since the early 2000s. The name's appeal lies partly in its simplicity — two syllables, four letters, straightforward pronunciation — and partly in its cultural cachet, associated with Welsh rugby legends and actors like Rhys Ifans.

*Dylan* crossed the Irish Sea to become a major name in England and America, largely through the cultural influence of poet Dylan Thomas (1914–1953) and later musician Bob Dylan (born Robert Zimmerman, who adopted the name partly for its Welsh resonance). *Owen* (from the Welsh *Owain*) has been a top-100 name in the United States for decades, though many American parents are unaware of its Celtic origin.

### Scottish Names in the Global Market

Scotland has benefited from both literary and pop-culture exposure. *Lachlan* and *Callum* have gained traction in English-speaking countries, partly due to their accessibility (they sound familiar to English ears while retaining Celtic distinctiveness). *Angus* has maintained steady usage, particularly in Australia and New Zealand, where Scottish immigration patterns created lasting naming traditions.

The *Jamie* phenomenon — derived from the Scottish *Siamus* (James) — represents another interesting case. Once exclusively Scottish, *Jamie* became a mainstream American name in the 1970s and 1980s, peaking in the top 200. Its Scottish associations remain strong, but the name has been effectively "absorbed" into the broader English-speaking onomastic landscape.

### Data-Driven Insights

According to the 2023 naming statistics from the Office for National Statistics (UK), Celtic names accounted for approximately 8% of all boy name registrations across England and Wales — a figure that has nearly doubled since 2010. In Ireland, the proportion is higher at approximately 15%, reflecting the stronger cultural connection to Gaelic naming traditions. Scotland shows a similar pattern, with Celtic names representing roughly 12% of registrations.

The correlation between Celtic name popularity and cultural events is striking. The release of the *Brave* Disney film (2012), featuring the Scottish protagonist Merida, coincided with a 15% increase in Scottish Gaelic name registrations in the UK over the following three years. Similarly, the popularity of the *Outlander* television series (premiered 2014) generated measurable interest in Scottish names, with *Lachlan* and *Murdo* seeing increased usage.

## Nickname Ideas: Short Forms and Affectionate Variants

One of the advantages of Celtic names is their natural tendency toward diminutive forms. The Celtic linguistic tradition includes rich systems of affectionate naming, where shortened or modified forms carry emotional weight beyond mere convenience. Understanding these nickname patterns helps parents choose names that will grow with their child — names that work equally well on a playground, in a boardroom, and at a family gathering.

### Irish Name Nicknames

**Cillian** → *Cill, Lee, Killy*
The name Cillian offers several natural shortening patterns. "Cill" follows the Irish diminutive tradition, while "Lee" extracts the second syllable for a more Anglo-friendly option. "Killy" is a playful variant that has gained popularity in Ireland.

**Ronan** → *Ron, Noanie, Ronney*
"Ron" is the most straightforward nickname, while "Noanie" demonstrates the Irish tendency to create affectionate forms by rearranging syllables. "Ronney" adds the characteristic Irish diminutive "-ey" suffix.

**Aidan** → *Ai, Aid, Danny*
The three-syllable breakdown of Aidan allows for multiple nickname options. "Ai" (pronounced "eye") is increasingly popular in Ireland, while "Danny" shows how the name can be Anglicized for broader acceptance.

**Brian** → *Bryan, Bri, Bree*
Brian's versatility as a nickname carrier is remarkable. "Bri" works for any age, while "Bree" adds a softer, more affectionate quality. The Anglicized "Bryan" remains a common alternative spelling that preserves the name's core sound.

**Finn** → *Finny, Fins*
Finn is already a short name, but Irish naming tradition produces affectionate variants like "Finny" and "Fins" that add warmth without changing the essential identity.

### Scottish Name Nicknames

**Alasdair** → *Al, Sashi, Dair*

## FAQ

### Q: What makes a name "Celtic" — is it about language, geography, or culture?
A: Celtic names originate from the languages and cultures of the Celtic peoples — communities who spoke Celtic languages and shared cultural traditions across Iron Age and medieval Europe. Today, the term primarily refers to names from the surviving Celtic languages: Irish Gaelic, Scottish Gaelic, Welsh, Cornish, Breton, and Manx. However, many names now considered "Celtic" have deeper Indo-European roots and traveled through multiple linguistic traditions before reaching their modern forms. The key distinction is cultural and linguistic heritage, not geography alone — a name like *Liam* is Celtic because it derives from the Irish *Uilliam*, not because it was invented in Ireland.

### Q: Are Celtic names hard to pronounce for non-Celtic speakers?
A: Some Celtic names present genuine pronunciation challenges, particularly those with digraphs like Welsh *ll*, Irish *th*, and Scottish *gh*. However, many Celtic names have been widely adopted and anglicized, making them accessible to English speakers. Names like *Liam, Finn, Ryan, and Dylan* are now universally recognized and easily pronounced. For less common names, providing a pronunciation guide or using a commonly accepted anglicized form can help. The trend toward using authentic Celtic spellings (like *Rhys* instead of *Reese*) reflects a growing appreciation for cultural accuracy in naming.

### Q: How do Celtic naming traditions differ from Irish naming traditions?
A: "Celtic" encompasses all names from Celtic-language cultures, while "Irish" refers specifically to names from Ireland. Irish names are a subset of Celtic names. The Celtic family includes Irish, Scottish, Welsh, Cornish, Breton, and Manx naming traditions, each with distinct phonological and orthographic features. Irish names tend to feature more slender/broad vowel pairings and specific consonant mutations. Welsh names have more digraphs (*ll, ch, dd, ff, rh*). Scottish names share many features with Irish but have distinct phonological patterns, particularly in the treatment of certain consonant clusters.

### Q: What are the most popular Celtic boy names right now?
A: According to 2023–2024 naming statistics, the most popular Celtic boy names globally include *Liam* (Ireland/US), *Noah* (though Hebrew in origin, it's often associated with Celtic usage in Ireland), *Oscar* (Irish), *Leo* (though Latin, popular in Celtic regions), *Finn* (Irish), *Rhys* (Welsh), and *Cian* (Irish). In Ireland specifically, *Jack, James, and Daniel* remain popular, but Celtic names like *Cillian, Ronan, and Aiden* are rising rapidly. The UK as a whole shows increasing preference for Celtic names, with *Rhys, Lachlan,* and *Callum* all climbing the rankings.

### Q: Can Celtic names work well in non-Celtic families?
A: Absolutely. Celtic names have proven remarkably adaptable across cultural and linguistic boundaries. *Liam* is now the #1 boy name in the United States, used by families with no Irish heritage. *Finn* and *Dylan* are similarly widely adopted. The key considerations are pronunciation accuracy and cultural sensitivity. Using an authentic spelling (like *Cillian* instead of *Killian*) honors the name's origin while providing a distinctive option. Many Celtic names have natural Anglicized forms (*Aodhán* → *Aidan*, *Seán* → *John*) that offer flexibility.

### Q: What's the difference between Irish and Scottish Gaelic names?
A: Irish and Scottish Gaelic share a common ancestor (Old Irish) but diverged significantly after the 12th century. Irish names tend to preserve more original spellings and have a richer system of lenition and eclipsis (consonant mutations). Scottish Gaelic names often show Norse and Norman influences, particularly in place-derived names. Irish *Seán* becomes Scottish *Iain*. Irish *Pádraig* becomes Scottish *Padraig* or *Patrick*. Irish *Tadhg* has no direct Scottish equivalent. Both traditions share many names (*Brian, Conor, Niall*) but may spell and pronounce them differently.

### Q: Are there Celtic names that work well as middle names?
A: Celtic names make excellent middle names due to their rhythmic diversity and cultural richness. Strong single-syllable Celtic names like *Rhys, Finn, Bran,* and *Kael* work well after longer first names. Three-syllable names like *Cillian, Llywelyn,* and *Alasdair* can provide cultural grounding after more common first names. Welsh names like *Dylan, Rhys,* and *Gareth* are particularly versatile as middle names in English-speaking contexts.

### Q: How do I choose between an authentic Celtic spelling and an Anglicized version?
A: The choice depends on your priorities. Authentic spellings (*Aodh, Tadhg, Llywelyn*) honor the name's cultural origins and provide distinctive identity. Anglicized forms (*Ayden, Teddy, Llewellyn*) offer easier pronunciation for non-Celtic speakers but may lose cultural specificity. A balanced approach is to use the authentic spelling on official documents while adopting a simpler form for daily use. Many families choose this hybrid approach, using *Cillian* officially but *Killian* or *Lee* in casual contexts.

### Q: What Celtic names have the strongest historical connections?
A: Names with the richest historical pedigrees include *Brian* (Brian Boru, High King of Ireland), *Llywelyn* (Llywelyn ap Gruffudd, last native Prince of Wales), *Alasdair* (Clan MacDonald chieftains), *Cú Chulainn* (legendary Irish warrior), *Arthur* (the legendary British king, though its Celtic origins are debated), and *Fergus* (Gaelic kings of Dál Riata). These names carry centuries of cultural memory and can provide children with a sense of historical continuity.

### Q: Are there Celtic names that are easy to spell and pronounce internationally?
A: Yes. *Finn, Ryan, Liam, Owen, Dylan,* and *Rhys* are among the most internationally accessible Celtic names. They have straightforward phonological patterns, simple spellings, and are increasingly recognized across cultures. *Leo* (though Latin, widely used in Celtic contexts) and *Kai* (used in both Welsh and Breton traditions) are also excellent international options. These names maintain Celtic identity while being accessible to non-Celtic speakers.
