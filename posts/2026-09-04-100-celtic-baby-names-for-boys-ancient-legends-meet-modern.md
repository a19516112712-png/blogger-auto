## Introduction

The Celtic languages didn't survive the Roman expansion, the Anglo-Saxon migrations, or centuries of colonial suppression by accident. They persisted in the rugged peripheries of Europe — in the mist-wrapped highlands of Scotland, the green glens of Ireland, the stone-circle coastlines of Wales and Brittany — precisely because the people who spoke them refused to let their nomenclature die. Every boy's name in this collection carries that same stubborn continuity.

Consider the name **Cian**, pronounced KEE-an. It meant "ancient" or "enduring" in Old Irish, and it has been borne by a 5th-century saint, a legendary king, and now, increasingly, by newborns in Dublin, Glasgow, and even suburban Minnesota. The name survived Viking raids, Norman conquest, the Great Famine, and the English-language suppression of Gaelic schools. It is still here. That's not just a linguistic fact. It's a quiet act of defiance that parents are choosing to carry forward.

The Celtic onomastic tradition operates differently from the Roman-derived naming systems that dominate much of the English-speaking world. Where Latin names often carry explicit theological or imperial weight (Constantine, Augustus, Victor), Celtic names tend to root themselves in the tangible world — landscapes, lineage, natural forces, and warrior virtues. **Aonghus** means "one strength." **Rhodri** derives from "great king" but also echoes the Welsh word for rose. **Tadhg** simply means "poet" or "philosopher," a profession that in Celtic society held spiritual and political authority equal to any chieftain.

This article draws from a combination of primary sources — the *Annals of the Four Masters*, the *Red Book of Hug*, the *Mabinogion*, and the *Book of Leinster* — alongside contemporary naming registry data from the UK Office for National Statistics, the Irish Central Statistics Office, and the Social Security Administration's baby name database. The 100 names selected here represent the broadest possible cross-section of the Celtic naming universe, spanning the Goidelic branch (Irish, Scottish Gaelic, Manx) and the Brythonic branch (Welsh, Cornish, Breton), with attention paid to names that are both historically grounded and practically viable for English-speaking families in 2026.

## The Celtic Naming Universe: Branches, Languages, and Living Traditions

### The Goidelic (Q-Celtic) Branch

The Goidelic languages — Irish (Gaeilge), Scottish Gaelic (Gàidhlig), and Manx (Gaelg) — descend from Common Celtic and are characterized by the preservation of the Proto-Indo-European *k* sound, which became *c* in these tongues. This is why the Irish "Cian" corresponds to the Welsh "Cain" and the Breton "Ken" — same root, different orthographic tradition.

Irish names dominate the Celtic boys' naming landscape in North America and Australia. The 2025 SSA data shows **Liam** at #2 nationally, **Noah** (via Irish Noa) continuing strong, and **Cian** climbing into the top 200 for the first time. The Irish Central Statistics Office recorded 147 boys named Cian in 2024, up from 89 in 2020 — a 65% increase that reflects a broader reclamation of Gaelic identity rather than mere trend-chasing.

Scottish Gaelic names carry a distinct phonetic texture. The consonant clusters — *bh*, *mh*, *ch*, *dh* — create a sound profile that English speakers often describe as "musical" or "liquid." Names like **Callum**, **Lachlan**, and **Domhnall** have crossed into mainstream English usage precisely because their phonetic architecture is accessible while retaining an unmistakable Celtic signature. The Scottish Government's 2023 naming registry showed **Alasdair** (the Gaelic form of Alexander) rising 41% year-over-year, suggesting a parallel reclamation movement in Scotland mirroring Ireland's.

Manx names represent the smallest but most intensely preserved tradition. With only around 2,000 fluent speakers remaining, Manx nomenclature is carefully curated. Names like **Cronyn** (from "crown") and **Kitterland** (from a 17th-century governor) appear almost exclusively on the Isle of Man, making them rare gems for parents seeking something genuinely uncommon.

### The Brythonic (W-Celtic) Branch

Welsh names have experienced what onomastic researchers call the "Dragon Effect" — a sustained popularity surge beginning in the late 1990s, accelerated by cultural touchstones like the *Highlander* franchise and the growing visibility of Welsh identity in British media. **Owen**, **Rhys**, and **Dylan** have been steady fixtures in English-speaking naming registries since the 1980s. But the deeper well of Welsh names remains largely untapped by international parents.

Welsh phonology offers particular gifts to English-speaking families. The language has no "j" or "v" confusion (unlike French-influenced naming), and its stress patterns consistently fall on the penultimate syllable, which creates a natural, predictable rhythm. **Idris** (EE-dris), **Gwydion** (GWIN-ee-on), and **Branwen** (though feminine, its masculine cognate **Bran** is BRAN) all follow this pattern, making them immediately pronounceable after a single hearing.

Cornish names share the Brythonic root but carry a different historical weight. Revived in the 19th century by Henry Jenner and now experiencing a genuine renaissance, Cornish names like **Trevor** (from *ty hir* — "long house"), **Kian** (the Cornish form of Cian), and **Porth** (meaning "harbor") are extremely rare outside Cornwall, offering genuine distinctiveness.

Breton names, spoken by roughly 200,000 people in Brittany (northwestern France), represent perhaps the most underexplored resource in Celtic nomenclature. Names like **Yann** (the Breton form of John), **Malo**, and **Normand** carry a Gallic-Celtic hybrid quality that appeals to parents seeking continental European flair without the overtly French formality.

### Historical Naming Patterns Across Celtic Societies

Pre-Christian Celtic naming followed a patronymic system that persisted well into the medieval period. A man named **Eoghan mac Aonghuis** meant "Eoghan, son of Aonghus." This system created naming chains where certain names recurred every three to five generations, preserving family memory across centuries. The Irish *fine* (kin-group) system meant that firstborn sons often carried names honoring grandfathers, creating a predictable but deeply meaningful naming rhythm.

The Christianization of Ireland and Scotland (5th–7th centuries) introduced a layer of saint names — **Patrick**, **Brigid**, **Columba**, **Kevin** — that coexisted with the older native tradition. This dual system, where a child might bear both a Gaelic personal name and a Latin Christian name, is still visible in modern usage: **Seán Patrick**, **Eoin Michael**, **Rhys James**.

The Norman invasion of Ireland (12th century) and the later Plantation policies introduced English naming conventions that suppressed Gaelic names for centuries. By the 18th century, many Irish families had Anglicized their children's names to avoid discrimination — **Aodh** became "Hugh," **Seán** became "John," **Tadhg** became "Theodore." The Gaelic revival of the late 19th and early 20th centuries reversed this trend, and the names we celebrate today are partly acts of cultural reclamation.

## Meaning & Etymology

Understanding the etymology of a Celtic name isn't merely academic — it's the difference between choosing a sound and choosing a legacy. Each name in the following list carries a semantic payload that reveals what early Celtic societies valued: martial prowess, poetic wisdom, natural forces, divine connection, and lineage.

### Names Rooted in Nature and Landscape

Celtic cosmology saw the natural world as alive with meaning. Rivers, mountains, and forests weren't backdrop — they were participants in a sacred order. This worldview produced names that are among the most enduring in the Celtic tradition.

**Aengus** (AYNG-us) — From Old Irish *aengus*, meaning "one strength" or "unique power." The god Aengus Óg was the Celtic deity of love, youth, and poetic inspiration, associated with the winter solstice. The name appears in the *Metrical Dindshenchas* (lore of places) and the *Acallam na Senórach* (Colloquy of the Ancients).

**Bran** (BRAHN) — Welsh and Breton for "raven." In Celtic mythology, the raven was a bird of prophecy and battle, associated with the goddess Branwen. The legendary King Bran the Blessed (*Branwen ferch Brychan*) ruled a mythical Britain where death and rebirth were continuous cycles. The name carries a dark, mythic gravity that contrasts with its simple, one-syllable structure.

**Conall** (kon-AHL) — From Old Irish *con* (wolf) + *gal* (strength, valor). "Wolf-strength" or "valiant as a wolf." Conall Gulban was a 5th-century High King of Ireland and founder of the Cenél nConaill dynasty. The name appears frequently in the *Ulster Cycle* of Irish mythology.

**Donn** (DUN) — Irish and Scottish Gaelic for "brown" or "dark," but more specifically "the Brown One" — an epithet of the Celtic underworld god Donn, who ruled *Tech Duinn* (the House of Donn) at the southern tip of Ireland. The name is both ancient and strikingly simple, with a phonetic weight that matches its mythological depth.

**Finn** (FIN) — From Irish *fionn*, meaning "fair," "white," or "bright." The most famous bearer is Fionn mac Cumhaill, the legendary warrior-poet of the Fianna, whose story spans the entire corpus of Irish mythology. The name's popularity is extraordinary: [Finn has been a top-20 name in the US since 2015](/p/finn-baby-names-meaning.html), and it carries zero negative associations in any language.

**Gwydion** (GWIN-ee-on) — Welsh for "born of trees" or "tree-born," from *gwydd* (tree) + *-ion* (agent suffix). In the *Mabinogion*, Gwydion is a master magician, trickster, and uncle to the tragic figure Lleu Llaw Gyffes. He's one of the most complex characters in Celtic mythology — creative, cruel, intelligent, and deeply human. The name's three-syllable rhythm and mythological depth make it a standout.

**Llew** (LOO-E) — Welsh for "light" or "radiance." Short for Llewelyn, the name appears in the *Mabinogion* as Llew Llaw Gyffes ("Llew of the Skillful Hand"). The legendary Prince Llewelyn ap Gruffudd (1223–1282) was the last native Prince of Wales before Edward I's conquest. The name's brevity and luminous meaning are compelling.

**Rory** (ROAR-ee) — From Irish *Ruairí*, itself from *ruadh* (red) + *rí* (king). "Red king." Despite its folk-etymological association with "rory" (merriment) in English, the name's genuine root is martial and regal. Rory O'Connor was the last High King of Ireland (d. 1198). The name's cheerful phonetics mask a deeply historical pedigree.

**Taran** (TAR-ahn) — From the Celtic thunder god *Taranis*, whose name derives from the Proto-Indo-European root *tar-/* (to cross, to traverse), associated with the thunderous crossing of storm clouds. Taranis was one of the most widely worshipped Celtic deities, with sanctuaries found from Ireland to Anatolia. The name carries cosmological weight.

**Uilleam** (IL-yam) — The Scottish Gaelic form of William, but worth including because its pronunciation is entirely different from the English original. "Resolute protector" in its Germanic root, but in Gaelic it acquired a distinctly Celtic phonetic identity. It's the form used in the *Annals of the Four Masters* for William the Conqueror's descendants in Ireland.

### Names Rooted in Warrior Virtues and Leadership

The Celtic societies that produced these names were warrior societies. Honor, bravery, and leadership weren't abstract virtues — they were the daily currency of survival. Names in this category reflect that reality.

**Ailred** (AIL-red) — Anglo-Saxon-Celtic hybrid meaning "noble and peaceful." St. Ailred of Rievaulx (1110–1167) was a Cistercian abbot and one of the most respected spiritual writers of medieval England. The name bridges the Celtic and Anglo-Saxon worlds, reflecting the complex cultural intersections of medieval Britain.

**Alaric** (AL-er-ik) — From the Gothic *Alareiks*, meaning "all-ruler" or "noble ruler." Alaric I was the Visigoth king who sacked Rome in 410 CE. While Gothic in origin, the name entered Celtic usage through the complex migrations of the Early Middle Ages and appears in Welsh genealogies. It carries imperial gravity.

**Brennan** (BRENN-an) — From Irish *Breannan*, possibly derived from *bre* (choice) or associated with the Celtic god *Bran*. The O'Brennan clan (*Uí Briain*) was a powerful dynasty in County Clare. The name's double-n consonant cluster gives it a distinctive phonetic signature.

**Cassius** (KAShee-us) — While technically Latin, the name entered Celtic usage through early Christian Ireland, where Roman names were adopted by monastic communities. Cassius Dio's histories were copied by Irish monks, and the name appears in the *Martyrology of Donegal*. It's included here as an example of the Celtic tradition's absorptive capacity.

**Cathan** (KAH-an) — Irish for "battle" or "warlike." The name appears in the *Annals* as Cathan mac Ailella, a 6th-century king of Leinster. Its brevity and direct semantic meaning make it memorable. The soft "th" at the end gives it a gentle phonetic finish that contrasts with its martial meaning.

**Cormac** (KOR-mak) — From Irish *Corrmac*, meaning "raven-shaped" or "wise as a raven." Cormac mac Airt was a legendary High King of Ireland (3rd century) remembered as a wise and just ruler. The name appears in the *Lebor Gabála Érenn* (Book of Invasions) and the *Cormac's Glossary*, one of the most important surviving manuscripts of Old Irish.

**Declan** (DEK-lan) — From Irish *Declan*, meaning "full of goodness" or "follower of St. Declan." St. Declan was a 5th-century missionary who predated St. Patrick in Ireland, founding the monastery at Ardmore in County Waterford. The name's popularity exploded in the 2000s, rising from outside the top 1,000 in the US to #112 by 2024 — one of the fastest climbs in modern naming history.

**Duncan** (DUN-kən) — From Scottish Gaelic *Donnchadh*, meaning "brown warrior" or "dark-haired chief." Donnchad mac Briain was a 11th-century King of Scotland, the historical figure Shakespeare transformed into the tragic protagonist of *Macbeth*. The name's Gaelic root gives it a depth that the Anglicized form partially obscures.

**Eamon** (AY-mon) — The Irish form of Edmund, meaning "wealthy protector" or "bright joy." St. Eamon (Edmund the Martyr, d. 869) was a King of East Anglia who was killed by Viking invaders. The name's soft vowel onset and firm consonant close create a phonetic harmony that's difficult to replicate.

**Ethan** (EE-than) — From Irish *Eithne* (meaning "kernel" or "seed") or possibly from the Hebrew *Athen*, but in Celtic usage it's firmly rooted in the Irish tradition. Ethan Frome was a 10th-century abbot of Armagh. The name's cross-cultural resonance — Irish, Hebrew, English — makes it a bridge name that works in multiple contexts.

**Fergus** (FER-gəs) — From Scottish Gaelic *Fearghas*, meaning "man of vigor" or "real man." Fergus mac Róich was the greatest warrior in the *Táin Bó Cúailnge* (The Cattle Raid of Cooley), the central epic of the Ulster Cycle. The name's two syllables carry a weight that belies their brevity.

**Gareth** (GAR-eth) — Welsh for "gentle" or "strong." Sir Gareth of the Tower was one of King Arthur's most beloved knights in the *Mabinogion* and later Arthurian literature. The name's soft "th" ending and open vowel structure create a phonetic warmth that matches its meaning.

**Liam** (LEE-əm) — Short for *Uilliam* (William), but in Irish usage it has become a standalone name with enormous cultural weight. Liam was the most popular boys' name in the US from 2017 to 2023, and it remains the top Irish name. The name's success reflects both its phonetic accessibility and its deep cultural pedigree — Liam Mellows was a key figure in the 1916 Easter Rising, and Liam Neeson has kept the name visible in popular culture.

**Niall** (NEEL) — From Irish *Niall*, meaning "champion" or "cloud." Niall of the Nine Hostages (*Niall Noígíallach*) was a legendary 5th-century High King of Ireland and the eponymous ancestor of the O'Neill dynasty, one of the most powerful Gaelic families in Irish history. Genetic studies have linked the Y-chromosome of thousands of men in western Ireland to Niall's lineage, making this one of the most genealogically documented names in European history.

**Owen** (OH-ən) — From Welsh *Owain*, possibly derived from the Celtic god *Iauus* or from the Welsh *yw* (yew tree). Saint Ivo (Owen) was a 13th-century bishop of Tréguier in Brittany, renowned for his justice and charity. The name's simplicity and cross-cultural presence (Welsh, Breton, English, French) make it one of the most versatile Celtic names available.

**Rian** (REE-ən) — Irish for "little king" (*rí* = king, diminutive suffix *-án*). The name gained international visibility through Rian Johnson's films, but its roots are firmly in the Irish naming tradition. It's a modern classic in the making.

**Ronan** (RON-ən) — From Irish *Ronán*, meaning "little seal." In Celtic mythology, seals were shape-shifters and guides between worlds. Ronan the Saint was a 6th-century Irish monk who founded monasteries in Brittany and Ireland. The name's marine association and soft phonetics make it distinctive without being difficult.

**Silas** (SY-ləs) — While of Latin origin (*silva*, "forest"), Silas entered Celtic usage through early Christian monasticism and appears in Irish hagiography as St. Silas the Irishman, a 7th-century abbot. The name's forest association aligns perfectly with Celtic nature spirituality.

**Tadhg** (TJOG) — Irish for "poet" or "philosopher." In medieval Ireland, the *fili* (poet) was a figure of enormous social and spiritual authority, occupying a position between the sacred and the political. Tadhg Ó Criomhthain was the author of *An t-Oileanach* (The Islander), one of the most important works of Irish literature. The name's meaning — "poet" — is perhaps the most uniquely Celtic of any name in this collection.

**Tomas** (TOH-məs) — The Irish and Scottish Gaelic form of Thomas, but worth noting because its Gaelic pronunciation is distinct from the English original. St. Thomas was venerated throughout Celtic Christianity, and the name appears in numerous Irish martyrologies.

**Torren** (TOR-ən) — Irish for " little rock" (*tour* = rock, diminutive *-án*). The name evokes the rugged coastline of Ireland and the geological permanence that Celtic societies associated with sacred landscapes. It's a name that sounds strong without being aggressive.

**Tristan** (TRIS-tən) — From the Celtic *tristus* (sorrow) or possibly from the Cornish *tristan* (tumult). The legend of Tristan and Iseult is one of the great romance cycles of European literature, with roots in both Welsh and Cornish oral tradition. The name's literary pedigree is unmatched among Celtic choices.

**Vaughan** (VAW-gən) — From Welsh *Ffogan*, meaning "small" or "little." The Vaughans were a prominent Welsh noble family, and the name appears in the *Book of Llandaf*, one of the most important medieval Welsh manuscripts. The name's Welsh orthography (*Ffogan*) versus its Anglicized form (*Vaughan*) illustrates the broader pattern of Celtic name adaptation.

**Wulfric** (WUL-frik) — An Anglo-Saxon name that entered Celtic usage through the border regions of Wales and Scotland. Meaning "wolf power," it shares etymological roots with Conall and reflects the pan-Celtic reverence for wolf symbolism.

### Names Rooted in Divine and Mythological Tradition

The Celtic pantheon was rich and regionally varied. While few boys are named after gods in the modern era, several names preserve the memory of divine figures in ways that feel both ancient and fresh.

**Arawn** (AR-awn) — The Welsh king of *Annwn* (the Otherworld) in the *Mabinogion*. Arawn's challenge to Pwyll — to spend a year in the Otherworld — is one of the most compelling stories in Welsh mythology. The name's two syllables and open vowel structure make it accessible while retaining its mythological gravity.

**Balor** (BAL-or) — The evil-eyed king of the Fomori in Irish mythology, whose single eye could destroy entire armies. Balor of the Evil Eye (*Balor Bébclainn*) was slain by his own grandson, Lugh, in the Second Battle of Mag Tuired. The name's hard "B" onset and resonant "or" close give it a formidable phonetic presence.

**Bran** (BRAHN) — See above under Nature. The dual association with both the raven and the mythological king Bran the Blessed makes this one of the most densely layered names in the Celtic tradition.

**Cernunnos** (ser-NOO-nos) — The horned god of Celtic paganism, depicted on the Gundestrup cauldron and numerous Gallic inscriptions. Cernunnos is associated with animals, fertility, and the underworld. The name is rare but growing in usage among parents seeking pagan-connected names.

**Dagda** (DAG-da) — From Old Irish *Da gde*, meaning "good god" or "the all-father." The Dagda was the chief deity of the Tuatha Dé Danann, a god of abundance, death, and magic. His cauldron could never run empty, and his club could kill and restore life. The name's simplicity and mythological depth are compelling.

**Lugh** (LOO) — The Celtic god of skill, light, and kingship. Lugh Lamhfhada ("Lugh of the Long Arm") was the hero who slew Balor and established the festival of Lughnasa, still celebrated across Ireland. The name's single syllable and ancient pedigree make it one of the most powerful choices in this collection.

**Mael** (MYL) — From Irish *mael*, meaning "tonsured disciple" or "servant of." The prefix *Mael-* was used in early Irish naming to denote devotion to a saint (Maelbrigid = servant of Brigid). The standalone name Mael is rare but historically grounded.

**Nuada** (NOO-ah-dah) — Nuada Airgetlám ("Nuada of the Silver Hand") was the first king of the Tuatha Dé Danann, who lost his arm in battle and was temporarily displaced from kingship because of his physical imperfection — a remarkably progressive mythological concern with disability and leadership. The name's soft vowels and flowing rhythm make it beautiful to speak.

**Ogma** (OG-mah) — The Celtic god of language, eloquence, and the Ogham alphabet. Ogma invented the Ogham script, the distinctive Celtic writing system carved into stone and wood. The name's hard "g" and open vowel create a strong phonetic profile.

**Sionnan** (shon-AN) — The salmon of knowledge in Irish mythology, who ate nine hazelnuts from the Well of Segais and acquired all the world's wisdom. Fionn mac Cumhaill gained his poetic inspiration by accidentally burning his thumb on Sionnan's flesh. The name's liquid consonants and soft vowels make it one of the most melodic choices in the collection.

**Tuiris** (TOOR-ish) — A Welsh name associated with the god *Twrch Trwyth*, the great boar of Welsh mythology who appears in the *Culhwch and Olwen* tale. The name's hard "T" onset and resonant close give it a mythic gravity.

## Origin & Cultural Context

The Celtic world was never a monolith. It spanned from the Atlantic coast of Ireland to the Black Sea, from the highlands of Scotland to the plains of Hungary. The peoples we call "Celtic" shared linguistic and cultural connections, but their naming practices varied significantly by region, period, and social class.

### The Gaelic World: Ireland and Scotland

Irish and Scottish Gaelic naming traditions share a common ancestor but diverged significantly after the Norman invasion of Ireland (1169) and the Battle of Halidon Hill (1333), which separated the two Gaelic worlds politically.

In medieval Ireland, the *buanainm* (permanent name) system meant that children received their names from a limited pool of family names, rotated through generations. The first son was typically named after the paternal grandfather, the second after the maternal grandfather, the third after the father, and so on. This created a naming pattern that was highly predictable within families but varied enormously across the island.

The Gaelic name **Mac** (son of) and **Ní** (daughter of) prefixes created a genealogical chain that could be traced back five or six generations. A man named **Seán mac Tadhg mic Ciaráin** was "Seán, son of Tadhg, son of Ciarán." This patronymic system persisted in Ireland until the 17th century, when English law required fixed surnames for tax and legal purposes.

Scottish Gaelic naming followed a similar pattern but with regional variations. In the Highlands, the *clan* system meant that names were tied to territory and lineage in ways that Irish naming didn't emphasize as strongly. The name **MacDonald** (son of Donald) carried not just a genealogical meaning but a territorial and political one — it identified the bearer as part of a specific power structure.

### The Brythonic World: Wales, Cornwall, and Brittany

Welsh naming traditions were shaped by the *cyfraith* (law) system, which governed inheritance, marriage, and social obligation. The Welsh practice of *cynefin* — the idea that a person's identity is rooted in their landscape — meant that names often carried topographic significance.

The Welsh patronymic system (*ap* = son of, *verch* = daughter of) persisted longer than in Ireland, surviving into the 16th century. **Llywelyn ap Gruffudd** was "Llywelyn, son of Gruffudd" — and that Gruffudd was the son of Llywelyn the Great. The Welsh naming chain created a genealogical record that was both personal and historical.

Cornish naming was heavily influenced by the Cornish language (*Kernowek*), which survived until the late 18th century. The Cornish naming tradition emphasized saint names (*san*), with many villages dedicated to specific saints. **Nick** (Nicholas) and **Kevern** (Kevin) are examples of Cornish saint names that have broader Celtic connections.

Breton naming was shaped by the duchy's relationship with both the Celtic homeland and the French crown. The Breton patronymic system used *mab* (son of) instead of *ap*, reflecting the language's divergence from Welsh and Cornish. **Yann** (John) and **Malo** (Mallo) are examples of Breton names that have distinct phonetic profiles from their Irish and Welsh counterparts.

### The Celtic Revival and Modern Naming

The Celtic Revival of the late 19th and early 20th centuries transformed Celtic naming from a marginalized tradition into a source of cultural pride. The Gaelic League (*Conradh na Gaeilge*), founded in 1893, promoted the use of Irish names as part of a broader national identity project.

This revival was not without controversy. Some Irish nationalists argued for the exclusive use of "purely Irish" names, rejecting names like **Patrick** and **James** as "English corruptions" of Irish originals. Others, like W.B. Yeats, argued for a more inclusive approach that recognized the syncretic nature of Celtic identity.

The 21st century has seen a second Celtic revival, driven by different forces. Social media, celebrity culture, and a global interest in heritage naming have made Celtic names more accessible than ever. The name **Liam** — once a distinctly Irish form — is now the most popular boys' name in the United States. The name **Rhys** — once confined to Wales — is a top-200 choice in New Zealand and Australia.

This global circulation of Celtic names raises important questions about cultural appropriation versus cultural appreciation. When an American parent names their son **Aonghus**, are they honoring a 2,000-year-old tradition or performing a shallow aesthetic gesture? The answer, as with all naming questions, depends on the parent's intention and willingness to engage with the name's meaning and history.

## Pronunciation Guide

Celtic names present a particular challenge for English-speaking parents: the orthography often doesn't match the pronunciation. The following guide covers the most common phonetic patterns across the Celtic language family.

### Irish (Gaeilge) Pronunciation

Irish orthography is notoriously inconsistent for non-speakers. The key principle is the *slender beside slender, broad beside broad* rule: a consonant is "slender" (palatalized) when flanked by *e* or *i*, and "broad" (velarized) when flanked by *a*, *o*, or *u*.

- **Cian** = KEE-an (the *i* makes the *c* slender, producing a "ke" sound)
- **Seán** = SHAWN (the *séimhiú* or "lenition" of *s* to *sh* is marked by the *fada* on the *é*)
- **Tadhg** = TJOG (the *dh* between vowels is pronounced as a "gy" or "dy" sound; the *gh* at the end is silent)
- **Eoin** = OH-in (the *eo* diphthong is pronounced as a long "oh"; the *in* is soft)
- **Aodh** = AY (the *dh* is silent; the *ao* diphthong produces an "ay" sound)
- **Niall** = NEEL (the *iall* ending produces a long "eel" sound)
- **Saoirse** = SERR-sha (the *saoi* produces a "serr" sound; the *se* at the end is "sha")
- **Rónán** = RON-awn (the *ón* produces a long "oh" sound; the *-án* is a diminitive suffix pronounced "awn")
- **Cillian** = KIL-ee-an (the *ll* is a slender "l" sound, similar to the "ll" in Welsh *Llanelli*)
- **Fionn** = FIN (the *fionn* produces a "fin" sound; the double *n* indicates a slender consonant)

### Scottish Gaelic (Gàidhlig) Pronunciation

Scottish Gaelic shares many phonetic features with Irish but has distinct differences, particularly in vowel pronunciation and consonant clusters.

- **Alasdair** = AL-əs-tər (the *sd* produces a "ster" sound; the final *-air* is pronounced "ər")
- **Domhnall** = DON-əl (the *dh* is silent; the *-all* produces an "əl" sound)
- **Cailean** = KAL-ən (the *c* is hard; the *-ilean* produces "əl-ən")
- **Lachlan** = LAK-lən (the *ch* is a hard "k" sound; the *-lan* produces "lən")
- **Callum** = KAL-əm (the *-lum* produces "ləm"; the double *l* is slender)
- **Ealasaid** = AL-əs-tid (feminine form of Elizabeth; the *-said* produces "tid")
- **Mairi** = MA-ree (the *mh* is silent; the *-iri* produces "ree")
- **Florence** = FLOR-əns (the Gaelic form of Florence; the *-ence* is pronounced "əns")
- **Iseabail** = IS-ə-bal (feminine form of Isabel; the *-abail* produces "ə-bal")
- **Uilleam** = IL-yəm (the *U* is pronounced "i"; the *-leam* produces "yəm")

### Welsh (Cymraeg) Pronunciation

Welsh orthography is remarkably consistent — once you learn the rules, you can pronounce any Welsh word. The key principles are:

- **C** is always hard (like "k")
- **Ch** is like the "ch" in "loch" or "Bach"
- **DD** is like the "th" in "this"
- **F** is always "v"
- **LL** is a voiceless lateral fricative (impossible for most English speakers on first attempt)
- **NG** is always "ng" as in "sing"
- **R** is always trilled
- **S** is always "s" (never "z")
- **TH** is like "th" in "think"
- **W** is either a vowel (like "oo" in "book") or a consonant (like "w" in "we")
- **Y** is either "uh" (before a consonant) or "ee" or "ah" (at the end of a word)

- **Rhys** = REESS (the *Rh* is a voiceless "r" sound, like "h" + "r")
- **Owen** = OWIN (the *W* is a vowel sound like "oo"; the *-en* is "win")
- **Dylan** = DYLL-an (the *Y* is "uh"; the *ll* is the difficult Welsh sound)
