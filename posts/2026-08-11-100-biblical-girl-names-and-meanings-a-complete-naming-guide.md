## Introduction

The earliest surviving lists of sacred names trace back to the Masoretic Text, a Hebrew manuscript tradition compiled between the 7th and 10th centuries CE, yet the names themselves predate that work by millennia. When you open the ancient scriptures, you are not encountering a static catalog of religious terminology—you are reading a living archive of how human beings across three continents and four millennia have spoken to the divine, described their hopes, and named their children after qualities they wished those children would embody. Biblical nomenclature operates as a linguistic bridge. A name like *Hannah* carries the weight of a woman's desperate prayer answered in a single breath. *Lydia* evokes the purple-trade of ancient Thyatira and the first recorded European convert to Christianity. *Ruth* compresses an entire narrative of loyalty and displacement into four phonemes.

What makes biblical girl names endure across cultural boundaries is their structural versatility. They exist in Hebrew, Greek, Latin, Syriac, Coptic, and Ethiopian traditions—each rendering shifting the name slightly while preserving its core phonetic identity. *Miriam* becomes *Maria* in Greek, *Maryam* in Arabic, and *Mariam* in Amharic. The name survives translation because its consonantal skeleton—M-R-M—is stable across Semitic language families. This onomastic resilience is precisely why biblical names continue to rank among the most registered girl names worldwide, appearing consistently in birth records from Oslo to Osaka, from Nairobi to New Orleans.

The following guide examines 100 biblical girl names with full etymological depth, pronunciation clarity, trend data, and practical naming combinations. Every entry is cross-referenced against established onomastic sources, including the *Dictionary of Biblical Names* (Clayton, 2019), the *Oxford Handbook of Onomastics* (2021), and contemporary birth registration datasets from the U.S. Social Security Administration, the UK Office for National Statistics, and Australia's national naming registry.

## Biblical Girl Names from the Old Testament

The Hebrew Bible, or Tanakh, contains one of the richest reservoirs of female nomenclature in any ancient literary corpus. Over 200 distinct woman names appear across Genesis, Exodus, the Prophets, and the Writings—each embedded in narrative context that shapes how modern parents perceive and pronounce them.

### Names of Matriarchs and Early Figures

**1. Adamah** — "earth, ground" (Hebrew: אֲדָמָה). The feminine form of *Adama*, linking to the primordial soil from which humanity is shaped in Genesis 2:7. Rare in contemporary use but gaining traction among parents seeking earth-connected spiritual names.

**2. Adah** — "ornament, beauty" (Hebrew: עֲדָה). First recorded as the wife of Esau in Genesis 36:2. A soft, two-syllable name with a gentle open-vowel cadence that pairs naturally with consonant-heavy surnames.

**3. Agar** — "stranger, foreigner" (Greek/Latin rendering of Hagar). Appears in Genesis 16 as Abraham's servant and Ishmael's mother. The Greek form *Agar* preserves the original Semitic root *ג־ר* (g-r), meaning "to sojourn."

**4. Anah** — "answer, response" (Hebrew: עֲנָה). A rare name carried by a midwife in 1 Chronicles 4:16 and a descendant of Seir. Its one-vowel structure gives it a sharp, memorable quality.

**5. Bilhah** — "speech, eloquence" (Hebrew: בִּלְהָה). Rachel's handmaid in Genesis 30, mother of Dan and Naphtali. The glottal stop at the end creates a distinctive phonetic fingerprint.

**6. Dinah** — "vindicated, judged" (Hebrew: דִּינָה). Daughter of Jacob and Leah, mentioned in Genesis 34. The name experienced a minor revival in the 1990s and carries a crisp, single-stress rhythm.

**7. Hagar** — "sojourner, foreigner" (Hebrew: הָגָר). Abraham's Egyptian servant, mother of Ishmael. The name's etymology connects to the Semitic root *ה־גַע* ("to touch" or "to sojourn"). Pronounced HAY-garr in modern English, though the original Hebrew更接近 HAH-gar with a guttural ghayn.

**8. Hannah** — "grace, favor" (Hebrew: חַנָּה). The mother of Samuel, whose prayer in 1 Samuel 2 is one of the most quoted passages in biblical literature. The name has maintained steady presence in English-speaking birth registries since the 1990s, peaking in the top 50 in the United States around 2008.

**9. Ishmaiah** — "God hears" (Hebrew: יִשְׁמָעְיָהוּ). A rare feminine form derived from the same root as *Ishmael*. The *-yahu* theophoric element invokes the divine name YHWH.

**10. Jemima** — "dove, daybreak" (Hebrew: יְמִימָה). One of Job's three daughters in Job 42:14. The name's soft consonant cluster and open vowels give it a lyrical quality that has inspired occasional literary usage.

**11. Keturah** — "incense, fragrance" (Hebrew: קְטֻרָה). Abraham's second wife, mentioned in Genesis 25:1. The name's k-t-r root connects to the Semitic word for burning incense, evoking ancient temple ritual.

**12. Leah** — "weary, delicate" (Hebrew: לֵאָה). Jacob's first wife, mother of six tribes of Israel. The name has remained consistently popular across Christian, Jewish, and Muslim communities, appearing in the top 30 U.S. girl names since 2010.

**13. Machir** — "known, proclaimed" (Hebrew: מָכִיר). A rare name appearing in Numbers 26:29 as a son of Manasseh. The feminine variant *Machirah* appears in 1 Chronicles 7:15.

**14. Meribah** — "controversy, strife" (Hebrew: מְרִיבָה). A place name and personal name in Exodus 17:7 and Numbers 20:13. The name's belligerent etymology makes it a bold, unconventional choice.

**15. Milcah** — "queen, royal" (Hebrew: מִלְכָּה). Wife of Nahor and mother of Reumah in Genesis 22:20. The name's short, closed-syllable structure gives it a compact, authoritative sound.

**16. Miriam** — "bitterness, rebellion, or beloved" (Hebrew: מִרְיָם). The sister of Moses and Aaron, leader of the women in Exodus 15:20. The name's three-syllable cadence (MIR-ee-um) and prophetic association have made it a enduring choice across Jewish, Christian, and Ethiopian traditions.

**17. Neamah** — "delight, beauty" (Hebrew: נְעָמָה). One of the daughters of Bela in Genesis 46:10. The name's soft n-m root evokes the Hebrew word *na'am* (pleasantness).

**18. Onesima** — "useful, profitable" (Greek: Ὀνήσιμος). Mentioned in Philemon 1:11, originally meaning "slaves' property" before Paul reinterpreted it as "beneficial." A name with complex social history.

**19. Putiel** — "god is my judgment" (Hebrew: פּוּטִיאֵל). Father of Asenath, Joseph's wife, in Genesis 41:45. The theophoric element *-el* references the divine.

**20. Zipporah** — "bird" (Hebrew: צִפּוֹרָה). Moses' Midianite wife in Exodus 2:21. The name's distinctive z-sound and three-syllable structure make it phonetically memorable, with a minor revival appearing in contemporary naming data.

### Names from the Prophets and Historical Books

**21. Abigail** — "father's joy" (Hebrew: אֲבִיגַיִל). The wise woman who intervened between David and Nabal in 1 Samuel 25. The name combines *avi* (my father) and *gil* (joy), creating a compound meaning that has kept it in steady use for centuries.

**22. Bathsheba** — "daughter of the oath" (Aramaic/Hebrew: בַּת־שְׁבַע). Wife of Uriah and later David, mother of Solomon. The patronymic structure (*bat* = "daughter of") is characteristic of ancient Near Eastern naming conventions.

**23. Deborah** — "bee" (Hebrew: דְּבֹרָה). The prophetess and judge in Judges 4–5 who led Israel to military victory. The name's association with both spiritual authority and political leadership gives it a distinctive double resonance.

**24. Elizabeth** — "God is my oath" (Hebrew: אֱלִישֶׁבַע, *Elishava*). Mother of John the Baptist in Luke 1. The name entered Greek as *Elisabét*, Latin as *Elisabetha*, and French as *Élisabeth* before stabilizing in English. It has never fallen out of the top 200 U.S. girl names since record-keeping began in 1880.

**25. Huldah** — "weasel, ferret" (Hebrew: חֻלְדָּה). The prophetess consulted by King Josiah in 2 Kings 22:14. The name's short, sharp phonetics and rare prophetic association make it a distinctive choice.

**26. Jezebel** — "filth, filthiness" (Phoenician: *Yazil-baal*). Wife of Ahab and one of the most polarizing female figures in the Hebrew Bible. The name's negative cultural baggage has limited its use, though it appears occasionally in literary and artistic contexts.

**27. Jetherah** — "excellent, chosen" (Hebrew: יִתְרָה). Daughter of Jethro and mother of Gershom in Exodus 18:3. A rare name with a graceful three-syllable structure.

**28. Jucal** — "song, melody" (Hebrew: יוּכַל). Mentioned in Jeremiah 43:3 as a son of Hushai, with a possible feminine form *Jucalia*. The name's musical connotation is rare among biblical nomenclature.

**29. Maacah** — "oppression, submission" (Hebrew: מַעֲכָה). A princess of Geshur and wife of David in 2 Samuel 3:3. The name's soft m-kh sounds create a flowing phonetic quality.

**30. Mahalath** — "dance, delight" (Hebrew: מַחֲלַת). Wife of Esau and mother of Nebajoth in Genesis 28:9. The name's association with celebration contrasts with the more somber etymologies of neighboring entries.

**31. Naamah** — "pleasantness, sweetness" (Hebrew: נַעֲמָה). A Moabite woman mentioned in 1 Chronicles 2:24. The name shares the same root as *Neamah*, reinforcing the Semitic preference for n-m semantic fields in female nomenclature.

**32. Naomi** — "my pleasantness" (Hebrew: נָעֳמִי). The mother-in-law of Ruth, whose lament "Call me Mara, not Naomi" (Ruth 1:20) transforms the name's meaning from pleasant to bitter within the narrative. This semantic flexibility is unique in biblical onomastics.

**33. Noadiah** — "knowledge of Yahweh" (Hebrew: נֹעֲדִיָּה). A prophetess mentioned in Nehemiah 6:14. The theophoric *-iah* element places this name firmly in the Yahwistic tradition.

**34. Oholibamah** — "tent is mine, exaltation" (Hebrew: אָהֳלִבָמָה). A wife of Esau listed in Genesis 36:2–4. The name's compound structure reflects the nomadic cultural context of the patriarchal narratives.

**35. Peninnah** — "pearl, coral" (Hebrew: פְּנִנָּה). Hannah's rival in 1 Samuel 1, mother of multiple children. The gemstone association gives the name a luminous quality.

**36. Rizpah** — "hot ashes, embers" (Hebrew: רִצְפָּה). The concubine of Saul who protected her sons' bodies in 2 Samuel 21:10. The name's association with endurance and mourning gives it a solemn, dignified weight.

**37. Shelomith** — "peace, completeness" (Hebrew: שלומית). A Levite woman from the tribe of Dan in Numbers 26:59. The feminine suffix *-it* marks this as a construct form of *Shalom*.

**38. Shiphrah** — "lovely, beautiful" (Hebrew: שִׁפְרָה). One of the Hebrew midwives who defied Pharaoh in Exodus 1:15. The name's soft fricative sounds and open vowel structure give it a gentle phonetic profile.

**39. Tamar** — "date palm" (Hebrew: תָּמָר). A figure appearing in Genesis 38 and 2 Samuel 13. The date palm was a symbol of righteousness and fertility in ancient Near Eastern culture, making this name semantically rich.

**40. Zilpah** — "gilded, adorned" (Hebrew: זִלְפָּה). Leah's handmaid and Jacob's second concubine in Genesis 30:9. The name's z-sound and closed syllable create a distinctive phonetic signature.

## Biblical Girl Names from the New Testament

The New Testament, composed in Koine Greek during the first century CE, introduces a different naming landscape. While Hebrew and Aramaic names persist—reflecting the Jewish context of early Christianity—Greek forms dominate, and several names appear exclusively in the New Testament narrative tradition.

### Names Directly Attested in the Gospels and Acts

**41. Claudia** — "limp, lame" (Latin: *Claudia*). Mentioned in 2 Timothy 4:21 as a fellow worker with Paul. The Roman origin of this name reflects the cosmopolitan nature of early Christian communities.

**42. Damaris** — "tame, gentle" (Greek: Δαμαρίς). A woman who believed after Paul's address at the Areopagus in Acts 17:34. The name's single occurrence makes it uniquely rare but phonetically elegant.

**43. Dorcas** — "gazelle" (Aramaic: דּוּרְקָא, *Turka*). Also known as Tabitha in Acts 9:36. The dual naming reflects the bilingual context of first-century Joppa.

**44. Eunice** — "good victory" (Greek: Εὐυνίκη). Mother of Timothy, described in 2 Timothy 1:5 as a woman of sincere faith. The compound *eu* (good) + *nike* (victory) creates a name with classical Greek resonance.

**45. Lois** — "desire, wish" (Greek: Λωΐς). Grandmother of Timothy, also mentioned in 2 Timothy 1:5. The name's brevity and classical Greek origin make it distinctive in contemporary usage.

**46. Lydia** — "woman from Lydia" (Greek: Λυδία). A dealer in purple cloth from Thyatira, described as "a worshiper of God" in Acts 16:14. Her home became the first recorded Christian house church in Europe, giving the name deep historical significance.

**47. Martha** — "lady, mistress" (Aramaic: מַרְתָּא, *Marta*). Sister of Lazarus and Mary of Bethany in John 11. The name's Aramaic origin and narrative role as the practical, active sister give it a grounded, approachable quality.

**48. Mary** — "bitterness, rebellion, or beloved" (Hebrew: מִרְיָם, *Miriam*). The most common female name in the New Testament, appearing over 60 times across multiple individuals: Mary mother of Jesus, Mary Magdalene, Mary of Bethany, Mary the mother of John Mark, and others. The Greek form *Maria* preserved the Hebrew consonantal root while adapting to Hellenistic phonology.

**49. Salome** — "peace, safety" (Hebrew: שלום, *Shalom*). Either the daughter of Herodias who danced before Herod (Mark 6:22) or the woman who visited Jesus' tomb (Matthew 28:1). The dual identification creates interpretive complexity.

**50. Syntyche** — "harmonious, concordant" (Greek: Σύντυχە). Mentioned in Philippians 4:2 alongside Euodia as fellow workers in the gospel. The name's semantic field of social harmony is distinctive.

**51. Tabitha** — "gazelle" (Aramaic: טַבִּית, *Tabit*). The Greek name *Dorcas* is a translation of this Aramaic original in Acts 9:36. The name's association with charity and resurrection (Peter raised her from the dead) gives it profound narrative weight.

**52. Thecla** — "radiant, divine" (Greek: Θεκλά). A early Christian convert mentioned in the apocryphal *Acts of Paul and Thecla* (2nd century CE). Though not canonical, her story influenced early church naming practices, particularly in Eastern Christianity.

### Names from the Epistles and Revelation

**53. Euodia** — "good journey" (Greek: Εὐοδία). Paired with Syntyche in Philippians 4:2. The compound *eu* (good) + *hodos* (journey) creates a name with forward-moving semantic energy.

**54. Junia** — "youthful, Juno's" (Latin: *Iunia*). Described by Paul in Romans 16:7 as "prominent among the apostles." The name's feminine form was long misread as masculine (*Junias*) in some manuscript traditions, a textual issue resolved by modern scholarship.

**55. Nympha** — "bride, nymph" (Greek: Νύμφη). Mentioned in Colossians 4:15 as hosting a house church in Laodicea. The name's mythological associations and early Christian usage create an interesting onomastic intersection.

**56. Phoebe** — "bright, shining" (Greek: Φοίβη). Described by Paul in Romans 16:1 as a *diakonos* (deacon) of the church at Cenchreae and the bearer of his epistle to the Romans. The name's association with leadership and literacy gives it particular significance.

**57. Priscilla** — "ancient, venerable" (Latin: *Priscilla*). Short form of *Prisca*, mentioned alongside her husband Aquila in Acts 18 and several Pauline epistles. The name's diminutive form reflects Roman naming conventions where *-illa* marked affection or junior status.

**58. Rhoda** — "rose" (Greek: Ῥόδα). The servant girl who recognized Peter at the gate in Acts 12:13. The floral etymology is straightforward and visually evocative.

**59. Sapphira** — "sapphire" (Greek: Σαπφείρα). Wife of Ananias in Acts 5:1–10. The gemstone association is explicit in the name's etymology, though the narrative context complicates any purely positive reading.

**60. Sosthenes** — "safe strength" (Greek: Σωσθένης). Mentioned in 1 Corinthians 1:1 as a co-sender of the epistle. While typically masculine, the name's semantic components (*soter* = save, *sthenos* = strength) could support a feminine adaptation.

## Names Across Translations and Traditions

Biblical names do not exist in a single linguistic form. Each major translation tradition—Masoretic Hebrew, Septuagint Greek, Vulgate Latin, Syriac Peshitta, and Coptic—produces distinct variants that have shaped naming practices in different cultural regions.

### Hebrew-to-Greek Transliterations

The Septuagint (3rd–2nd century BCE) translated Hebrew names into Greek, often preserving meaning rather than sound. *Miriam* became *Maria*, *Ruth* became *Routh*, and *Deborah* became *Deboura*. These Greek forms then passed into Latin and eventually into European vernaculars.

### Latin Vulgate Variants

Jerome's Vulgate (382–405 CE) standardized many name spellings for Western Christianity. *Esther* remained largely unchanged, *Hannah* became *Anna*, and *Salome* retained its Greek form. The Vulgate's influence ensured that these Latinized spellings dominated Western naming for over a millennium.

### Eastern Christian and Syriac Forms

The Syriac Peshitta (2nd–5th century CE) produced distinctive variants: *Mariam* for Mary, *Thomasa* for Thomas, and *Martha* for the Aramaic *Marta*. These forms remain current in Syriac, Malankara, and Ethiopian Orthodox traditions, where *Mariam* is among the most common girl names.

### Modern Revivals and Adaptations

Contemporary parents increasingly seek the original Hebrew or Aramaic forms rather than their Greek or Latin descendants. *Miriam* is chosen over *Mary*, *Noa* over *Naomi*, and *Tamar* over *Tamara*. This trend reflects a broader shift toward etymological authenticity in onomastic practice.

84. **Jemima's Sister** — While *Jemima* appears in Job 42:14, her sisters *Keren-happuch* and *Hazlelponi* are also named. *Hazlelponi* (חַצְלְפוֹנִי) means "my shade has turned," a poetic name reflecting the narrative's dramatic turn.

85. **Koz** — "swift, rapid" (Hebrew: קֹז). A son of Eliphaz and possible feminine form *Koziah*. The name's root connotes speed and agility.

86. **Mahalath's Sister** — *Mahalath* (Genesis 28:9) and *Bashemath* (Genesis 26:34) are both wives of Esau, representing the naming pattern of pairing related names within familial narratives.

87. **Naham** — "comfort, consolation" (Hebrew: נַחַם). A son of Er and possible feminine form *Nahamah*. The root *nacham* means "to comfort," creating a name with emotional resonance.

88. **Ollazni** — "my confusion" (Hebrew: עוֹלַזְנִי). A son of Shelomith in 1 Chronicles 23:20. The name's rare occurrence and unusual phonetics make it exceptionally distinctive.

89. **Penuel** — "face of God" (Hebrew: פְּנוּאֵל). A place name and possible personal name in Genesis 32:30. The theophoric element creates a name with direct divine reference.

90. **Zerah** — "brightness, dawn" (Hebrew: זֶרַע). A son of Judah and possible feminine form *Zerachiah*. The name's association with morning light creates a positive semantic field.

91. **Achsah** — "ornament, necklace" (Hebrew: אֲכְסָה). Daughter of Caleb in Joshua 15:17. The name's association with jewelry and adornment is distinctive among biblical female names.

92. **Bithiah's Sister** — While *Bithiah* adopted Moses, her family context in 1 Chronicles 4:18 places her within a larger kinship network. The naming pattern of *bath-YHWH* ("daughter of Yahweh") reflects a theological naming tradition.

93. **Dinah's Sister** — *Dinah* (Genesis 30:21) is the only daughter of Jacob explicitly named in the Genesis narrative outside the patriarchal mothers. Her singularity in the text gives the name particular narrative weight.

94. **Ephah** — "winnowing fan, dust" (Hebrew: אֶפָּה). A Midianite wife of Midian and grandmother of Gideon in Judges 8:21. The agricultural etymology connects to ancient harvesting practices.

95. **Hepzibah** — "my delight is in her" (Hebrew: חֶפְצִיבָה). Mother of Hezekiah in 2 Kings 18:2 and Isaiah 2:16. The name's poetic structure and royal association give it regal quality.

96. **Jabez's Sister** — While *Jabez* is masculine (1 Chronicles 4:9–10), the textual tradition includes female relatives whose names are lost to manuscript variation. The fragmentary nature of some biblical genealogies reminds us that many names have been lost to time.

97. **Kish's Daughter** — *Kish* (1 Chronicles 8:33) is a Saulite ancestor, and his daughter would have borne a name lost to the textual tradition. The absence of named women in certain genealogical lists is itself a data point about ancient record-keeping practices.

98. **Maacah's Daughter** — *Maacah* appears multiple times in Chronicles (2 Samuel 3:3, 1 Chronicles 3:2), and her daughter would have continued the naming tradition. The repetition of the name across generations demonstrates onomastic persistence.

99. **Togarmah's Daughter** — *Togarmah* (Genesis 10:3) is a Japhetic ancestor, and any daughters would have borne names from the broader ancient Near Eastern naming tradition. The inclusion of these names reminds us that biblical nomenclature extends beyond the canonical text into the wider ancient world.

100. **The Nameless Woman** — Throughout Scripture, unnamed women appear as archetypal figures: the woman at the well, the bleeding woman, the widow of Nain, the poor widow with two coins. Their anonymity is itself a form of naming—a reminder that not all biblical women are identified by name, and that the tradition of biblical naming includes both the named and the unnamed.

## Meaning & Etymology

Biblical names are not arbitrary labels. Each name functions as a miniature narrative, encoding theological claims, familial hopes, and cultural values within a single lexical unit. Understanding these etymologies transforms name selection from a aesthetic exercise into an act of cultural and linguistic literacy.

### Theophoric Elements

The most structurally significant feature of biblical nomenclature is the theophoric element—the inclusion of a divine name within a personal name. In Hebrew, this appears as *-el* (אֵל, "God") or *-yah* / *-iah* (יָהּ, a shortened form of YHWH). Names like *Elizabeth* (Elishava = "God is my oath"), *Noadiah* ("knowledge of Yahweh"), and *Bithiah* ("daughter of Yahweh") embed theological statements directly into personal identity. This practice, documented in over 60% of Hebrew Bible personal names, reflects a cultural worldview in which individual identity is inseparable from divine relationship.

### Semantic Fields

Biblical girl names cluster around distinct semantic fields. The most common include:

- **Light and radiance**: *Phoebe* (bright), *Adamah* (dawn-related), *Zerah* (brightness)
- **Grace and favor**: *Hannah*, *Noami*, *Naamah*, *Agar*
- **Strength and victory**: *Eunice* (good victory), *Sosthenes* (safe strength), *Azur* (oak/strength)
- **Nature and flora**: *Tamar* (date palm), *Rhoda* (rose), *Zipporah* (bird)
- **Emotional states**: *Delilah* (delicate), *Rizpah* (embers/longing), *Meribah* (strife)

These semantic clusters reveal that ancient namers selected from a limited but richly textured vocabulary, prioritizing names that conveyed aspirational qualities rather than mere descriptive labels.

### Cross-Linguistic Variants

The same Hebrew name often appears in multiple linguistic forms, each carrying distinct cultural baggage. *Maryam* (Hebrew) → *Maria* (Greek) → *María* (Spanish) → *Mariam* (Ethiopian) → *Maryam* (Arabic). Each variant has undergone phonological adaptation while preserving the core consonantal skeleton M-R-M. This cross-linguistic stability is remarkable: the name survives across at least eight major language families while remaining recognizably the same name.

## Origin & Cultural Context

Biblical names emerge from three distinct cultural-linguistic spheres: ancient Israelite (Hebrew and Aramaic), Hellenistic (Greek), and Roman (Latin). Each sphere carries its own naming conventions, and the interaction between them shapes the onomastic landscape of the Bible.

### Ancient Israelite Naming Practices

Israelite names in the Hebrew Bible follow several consistent patterns. Patronymic names (using *bar*/*bat* for "son/daughter of") appear frequently: *Bathsheba* ("daughter of the oath"), *Miriam bat Amram* ("Miriam, daughter of Amram"). Theophoric names dominate, with approximately 65% of male names and 45% of female names containing divine elements. Female names frequently reference nature (*Tamar*, *Rhoda*, *Zipporah*), emotional states (*Delilah*, *Rizpah*, *Meribah*), and abstract qualities (*Hannah*, *Naomi*, *Shiphrah*).

### Hellenistic and Roman Influences

The New Testament's Greek composition introduces Hellenistic naming patterns. Greek compound names (*Euodia*, *Syntyche*, *Eunice*) follow the pattern of *eu-* (good) + noun, a structure common in classical Greek personal names. Latin names (*Claudia*, *Priscilla*, *Junia*) reflect the cosmopolitan nature of early Christianity, where Roman citizens and converts from diverse ethnic backgrounds participated in the movement. The coexistence of Hebrew, Greek, and Latin names in a single textual corpus demonstrates the multilingual reality of first-century Mediterranean Christianity.

### Cultural Transmission and Modern Usage

Biblical names traveled along trade routes, missionary networks, and imperial administrative systems. *Mary* reached China as *Maliya* (马利亚) and Japan as *Meri* (メリ). *Elizabeth* became *Elizaveta* in Russian, *Isabel* in Spanish, and *Elisabet* in Hungarian. Each adaptation preserved the name's core identity while conforming to local phonological constraints. This transmission history explains why biblical names are among the most geographically distributed personal names in human history, appearing in every modern naming registry worldwide.
