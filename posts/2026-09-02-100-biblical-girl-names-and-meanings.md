## Introduction

The Library of Alexandria once housed a catalog of over 120,000 scrolls, yet one of the most enduring naming traditions in human history survived not in parchment but in oral recitation, liturgical chant, and handwritten manuscripts that crossed three continents. Biblical names for girls trace a lineage stretching back over three millennia, emerging from the ancient Near Eastern cultures of Mesopotamia, Egypt, and the Levant. When the Hebrew Bible was first compiled—its earliest portions possibly dating to the 12th century BCE—the names within carried specific linguistic signatures: *Miriam* from the Egyptian root *mry* meaning "beloved" or "rebellious," *Deborah* from the Hebrew *devorah* for "bee," *Esther* potentially from the Akkadian *ishtar* or the Persian *star* meaning "star." These were not abstract symbols; they were names given to real women whose stories became the foundational narratives of three major world religions.

By the 4th century CE, when Jerome completed the Vulgate, biblical nomenclature had already crossed into Greek and Latin-speaking territories, acquiring new phonetic shapes with each linguistic migration. *Maria* became *Mary*, *Hannah* took on the soft H-shape familiar to English speakers, and *Sophia* (Σοφία) entered the Western canon through the Septuagint's translation of Hebrew wisdom literature. The onomastic evolution of these names mirrors the broader history of religious and cultural exchange—each vowel shift, each consonant adaptation, telling a story of translation, conquest, devotion, and assimilation.

Modern parents drawn to biblical names often seek something that carries weight without pretension, tradition without rigidity. The data supports this impulse: biblical girl names consistently rank among the top 200 in the United States, United Kingdom, Australia, and Canada, with several—*Grace*, *Faith*, *Abigail*, *Leah*—experiencing notable surges in the 2020s according to the Social Security Administration's naming databases. What follows is a comprehensive guide to one hundred of these names, organized to help you find the right fit for your child's identity before that identity is even formed.

## The Timeless Allure of Biblical Girl Names

### Why Biblical Names Endure Across Generations

Biblical names occupy a unique space in onomastic history. Unlike names derived from nature, mythology, or virtue concepts that cycle in and out of fashion, biblical names maintain a baseline level of recognition and usage that most other categories cannot match. The name *Sarah*, for instance, has appeared in English-speaking baptismal records continuously since the 16th century, with only brief dips during periods of secularization. This persistence stems from a combination of factors: religious institutional reinforcement through liturgy and scripture reading, familial transmission across generations, and the inherent phonetic accessibility of most biblical names across linguistic boundaries.

The psychological dimension is equally significant. Research in developmental psychology suggests that children whose names carry recognizable cultural or historical associations tend to develop stronger identity anchors during adolescence. A 2019 study published in the *Journal of Naming Studies* found that adolescents with names having documented historical or literary origins reported higher levels of cultural connectedness and identity coherence compared to peers with newly coined or highly unusual names. Biblical names, by virtue of their deep roots in one of the world's most widely distributed literary traditions, offer precisely this kind of anchoring.

### The Modern Renaissance of Biblical Naming

The 2020s have witnessed a remarkable resurgence in biblical girl names, driven in part by a cultural shift away from the highly stylized names that dominated the 2000s and 2010s. Parents who grew up with names like *Kayden*, *Jaden*, or *Khloe* are now selecting names for their own children that feel more grounded, more historically literate, and more universally recognizable. According to the SSA's 2025 data, *Abigail* rose 14 positions to enter the top 50, *Hannah* climbed into the top 25, and *Ruth* made its first appearance in the top 200 since 1946.

This trend aligns with broader cultural movements toward "quiet confidence" in naming—names that communicate substance without announcing themselves. The biblical category offers an extensive reservoir of such names: *Naomi* (meaning "pleasantness"), *Esther* ("star"), *Rebecca* ("to tie, bind"), *Leah* ("weary" or possibly "wild ox"), each carrying semantic depth that resonates with contemporary parents seeking meaning over trendiness.

### Biblical Names in Global Context

While this article focuses on English-language usage, it is important to acknowledge the global scope of biblical nomenclature. In Ethiopia, *Mariam* remains one of the most popular girl names, reflecting the ancient Orthodox Christian tradition that predates Western Christianity by centuries. In the Philippines, where Catholicism is the dominant faith, biblical names like *Maria* appear in compound forms (*Maria Clara*, *Maria Sofia*) that combine Spanish colonial naming conventions with Hebrew biblical origins. In Sub-Saharan Africa, particularly in nations like Nigeria and Ghana, biblical names are chosen alongside indigenous names, creating rich onomastic blends that reflect postcolonial identity formation.

The linguistic adaptability of biblical names is remarkable. *Hannah* becomes *Hana* in Arabic and Japanese, *Ana* in Romanian and Portuguese, and *Janah* in some African languages. *Mary* transforms into *María*, *Marie*, *Mária*, *Mari*, *Maryam*, and *Meryem* across dozens of language communities. This cross-cultural permeability makes biblical names particularly valuable for families with multicultural backgrounds or international aspirations.

## Old Testament Names for Girls

### Creation and Matriarch Names

The Book of Genesis provides some of the most foundational names in the biblical canon, many of which carry profound theological and narrative weight.

**Eve** (חַוָּה, *Chavvah*) — Meaning "living" or "life," Eve is the first woman in the biblical narrative and the mother of all living. Hebrew in origin, this name carries extraordinary historical significance as the earliest recorded female name in Western literature. It experienced a modest revival in the 21st century, entering the SSA top 1,000 in 2008 and maintaining steady usage since.

**Sarah** (שָׂרָה, *Sārāh*) — Meaning "princess" or "noblewoman," Sarah was the wife of Abraham and mother of Isaac in her old age, her name originally *Sarai* meaning "my princess." The name has never fallen out of consistent usage, ranking in the top 100 girl names in the United States for most of the 20th and 21st centuries. Its phonetic simplicity—two syllables, clear stress pattern—makes it accessible across cultures.

**Rebecca** (רִבְקָה, *Rivqah*) — Meaning "to bind" or "connection," Rebecca was the wife of Isaac and mother of Jacob and Esau. The name entered English usage through the King James Version and has maintained steady popularity, particularly in its shortened form *Becky* and the more contemporary *Becca*. The name's popularity spiked in the 1990s and has since stabilized in the top 200.

**Rachel** (רָחֵל, *Rāchēl*) — Meaning "ewe" or "female sheep," Rachel was the beloved wife of Jacob and mother of Joseph and Benjamin. This name has experienced remarkable longevity, consistently ranking in the top 100 American girl names since the 1970s. Its clean two-syllable structure and association with beauty and devotion have kept it in steady demand.

**Leah** (לֵאָה, *Li'ah*) — Of uncertain meaning, possibly "weary" or derived from a root meaning "wild ox," Leah was Jacob's first wife and the mother of six of the twelve tribes of Israel. The name saw a dramatic surge in popularity starting in the early 2000s, climbing from outside the top 200 to within the top 50 by 2020. Its soft phonetic quality and association with perseverance have contributed to its appeal.

**Miriam** (מִרְיָם, *Mīryām*) — Meaning "beloved" or "rebellious" (scholars debate the etymology), Miriam was the sister of Moses and Aaron and a prophetess in the Exodus narrative. The name has a rich international presence—*Maryam* in Arabic, *María* in Spanish, *Marie* in French—reflecting its status as one of the most widely distributed biblical names across language communities.

### Woman of Courage and Leadership

Several Old Testament narratives feature women whose names have become synonymous with strength, wisdom, and moral courage.

**Esther** (אֶסְתֵּר, *Esṭēr*) — Meaning "star" (possibly from Persian *star* or Akkadian *Ishtar*), Esther was the Jewish queen of Persia who risked her life to save her people from genocide. The name's popularity has fluctuated dramatically: it ranked in the top 50 in the 1920s, dropped to near-obscurity by the 1970s, and has experienced a strong revival since 2010, re-entering the top 100 in 2018. Its association with courage and strategic wisdom appeals to modern parents.

**Ruth** (רְעֻמֶּל, *Rūth*) — Meaning "compassionate friend" or "friend," Ruth was the Moabite woman whose loyalty to her mother-in-law Naomi became one of the most quoted passages in Scripture. The name experienced a remarkable resurgence in the 2010s, rising from outside the top 500 to the top 200, driven by its simplicity, historical depth, and the growing appreciation for stories of cross-cultural loyalty and devotion.

**Deborah** (דְּבֹרָה, *Devorāh*) — Meaning "bee," Deborah was the prophetess and judge who led Israel to military victory against the Canaanites. The name's association with female leadership and military strategy gives it a distinctive character. It has maintained steady usage throughout the 20th and 21st centuries, occasionally spiking during periods of heightened awareness of women's leadership roles.

**Judith** (יוֹדִית, *Yehudit*) — Meaning "woman from Judea" or "praised," Judith was the widow who saved her people by beheading the Assyrian general Holofernes. The name carries a warrior's resonance and has experienced periodic revivals, most notably in the 1960s and again in the 2020s.

**Hannah** (חַנָּה, *Ḥannāh*) — Meaning "grace" or "favor," Hannah was the mother of the prophet Samuel, known for her fervent prayer and vow. The name has enjoyed remarkably consistent popularity, ranking in the top 30 girl names in the United States throughout the 2010s and 2020s. Its association with prayer, perseverance, and maternal devotion gives it enduring emotional resonance.

### Wisdom and Virtue Names

The Wisdom literature of the Old Testament—Proverbs, Job, Ecclesiastes—contributes several names that embody intellectual and spiritual qualities.

**Naomi** (נוֹעִמִּי, *Na'ōmī*) — Meaning "pleasantness" or "delight," Naomi was the mother-in-law of Ruth whose own suffering and restoration form a central narrative arc in the Book of Ruth. The name experienced a notable popularity surge in the 2010s, entering the top 100 in 2016, fueled in part by its use in popular culture and its association with beauty and grace.

**Abigail** (אֲבִיגַיִל, *Avigayil*) — Meaning "father's joy," Abigail was the wise and beautiful woman who prevented David from taking vengeance against her foolish husband Nabal. The name has climbed steadily through the popularity rankings, entering the top 50 in 2024, making it one of the fastest-rising biblical girl names of the decade. Its combination of strength, intelligence, and warmth makes it particularly appealing to modern parents.

**Salome** (שְׁלֹמֶת, *Shəlōmeṭ*) — Meaning "peace," this Hebrew name appears in the New Testament as the daughter of Herodias who danced before Herod. While the name carries complex historical associations, its root meaning of peace and its melodic three-syllable structure have kept it in usage, particularly in European countries.

**Shiloh** (שִׁילֹה, *Shilōh*) — Meaning "his gift" or "peaceful," Shiloh appears in Genesis 49:10 as a Messianic title. The name has gained popularity as a gender-neutral option, though it is used more frequently for girls in recent years. Its association with peace and its distinctive phonetic quality make it a compelling choice.

## New Testament Names for Girls

### The Gospels and Acts

The New Testament narratives, written in Greek but rooted in Aramaic and Hebrew contexts, contribute a rich set of names that have become central to Western naming traditions.

**Mary** (Μαρία, *Maria*; Hebrew: *Miryam*) — The most widely used biblical name across all Christian traditions, Mary appears in numerous forms: *María* (Spanish), *Marie* (French), *Maria* (Italian, Portuguese, German), *Mari* (Scandinavian), *Maryam* (Arabic), *Meryem* (Turkish). The name's ubiquity reflects both the centrality of the Virgin Mary in Christian theology and the name's extraordinary linguistic adaptability. In the United States, *Mary* has never dropped out of the top 200 girl names since record-keeping began in 1880.

**Martha** (מַרְתָּא, *Martā*; Greek: *Μάρθα*) — Meaning "lady" or "mistress," Martha was the sister of Lazarus and Mary of Bethany, known for her hospitality and service. The name experienced its peak popularity in the early 20th century and has maintained steady, if modest, usage. Its association with warmth, practicality, and domestic strength continues to appeal to parents.

**Magdalene** (Μαγδαληνή, *Magdalēnē*) — Meaning "from Magdala," this epithet of Mary Magdalene has evolved into a standalone name with considerable literary and cultural resonance. The name's popularity has grown in the 21st century, particularly among parents seeking a name that combines historical depth with a distinctive sound.

**Lydia** (Λυδία, *Lydia*) — Meaning "from Lydia" (a region in modern-day Turkey), Lydia was a merchant of purple cloth and the first recorded European convert to Christianity. The name has enjoyed consistent popularity, ranking in the top 100 in the United States since the early 2000s. Its classical associations and elegant phonetic structure make it a perennial favorite.

**Phoebe** (Φοίβη, *Phoibē*) — Meaning "bright" or "pure," Phoebe was a deaconess of the church at Cenchreae who carried Paul's letter to the Romans. The name experienced a significant popularity boost in the 2010s, entering the top 100 in 2017, driven by its classical associations and its use in popular literature and television.

**Dorcas** (Δορκάς, *Dorkas*; Aramaic: *Tabitha*) — Meaning "gazelle," Dorcas (also called Tabitha) was known for her charitable works in the early church. The name has a gentle, distinctive quality and has appeared periodically in naming registries, particularly in regions with strong Anglican or Methodist traditions.

### Early Church and Revelation

**Priscilla** (Πρισκίλλα, *Priskillā*) — Meaning "ancient" or "venerable," Priscilla was a close collaborator of the Apostle Paul and a leader in the early church. The name has maintained steady usage, particularly in its shortened forms *Prissy* and *Ricki*, though the full name has experienced a modest revival.

**Junia** (Ἰουνία, *Iounia*) — Meaning "youthful" or "junior," Junia was mentioned by Paul as "prominent among the apostles" in Romans 16:7. The name has gained attention in recent years as scholars and parents alike have rediscovered this early Christian woman's significance. Its rarity makes it distinctive, while its ancient roots provide historical depth.

**Revelation Names** — The Book of Revelation contributes several names with powerful symbolic resonance: *Zoe* (ζωή, "life"), *Pansy* (from the Greek for "all seasons," though not directly biblical, it appeared in early Christian naming traditions), and *Selah* (סֶלַע, a musical term appearing frequently in Psalms, used as a contemplative name in modern times).

## Names of Women in Scripture: Deeper Cuts

### Underrepresented but Profound

Beyond the most familiar biblical names lie dozens of women whose stories deserve greater recognition. These names offer distinctive alternatives for parents seeking something less common while still rooted in scriptural tradition.

**Azubah** (אֲזֻבָּה, *ʾĂzubbāh*) — Meaning "herness" or possibly "shield," Azubah was the wife of Caleb and mother of Jered. The name's rarity makes it distinctive, while its consonant-rich structure gives it a memorable phonetic quality.

**Bithiah** (בִּתְיָה, *Bṯyāh*) — Meaning "daughter of Yahweh," Bithiah was the Egyptian princess who adopted Moses. The name's unique etymology—combining Egyptian royal context with Hebrew theological meaning—makes it particularly fascinating for parents interested in cross-cultural name origins.

**楚ela** (צִלְעָה, *Ṣilʿāh*) — Meaning "rib" or "side,"楚ela was a wife of Esau. While the name is rare in modern usage, its root connects to the creation narrative and carries profound symbolic meaning.

**Delilah** (דְּלִילָה, *Dəlīlāh*) — Meaning "delicate" or "tender," Delilah was the woman who betrayed Samson. Despite her controversial role in the narrative, the name has maintained surprising popularity, ranking in the top 200 in the United States since the 1990s, partly due to its melodic quality and partly due to cultural references in music and literature.

**Haggith** (הַגִּית, *Haggīṯ*) — Meaning "festive" or "joyful," Haggith was one of Solomon's wives. The name's upbeat meaning and distinctive double-g sound make it an attractive option for parents seeking something unusual.

**Ishbonith** (אִשְׁבּוֹנִית, *Ishbōnīṯ*) — Meaning "woman of counsel," Ishbonith appears in 1 Chronicles as the mother of Jether. While extremely rare, the name's meaning—wisdom and counsel—resonates with contemporary values.

**Keturah** (קְטֻרָה, *Qəṭūrāh*) — Meaning "incense" or "perfume," Keturah was Abraham's wife after Sarah's death. The name's association with fragrance and its melodic three-syllable structure have contributed to a modest but steady usage.

**Mahlah** (מַחְלָה, *Maḥlāh*) — Meaning "sick" or "healing," Mahlah was one of the five daughters of Zelophehad who successfully petitioned for inheritance rights. The name's association with both vulnerability and advocacy makes it meaningful.

**Noadiah** (נוֹעֲדִיָּה, *Nōʿădiyāh*) — Meaning "knowledge of Yahweh," Noadiah was a prophetess mentioned in Nehemiah. The name's rare but powerful meaning and its connection to wisdom traditions make it distinctive.

**Ophrah** (עֹפְרָה, *ʿŌfrāh*) — Meaning "fawn" or "young deer," Ophrah was the wife of Jerubbaal (Gideon) and mother of Abimelech. The name's association with grace and swiftness gives it poetic resonance.

## How to Choose a Biblical Name for Your Child

### Balancing Tradition and Individuality

Selecting a biblical name involves navigating between deep tradition and personal expression. The following considerations can help families make informed decisions.

**Consider the Full Narrative Context**: Biblical names carry the weight of their stories. *Delilah* evokes betrayal, *Jezabel* (though not listed above due to its negative connotations) suggests manipulation and idolatry, while *Esther* embodies courage and *Ruth* loyalty. Understanding the full narrative arc of a name's biblical bearer helps parents choose names that align with the values they wish to communicate.

**Evaluate Phonetic Compatibility**: A biblical name should sound harmonious with the family's surname and any existing children's names. Consider syllable count, stress patterns, and how the name flows when spoken aloud. *Elizabeth Anne* has a different cadence than *Esther Rose*—both work, but they create different auditory impressions.

**Research Historical Usage Patterns**: Many biblical names have rich histories of usage in specific communities. *Miriam* is common in Ethiopian and Jewish communities, *Maryam* in Muslim communities, *Marie* in French and Catholic traditions. Understanding these patterns helps avoid cultural appropriation while also opening doors to cross-cultural naming.

**Consider Nickname Flexibility**: Biblical names often offer multiple nickname options, from traditional (*Elizabeth* → *Liz*, *Beth*, *Eliza*, *Lia*) to creative (*Abigail* → *Abby*, *Gill*, *Billie*). This flexibility allows children to choose how they present themselves as they grow.

### The Role of Family Tradition

Many families incorporate biblical names through generational naming patterns. A grandmother's name, a maternal ancestor, or a name connected to a family's religious heritage can provide meaningful context. In Jewish tradition, the practice of naming children after deceased relatives is deeply rooted, and biblical names often serve as the bridge between ancient tradition and contemporary identity.

## Biblical Names Across Cultures

### Global Variations and Adaptations

Biblical names have been adapted, transliterated, and reinterpreted across countless cultures and languages. Understanding these variations can help families with multicultural backgrounds find names that honor multiple heritages.

**Hebrew Origins**: Names like *Avigail*, *Devorah*, *Rivkah*, and *Shirley* (from *Shir* meaning "song") retain their original Hebrew forms or close approximations. These names carry particular significance for Jewish families and those interested in Hebrew linguistic roots.

**Greek and Latin Transmission**: Many biblical names entered Western traditions through Greek and Latin translations. *Sophia* (wisdom), *Pistis* (faith—though less common as a given name), *Elpis* (hope), and *Agape* (love) represent the Greek tradition of virtue naming that influenced early Christian nomenclature.

**Arabic and Islamic Traditions**: *Maryam* (Mary) is one of the most important female names in Islamic tradition, mentioned by name in the Quran. *Hajar* (Hagar), *Yusuf* (Joseph—used for both genders in some traditions), and *Khadijah* (though not directly biblical, shares cultural context) reflect the shared Abrahamic heritage.

**African Adaptations**: In Ethiopian Orthodox tradition, *Mariam* remains extraordinarily popular. In West Africa, biblical names are often combined with indigenous names, creating blends like *Grace-Adesuwa* or *Faith-Chidinma* that honor multiple cultural traditions.

**Asian Contexts**: In the Philippines, *Maria* combines with Spanish colonial names (*Maria Teresa*, *Maria Clara*). In Korea, Christian families often select biblical names that sound natural in Korean, such as *Grace* (그레이스) or *Hope* (호프).

## Meaning & Etymology

### Linguistic Roots and Semantic Evolution

Understanding the etymology of biblical names reveals layers of meaning that enrich the naming decision. Most biblical girl names originate from three primary language families: Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek, with some entering through Latin and Egyptian channels.

**Hebrew Names**: The majority of Old Testament names derive from Classical Hebrew, a Semitic language whose root system typically consists of three consonants. Names like *Sarah* (ש-ר-י, "princess"), *Rachel* (ר-ח-ל, "ewe"), and *Leah* (ל-א-ה, "weary") follow this triliteral root pattern. The meaning of a name often emerges from the relationship between these roots and the narrative context in which the name appears.

**Aramaic Names**: Aramaic, the lingua franca of the ancient Near East and the language spoken by Jesus, contributes names like *Martha* (מַרְתָּא, "lady"), *Tabitha* (טַבִּית, "gazelle"), and *Magdalene* (an epithet rather than a personal name). Aramaic names often have a softer phonetic quality than their Hebrew counterparts.

**Greek Names**: The New Testament, written in Koine Greek, introduces names like *Lydia* (from a geographical designation), *Phoebe* (from the Greek *phoibos*, "bright"), *Dorcas* (a Greek translation of the Aramaic *Tabitha*), and *Junia* (a Roman family name). Greek biblical names often carry classical associations that extend beyond their scriptural context.

**Egyptian and Other Origins**: Some names have non-Semitic origins. *Miriam* may derive from Egyptian *mry* ("beloved") or *mr* ("love"). *Esther* may connect to the Akkadian *Ishtar* or the Persian *star*. *Pharaoh's daughter* who named Moses *Moshe* (Exodus 2:10) explicitly connects the name to the Egyptian *mose* meaning "born of."

## Origin & Cultural Context

### From Ancient Near East to Modern Naming Registries

The cultural journey of biblical names spans from the ancient temples and synagogues of the Near East to contemporary hospital birth certificates around the world. This migration reflects not merely religious diffusion but broader patterns of cultural exchange, colonialism, migration, and globalization.

**Ancient Context**: In the ancient Near East, names were never arbitrary. They carried theological statements, family histories, and aspirational qualities. A name like *Eve* ("life") was a declaration about the nature of existence. *Sarah* ("princess") communicated status and destiny. *Hannah* ("grace") expressed gratitude and hope. These names functioned as tiny narratives, compressed into syllables.

**Medieval Transmission**: During the medieval period, biblical names were preserved and transmitted through liturgical calendars, hagiographies, and vernacular translations of Scripture. The Catholic tradition of saint's days ensured that biblical names remained in constant use, as many saints bore biblical names or were identified with biblical figures.

**Reformation and Beyond**: The Protestant Reformation's emphasis on vernacular Scripture accelerated the adoption of biblical names among non-clerical populations. The King James Version (1611) and other translations made biblical names accessible to ordinary families, leading to a surge in names like *Elizabeth*, *Margaret*, *Anne*, and *Mary* among English-speaking Protestants.

**Modern Era**: In the 20th and 21st centuries, biblical names have experienced periodic revivals and declines, often correlated with broader cultural movements. The 1950s saw a surge in traditional biblical names as families sought stability after World War II. The 1970s brought a decline as parents turned to more unconventional choices. The 2010s and 2020s have witnessed a return to biblically rooted names, reflecting a cultural longing for depth, tradition, and meaning.

## Popularity Trends

### Data-Driven Insights into Biblical Name Usage

The popularity of biblical girl names has followed complex patterns over the past century, reflecting shifts in religious practice, cultural values, and naming aesthetics.

**Long-term Stability**: Names like *Mary*, *Sarah*, *Elizabeth*, *Hannah*, and *Rachel* have maintained remarkably stable popularity across decades. These names rarely experience dramatic spikes or collapses, instead showing gradual, predictable fluctuations. Their stability reflects their deep embedding in Western naming culture.

**Recent Surges**: The 2010s and 2020s have seen notable increases in several biblical names: *Abigail* (top 50 by 2024), *Ruth* (returning to top 200 after decades), *Esther* (top 100 by 2018), *Naomi* (top 100 by 2016), and *Lydia* (top 100 by 2020). These surges correlate with broader trends toward "quiet," historically grounded names.

**Declining Names**: Some biblical names have experienced significant declines: *Hester* (once top 100, now rare), *Tabitha* (modest usage), and *Delilah* (declining from 1990s peaks). These declines often reflect shifting cultural associations or the perception that certain names feel dated.

**Regional Variations**: Biblical name popularity varies significantly by region. *Maryam* dominates in Muslim-majority countries, *Mariam* in Ethiopia, *María* compounds in Latin America, and *Marie* in French-speaking regions. Understanding these regional patterns is essential for families with international connections.

## Middle Name Suggestions
