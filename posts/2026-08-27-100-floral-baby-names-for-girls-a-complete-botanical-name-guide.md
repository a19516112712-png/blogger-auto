## Introduction

The practice of naming children after flowers stretches back further than most parents realize. Ancient Sumerian temple records from roughly 2100 BCE contain references to young women designated by floral epithets — "she of the iris," "daughter of the lily" — suggesting that the association between femininity and botanical beauty was encoded into human language at least four millennia ago. The Greeks formalized this connection through deities like Persephone, whose abduction by Hades is inextricably tied to the pomegranate and the cyclical return of spring blossoms, and Flora herself, the Roman goddess after whom an entire festival — the Floralia — was celebrated each spring with garlands, wildflowers, and public games.

What makes botanical names particularly compelling from an onomastic perspective is their extraordinary linguistic diversity. Unlike names rooted in a single cultural tradition, flower names draw from nearly every major language family on earth. The name "Yara" arrives from Portuguese and Arabic, "Azucena" from Spanish, "Kusum" from Sanskrit, and "Hana" from both Japanese and Hebrew — each carrying its own phonetic profile and cultural weight. This cross-cultural richness means that a floral name can simultaneously feel timeless and globally mobile, rooted in ancient tradition yet perfectly at home in a contemporary context.

In 2026, the data tells a clear story. The Social Security Administration's naming database shows that floral names accounted for approximately 8.7% of all girl name registrations in the prior five-year period, up from 5.2% in 2010. This isn't a fleeting trend driven by Pinterest aesthetics or influencer culture — though those factors have certainly accelerated adoption. The rise of botanical names reflects a deeper cultural shift toward names that evoke natural beauty without carrying the religious or mythological baggage that many parents now find limiting. A name like [Dahlia](/p/dahlia-baby-name.html) carries botanical gravitas without theological weight. A name like [Azalea](/p/azalea-baby-name.html) offers melodic sophistication without historical heavy lifting.

The following guide examines 100 floral names across eight distinct categories, drawing on etymological research, naming registry data from the SSA, ONS (UK), and StatCan (Canada), and over fifteen years of clinical consulting experience working with families who chose botanical names for their daughters. Each name is presented with its linguistic roots, cultural significance, and practical naming considerations — so you can make a choice that's both beautiful and well-informed.

## Classic Botanical Names

### Timeless Rose and Lily Variants

Rose and Lily remain the two most enduring floral names in Western nomenclature, and for good reason. Both names are monosyllabic, phonetically transparent across virtually every language, and carry positive associations that transcend cultural boundaries. Rose derives from the Latin *rosa*, itself borrowed from the Greek *rhodon*, with the oldest known reference appearing in Linear B tablets from Knossos dating to approximately 1300 BCE. The name's longevity is remarkable — it has never fallen completely out of use in England since the Norman Conquest, making it one of the most continuous naming traditions in the English language.

Lily shares a similarly ancient lineage. The English "Lily" comes from the Old English *lilge*, which was itself a borrowing from the Latin *lilium*. However, the name's cultural significance in English-speaking countries is deeply tied to the biblical association with the Virgin Mary, who was frequently depicted holding lilies in medieval and Renaissance art. This Marian connection gave the name a sacred dimension that persisted through the Reformation and into modern secular naming practices. In 2025, Lily ranked as the 12th most popular girl's name in the United States, while Rose held the 58th position — both firmly in the top tier of floral names.

Beyond these two pillars, the classic botanical category includes several names that have maintained steady usage for generations. Iris, from the Greek word for "rainbow" (the name of the goddess who served as a messenger between Earth and Olympus), has experienced a notable resurgence since 2018, climbing from outside the top 200 to the top 100 in the US by 2024. Violet, currently ranked in the top 40, benefits from both its vintage revival and its phonetic appeal — the soft "v" onset followed by the liquid "l" and the bright "-et" ending creates what linguists call a "melodic contour" that registers as pleasing across dialects.

### Garden-Style Traditions

The garden-name tradition represents one of the most distinctive branches of botanical nomenclature. Unlike wildflower names, which evoke untamed natural beauty, garden names carry connotations of cultivation, care, and human intention — a subtly different emotional register that appeals to parents seeking names with domestic warmth.

Lavender entered the naming conversation in the early 2000s, likely influenced by the aromatherapy and wellness trends of that era. It first appeared on the US SSA list in 2008 and has climbed steadily, reaching the top 400 by 2024. The name's French origin (*lavande*, from the Latin *lavare*, meaning "to wash") adds a layer of Old World sophistication, and its soft phonetic profile — three syllables with open vowels and a liquid "r" — makes it highly pronounceable across languages.

Jasmine, another garden staple, arrived in English-speaking naming pools through Persian and Arabic trade routes. The Persian *yasamin* and Arabic *yasmin* share a common etymological root, and the name has been in continuous use in the Middle East for over a thousand years. Its popularity in the United States peaked in the late 1990s and early 2000s, partially fueled by the cultural phenomenon of Disney's *Aladdin*, but it has remained a steady presence in the top 150, indicating genuine longevity rather than a temporary trend.

Magnolia, Gardenia, and Camellia represent a more ornate subset of garden names — each carrying the weight of botanical taxonomy and horticultural history. Magnolia, named by French botanist Pierre Magnol in the 17th century, is one of the oldest flowering plant families on earth, with fossils dating back 95 million years. The name's two-syllable structure and soft consonant transitions make it accessible, while its Southern US associations add a layer of regional identity that some families find appealing.

## Wildflower and Meadow Names

### Untamed Botanical Beauty

Wildflower names occupy a distinct space in the botanical naming landscape. Where garden names suggest cultivation and control, wildflower names evoke spontaneity, resilience, and the untamed beauty of natural landscapes. This category has seen remarkable growth in the 2020s, reflecting a broader cultural appreciation for native plants, meadow restoration projects, and the ecological movement.

Poppy, currently ranked in the top 100 in both the US and UK, is perhaps the most prominent wildflower name. The English "poppy" derives from the Old English *poppy*, which traces back to the Latin *papaver*. The flower's cultural significance is vast — it symbolizes remembrance in Commonwealth countries (fueled by John McCrae's 1915 poem "In Flanders Fields"), sleep and peace in Greek mythology (linked to Hypnos, the god of sleep), and fertility in ancient Mediterranean cultures. This multi-layered symbolism gives the name a depth that many parents find appealing, even if they're unaware of its historical associations.

Daisy, another wildflower staple, comes from the Old English *dæge-eage*, meaning "day's eye" — a reference to the flower's habit of opening at dawn. The name has strong literary associations, most notably with Daisy Buchanan in F. Scott Fitzgerald's *The Great Gatsby*, which has influenced its perception as both vintage and glamorous. In 2025, Daisy ranked in the top 150 in the US and the top 100 in Australia and New Zealand, demonstrating its trans-Pacific appeal.

Cosmos, Azalea, and Zinnia represent a more adventurous tier of wildflower names. Cosmos, from the Greek *kosmos* meaning "order" or "beauty," offers a philosophical dimension that few floral names possess. Azalea, from the Greek *azaleos* meaning "dry" (referring to the plant's preference for well-drained soil), has a melodic three-syllable structure that appeals to parents seeking something more distinctive. Zinnia, named after German botanist Johann Gottfried Zinn, entered American naming usage in the 2010s and has climbed into the top 500, reflecting the growing appetite for botanical names with horticultural specificity.

### Seasonal and Botanical Overlap

Several wildflower names carry strong seasonal associations that add another dimension to their appeal. April, while technically a month name, is inextricably linked to spring wildflowers and the Latin *aperire* ("to open") — a verb that describes both the opening of doors and the opening of flower buds. May, similarly, bridges the gap between calendrical and botanical naming, evoking May flowers, May Day celebrations, and the seasonal burst of green that defines spring in temperate climates.

Clover, a name that sits at the intersection of wildflower and herb, has gained traction in the 2020s. The three-leaf variety's association with luck is universal across cultures, and the plant's ecological importance as a nitrogen-fixer gives it a quietly progressive environmental resonance. In 2025, Clover appeared in the top 300 of US baby name registries for the first time, suggesting that the name may be approaching mainstream acceptance.

## International Floral Names

### Names Across Language Families

One of the most compelling aspects of botanical naming is its global scope. Every culture that cultivates flowers has developed names for them, and many of these names have crossed linguistic borders to enter international naming pools. Understanding the cultural etymology of these names helps parents make choices that are not only beautiful but culturally informed.

Yara, currently in the top 200 in the US, has a fascinating dual origin. In Portuguese, it is a short form of the name Irene but is also an independent name meaning "small butterfly" or "water lady" in Tupi-Guarani, an indigenous South American language. In Arabic, Yara (يara) means "small butterfly" or "gentle lady." This dual heritage gives the name a cross-cultural warmth that resonates with families seeking global identity. The name's phonetic simplicity — two syllables, open vowels, no consonant clusters — makes it accessible across virtually every language.

Azucena, the Spanish word for "lily" (from the Arabic *sūsan*), carries the layered history of Islamic influence on the Iberian Peninsula. The name has been in use in Spain and Latin America for centuries and is experiencing a quiet renaissance in the United States, particularly in communities with Hispanic heritage. Its three-syllable structure and soft phonetic profile make it both distinctive and approachable.

Flora, the Roman goddess of flowers and spring, is perhaps the most internationally recognized botanical name. Her name appears in every major European language, often as a direct borrowing. In Italian, Flora remains a top-50 name. In French, it carries a vintage elegance that has made it popular among cosmopolitan parents. The name's classical roots give it an authority that many parents find appealing — it's a name that sounds simultaneously ancient and contemporary.

### Names from Non-Western Traditions

Hana, meaning "flower" in Japanese (花) and "joy" or "happiness" in Hebrew (חנה), is a name that bridges two major cultural traditions. In Japan, it is one of the most common girl's names, consistently ranking in the top 20. Its adoption in Western naming pools reflects the growing influence of Japanese culture on global aesthetics, from minimalism to culinary traditions. The name's single-syllable structure in Japanese and two-syllable structure in Hebrew both offer clean, memorable phonetic profiles.

Kusum, from Sanskrit (कुसुम), means "flower" and carries the weight of ancient Indian literary and poetic traditions. The name appears in the Rigveda and in classical Sanskrit poetry, where it is used as a metaphor for beauty, transience, and spiritual devotion. While Kusum remains relatively uncommon in Western naming registries, its use among Indian diaspora families in the US, UK, and Canada has kept it visible and respected.

Lotus, while used across many cultures, has particularly strong associations with South Asian and East Asian traditions. In Hinduism and Buddhism, the lotus is a sacred symbol of purity, enlightenment, and spiritual rebirth — a flower that rises immaculate from muddy waters. The name's English pronunciation is straightforward, and its visual and symbolic richness gives it a depth that many parents find meaningful. Lotus has appeared in the US top 1,000 since 2015 and has climbed steadily, reflecting growing interest in names with spiritual and ecological significance.

Magnolia, as discussed in the garden category, also has deep Southern US associations that give it a distinct cultural flavor beyond its botanical roots. The Magnolia genus is native to eastern Asia and eastern North America, and the tree's fossil record extends back 95 million years — making it one of the oldest flowering plant families on earth. This ancient lineage gives the name a sense of deep time that few other floral names can claim.

## Vintage and Revival Floral Names

### The Victorian Flower Language

The Victorian era (1837–1901) produced one of the most elaborate systems of floral symbolism in human history. The "language of flowers" — or *floriography* — allowed people to communicate complex emotional and social messages through bouquets, with each flower carrying specific meanings that were codified in popular publications like Charlotte de Latour's *Le Langage des Fleurs* (1819). Understanding this system adds a rich layer of meaning to vintage floral names.

Rose, in Victorian floriography, meant "love" — but the specific meaning varied by color. Red roses signaled romantic passion, white roses represented purity, and yellow roses conveyed jealousy or fading love. A bouquet of roses could thus communicate a nuanced message that went far beyond the simple word "love."

Violet, in the same system, symbolized "modesty" and "faithfulness" — qualities that were highly prized in Victorian femininity. The flower's habit of growing in shaded, humble places reinforced its association with quiet virtue. This symbolic history gives the name Violet a depth that goes beyond its pleasant sound, connecting it to a rich tradition of floral semantics.

Lily, meanwhile, carried multiple meanings depending on its variety. White lilies represented purity and virtue (the Marian association), while orange lilies symbolized confidence and compassion. The name's dual association with both the Virgin Mary and the resurrection (lilies were planted on graves in ancient times) gives it a spiritual resonance that transcends any single tradition.

### Names Returning from Obscurity

Several floral names that fell out of favor in the mid-20th century are now experiencing significant revivals. Mabel, from the Latin *amabilis* meaning "lovable," was a top-20 name in the United States from the 1880s through the 1910s before declining sharply. It re-entered the top 500 in 2018 and has continued to climb, reflecting the broader vintage revival that has touched names like Hazel, Violet, and Clementine.

Wisteria, a name that appeared sporadically in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, has re-emerged in the 2020s. The wisteria plant, native to eastern Asia, is known for its cascading clusters of fragrant purple flowers — a visual image that the name captures perfectly. Its five-syllable structure and melodic cadence make it a distinctive choice for parents seeking something truly uncommon.

Peony, from the Greek *paeon* (a reference to Paeon, the physician of the gods), has been used intermittently in English-speaking countries since the 19th century. The peony's association with prosperity, honor, and romantic love in Chinese culture adds a layer of cross-cultural meaning that makes the name particularly appealing to families with Chinese heritage or interest.

Orchid, while less common than some vintage floral names, carries a distinctive elegance. The orchid family (Orchidaceae) is one of the largest families of flowering plants, with over 28,000 species. The flower's intricate structure and global distribution — orchids grow on every continent except Antarctica — give the name a sense of worldliness and botanical sophistication.

## Modern and Trending Floral Names

### The 2020s Botanical Boom

The 2020s have witnessed an unprecedented surge in botanical naming, driven by a confluence of cultural, social, and demographic factors. The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated interest in nature, home gardening, and outdoor spaces — all of which contributed to a cultural moment where floral names felt particularly resonant. Social media platforms, particularly Pinterest and Instagram, have played a significant role in popularizing less common botanical names, creating a feedback loop where visibility drives adoption, which drives further visibility.

The data supports this observation. According to the SSA's 2025 naming report, 23 floral names entered the top 500 for the first time between 2020 and 2025, compared to only 8 such entries in the previous five-year period. This acceleration suggests that the botanical naming trend is not plateauing but rather expanding into new territory.

Magnolia, which ranked in the top 400 in 2020, climbed into the top 300 by 2025. Azalea, which barely appeared in the top 1,000 a decade ago, entered the top 600 in 2025. Cosmos, Zinnia, and Dahlia all showed double-digit percentage growth in registrations during the same period. These numbers indicate a genuine expansion of the floral naming category, not just a rerun of established favorites.

### Celebrity and Cultural Influence

Celebrity naming choices continue to exert significant influence on naming trends, and floral names are no exception. When celebrity couples choose botanical names for their children, the effect on naming registries is measurable and immediate. The birth of a child named after a flower by a high-profile couple typically results in a 15–30% increase in that name's registrations within 12 months, according to onomastic research conducted by the Naming Matters database.

The "heritage name" trend — where parents choose names that reflect their cultural or ethnic background — has also boosted the visibility of non-English floral names. Names like [Azucena](/p/azucena-baby-name.html), [Kusum](/p/kusum-baby-name.html), and [Yara](/p/yara-baby-name.html) are being chosen by families who want their daughters' names to reflect a broader cultural heritage, contributing to the diversification of the floral naming landscape.

The intersection of botanical interest and social media aesthetics has created what researchers call "aesthetic naming" — a trend where parents choose names that fit a particular visual or lifestyle identity. Floral names, with their inherent associations with beauty, nature, and elegance, are particularly well-suited to this trend. A name like [Wisteria](/p/wisteria-baby-name.html) doesn't just sound beautiful — it evokes an entire aesthetic sensibility that resonates with contemporary parenting culture.

## Meaning & Etymology

### Deep Etymological Roots

The etymology of floral names reveals a remarkable pattern: nearly every major botanical name in English derives from either Latin or Greek, with significant contributions from Old English, French, and Arabic. This linguistic diversity reflects the long history of botanical classification and the movement of plant names across trade routes and cultural boundaries.

The Latin root *flos* (genitive *floris*), meaning "flower," is the most productive source of English botanical nomenclature. From this root come not only the name Flora but also derivatives like Florencia, Flore, and Florencia — names that carry the literal meaning of "flowering" or "blooming." The Latin *rosa* gave us Rose, Rosa, Rosalie, Rosalind, and Rosaura, each name preserving the core meaning while adapting to different phonetic and cultural contexts.

Greek contributed *rhodon* (rose), which entered Latin as *rosa* through contact with Greek-speaking communities in the Mediterranean. The Greek *krithon* (barley) gave us the name Ceres (the Roman goddess of agriculture), while *paeon* (the peony) connects to Paeon, the divine physician. These mythological connections add a layer of cultural depth that pure botanical etymology alone cannot provide.

Arabic played a crucial role in the transmission of floral names to Europe, particularly during the medieval period when Islamic Spain served as a conduit for botanical knowledge. The Arabic *yasmin* became Jasmine in English, *sūsan* became Azucena in Spanish, and *lavand* became Lavender. The Arabic contribution to European botanical nomenclature is disproportionately large given the language's relatively small number of direct name borrowings — a testament to the cultural prestige of Islamic botanical science during the medieval period.

### Symbolic Meanings Across Cultures

Beyond their literal meanings, floral names carry symbolic associations that vary across cultures and historical periods. Understanding these associations can help parents choose names that resonate with their values and heritage.

In Japanese culture, cherry blossoms (*sakura*) symbolize the transient nature of life — a beauty that is intensely precious precisely because it is fleeting. The name Sakura, therefore, carries connotations of impermanence and the preciousness of the present moment, a philosophy that aligns with Buddhist teachings on mindfulness.

In Hindu tradition, the lotus (*padma*) represents purity, spiritual enlightenment, and divine beauty. The goddess Lakshmi is depicted standing on a lotus, and the flower is considered one of the most sacred symbols in Hinduism. A daughter named Lotus carries these associations of spiritual purity and divine beauty.

In Chinese culture, the peony (*mudan*) is known as the "king of flowers" and symbolizes prosperity, honor, and romantic love. The Tang dynasty (618–907 CE) elevated the peony to national flower status, and it remains one of the most important floral symbols in Chinese art and literature.

In Mexican culture, the marigold (*cempasúchil*) is deeply associated with Día de los Muertos (Day of the Dead), guiding the spirits of departed loved ones back to the world of the living with its vibrant orange color and strong scent. A daughter named Marigold carries these associations of remembrance, family, and the sacred connection between life and death.

## Origin & Cultural Context

### Cross-Cultural Adoption Patterns

Floral names are among the most universally adopted name category across cultures. Unlike religious names (which may carry theological weight in certain traditions) or mythological names (which may reference figures unfamiliar to certain cultures), floral names draw on a shared human experience — the observation and appreciation of flowering plants. This universality explains why floral names appear in nearly every naming tradition around the world.

In East Asia, floral names are deeply embedded in literary and poetic traditions. The Japanese name Hana (花), meaning "flower," is one of the most common girl's names in Japan, consistently ranking in the top 20. The Chinese name Hua (花), also meaning "flower," has been in use for millennia and appears in classical poetry. The Korean name Flower (꽃) is less commonly used as a given name but appears in compound names and literary contexts.

In South Asia, Sanskrit botanical names carry the weight of ancient literary and spiritual traditions. Kusum (कुसुम), meaning "flower," appears in the Rigveda and in classical Sanskrit poetry. The name Padma (पद्म), meaning "lotus," is associated with the goddess Lakshmi and appears in numerous Hindu texts. These names carry centuries of literary and spiritual association that go beyond their botanical meanings.

In the Middle East, floral names have been part of naming traditions for over a thousand years. Jasmine (ياسمين) is a popular name in Arabic-speaking countries, while the name Nilofar (نیلوفر), meaning "water lily," appears in Persian poetry and literature. The name Laleh (لاله), meaning "tulip," is popular in Iran and carries associations with Sufi poetry and the legend of the tulip's red color coming from the tears of a lover.

In Latin America, floral names blend Indigenous, Spanish, and Portuguese traditions. Azucena, from the Arabic *sūsan* via Spanish, carries the layered history of Islamic influence on the Iberian Peninsula. Yara, with its Tupi-Guarani and Arabic roots, reflects the multicultural heritage of Brazil and the broader Latin American region.

### Regional Naming Preferences

Regional data reveals interesting patterns in floral name adoption. In the United States, classic names like Rose, Lily, and Violet dominate the top of floral name charts, while more adventurous choices like Azalea, Cosmos, and Wisteria are gaining ground. In the United Kingdom, Lavender, Poppy, and Daisy are particularly popular, reflecting British garden culture and the association of poppies with remembrance.

In Australia and New Zealand, native floral names are gaining traction. Banksia, named after the botanist Joseph Banks, and Waratah, from the Australian native flower, reflect a growing interest in indigenous flora. These names carry ecological and cultural significance that resonates with Australian and New Zealander parents seeking names with local identity.

In Canada, French-language floral names like Fleur, Liliane, and Rosemarie remain popular in Quebec, while English-language choices like Lily, Rose, and Violet dominate in other provinces. The bilingual nature of Canadian naming culture creates a unique dynamic where floral names can bridge both linguistic traditions.

### Cross-Linguistic Pronunciation Notes

Many floral names are remarkably consistent across languages. Lily, Rose, and Iris are pronounced similarly in English, French, German, and Spanish, making them excellent choices for multilingual families. Names like Hana (花/حنا) share the same pronunciation in both Japanese and Hebrew, though the meaning differs — "flower" in Japanese and "grace" or "favor" in Hebrew.

Names with diacritical marks or specific phonetic requirements, such as Azucena (Spanish) and Sakura (Japanese), may require slight pronunciation adjustments in different linguistic contexts. Parents should consider how their chosen name will be pronounced in all the languages their child may encounter throughout life.

### Regional Variations

Floral name preferences vary significantly by region. In Australia, native floral names like Banksia and Waratah are gaining popularity among parents seeking names with local ecological significance. These names carry a sense of place and environmental awareness that resonates with Australian identity.

## FAQ

### Q: What is the most popular floral baby name for girls in 2026?
A: Lily remains the most popular floral girl's name in 2026, consistently ranking in the top 15 across US, UK, and Canadian registries. Rose follows closely in the top 60, while Violet has climbed into the top 40. Poppy leads the wildflower category in the UK, while Lavender and Magnolia are the fastest-growing names in the US.

### Q: Are floral names considered trendy or timeless?
A: Floral names occupy a unique position — they are simultaneously timeless and trendy. Names like Rose, Lily, and Violet have been in continuous use for centuries and will remain classic. More adventurous choices like Azalea, Cosmos, and Wisteria are currently trending but have historical precedents that suggest they may become lasting additions to the naming landscape. The key is choosing a name that resonates with your family's values, regardless of its current popularity ranking.

### Q: What floral name means "flower" in different languages?
A: Hana means "flower" in Japanese and "joy" in Hebrew. Kusum means "flower" in Sanskrit. Fleur means "flower" in French. Fiore means "flower" in Italian. Azucena means "lily" in Spanish. Bloom and Blossom are English words that function as names. Sakura means "cherry blossom" in Japanese. These names offer a beautiful way to honor different cultural traditions while celebrating the universal symbolism of flowers.

### Q: How do I choose a floral name that won't feel dated in 20 years?
A: The best strategy is to choose a name with historical precedent rather than one that emerged purely from current trends. Names like Rose, Lily, Violet, Iris, and Jasmine have been in use for centuries and have proven longevity. Names that entered registries in the 2020s (like Cosmos, Zinnia, and Wisteria) may feel dated if they're perceived as "of this moment," but those with historical usage (like Zinnia, named after 18th-century botanist Johann Zinn) have a stronger claim to timelessness. Consider also how the name will age — will it suit a child, a professional woman, and an elder equally well?

### Q: Can floral names work for boys?
A: While this guide focuses on girl names, several floral names have been used for boys, particularly in non-English cultures. Florian (from Latin *flos*, flower) is a traditional boy's name in German-speaking countries. Lavender has occasionally been used for boys in the US. Rose has been used as a boy's name in some Southern US communities. Laurel, meaning "bay tree," has historical usage for boys in classical and medieval contexts. The floral naming tradition is not exclusively feminine, though contemporary usage skews heavily toward girls.

### Q: What are the most unique floral names that are still pronounceable?
A: For parents seeking distinctive options that remain accessible, consider Wisteria, Zinnia, Cosmos, Dahlia, Camellia, Azalea, Phlox, and Lupin. These names have clear phonetic profiles that most English speakers can pronounce on first encounter, while remaining sufficiently uncommon to avoid frequent name collisions in classroom and professional settings. Names like [Wisteria](/p/wisteria-baby-name.html) and [Azalea](/p/azalea-baby-name.html) are particularly distinctive while maintaining melodic appeal.
