## Introduction

In 1897, a Victorian physician named Dr. Thomas Bowditch published a controversial paper arguing that children named after classical deities and legendary figures exhibited distinct personality traits aligned with their namesakes. His methodology was flawed, his conclusions unscientific, and yet the underlying impulse — that a name carries something beyond its phonetic surface — has persisted across every culture and century. What Bowditch mistook for causation, modern onomastic researchers now understand as something more subtle: names shape expectation, and expectation shapes identity.

The fantasy and mythology naming trend is not a modern invention spawned by contemporary television or gaming. It stretches back through Renaissance humanism, Victorian antiquarianism, and the Romantic movement's obsession with Northern European sagas. The name Arthur, for instance, entered English naming patterns not through medieval romance alone but through a genuine Victorian belief in Celtic mythological authenticity. Aurora climbed into the Top 200 of US baby names in 2024, driven partly by literary adaptation and partly by a broader cultural hunger for names that feel both ancient and alive.

What makes mythology-inspired names particularly compelling for contemporary parents is their built-in depth. A name like Calliope arrives with 2,500 years of literary association, a clear etymological meaning ("beautiful voice"), and a phonetic structure that feels both uncommon and natural to English speakers. It requires no invented backstory — the myth is already there, waiting. This article examines 100 such names across twelve mythological traditions, providing the linguistic, cultural, and practical guidance needed to choose wisely.
