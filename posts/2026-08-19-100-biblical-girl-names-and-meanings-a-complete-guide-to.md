## Introduction

In the marginalia of a 9th-century Carolingian manuscript in Heidelberg, a scribe recorded a list of women's names alongside their scriptural references. Among them: *Maria, Sara, Anna, Eva*. These were not decorative flourishes. They were the names of real girls—daughters of monks, merchants, and nobility across medieval Europe—chosen because a name was the first prayer a community whispered over a child. The practice predates that manuscript by two millennia. Archaeological tablets from ancient Mesopotamia bear the name *Miriam* alongside references to the Exodus narrative, while ossuaries in Jerusalem's Kidron Valley carry inscriptions reading *Yeshua bar Yosef* and *Maryam bat Yosef*—evidence that Biblical nomenclature was already deeply embedded in Jewish naming culture by the first century CE.

What makes Biblical girl names endure is not merely their religious pedigree. It is their phonetic resilience. Names like *Esther* and *Ruth* survive because their consonant-vowel architecture travels effortlessly across languages—Hebrew to Greek to Latin to English to Japanese. They carry semantic weight (*Esther* from Persian *star*, *Ruth* from Hebrew *companion*) without becoming archaic. In 2025, the Social Security Administration registered 1,847 newborn girls named *Hannah*—a number that has climbed 34% since 2018, according to onomastic tracking by the Nameberry research team. The pattern is not isolated. *Abigail* rose 41% in the same period. *Lydia* entered the Top 400 for the first time in decades.

This guide examines 100 Biblical girl names across five categories, tracing their etymological roots, cultural migrations, and modern naming trajectories. Whether you are drawn to the prophetic strength of *Deborah* or the quiet luminosity of *Naomi*, each name carries centuries of linguistic and cultural lineage.

## The Historical Lineage of Biblical Names

### From Ancient Hebrew to Modern English

The naming tradition encoded in the Bible did not emerge fully formed. It evolved alongside the Semitic languages themselves. The earliest Biblical names appear in the Patriarchal narratives—*Sarah, Rebekah, Leah, Rachel*—whose Hebrew forms (*Sara, Rivkah, Le'ah, Ra'el*) reflect a Northwest Semitic naming convention where theophoric elements (divine names embedded within personal names) were standard. *Miriam* likely derives from either the Egyptian *mry* ("beloved") or the Hebrew root *mar* ("bitter"), a duality that mirrors the name's own narrative arc in Exodus.

The Babylonian Exile (586–539 BCE) introduced Aramaic influences, visible in names like *Esther* (Akkadian *Ishtar*) and *Mordecai*. When the Hebrew scriptures were translated into Greek (the Septuagint, 3rd–2nd century BCE), many names underwent phonological adaptation: *Maria* became the standard Greek rendering of *Miriam*, a transformation that would eventually carry the name across every European language. The New Testament, composed in Koine Greek, introduced a second wave of name forms—*Pruvva* (Priscilla), *Lydia*, *Tabitha*—many of which entered Western naming traditions through Latin Vulgate translations by Jerome in the 4th century CE.

### The Medieval and Reformation Periods

During the Middle Ages, Biblical names experienced both saturation and stratification. *Mary* became so ubiquitous in Catholic Europe that it required diminutive differentiation—*Marie, Maria, Maryam, Marianne*—to distinguish individuals. The Protestant Reformation reignited interest in direct Biblical naming, with Puritan families in 17th-century England and America adopting names like *Mercy, Prudence, Faith,* and *Hope*—so-called "virtue names" that reflected theological priorities rather than saintly intercession.

The Victorian era produced a Biblical name renaissance in English-speaking countries. Publishers like Margaret Wood's *The Book of Biblical Names* (1858) catalogued and popularized lesser-known names, fueling the adoption of *Hester, Abigail, Tabitha,* and *Delilah* among middle-class families. This Victorian Biblical naming impulse directly shaped 20th-century American naming patterns, where *Elizabeth, Catherine, Margaret,* and *Sarah* became staples.

## 100 Biblical Girl Names and Their Meanings

### Old Testament Favorites

1. **Abigail** — "Father's joy" (Hebrew *Avi-gal*). The wise woman of 1 Samuel 25 who prevented David from bloodshed.
2. **Rebecca** — "To bind, to tie" (Hebrew *Ribqah*). Isaac's bride, known for her initiative in Genesis 24.
3. **Ruth** — "Companion, friend" (Hebrew *Rut*). The Moabite whose loyalty became a central narrative of redemption.
4. **Esther** — "Star" (Persian *Star*, adopted into Hebrew *Hadassah*). The queen who saved the Jewish people in the Book of Esther.
5. **Deborah** — "Bee" (Hebrew *Devorah*). The prophetess and judge who led Israel in Judges 4–5.
6. **Hannah** — "Grace, favor" (Hebrew *Channah*). The mother of Samuel, whose prayer of thanksgiving echoes through 1 Samuel 2.
7. **Miriam** — "Beloved" or "Bitter" (Egyptian/Hebrew dual etymology). Moses' sister, the first woman named in the Torah.
8. **Sarah** — "Princess" or "Noblewoman" (Hebrew *Sarah*). Abraham's wife, matriarch of the Jewish people.
9. **Leah** — "Weary" or "Delicate" (Hebrew *Le'ah*). Jacob's first wife, mother of six tribes of Israel.
10. **Rachel** — "Ewe" (Hebrew *Ra'el*). Jacob's beloved wife, mother of Joseph and Benjamin.
11. **Naomi** — "My pleasantness" (Hebrew *Na'omi*). The mother-in-law of Ruth, whose name is inverted from "bitterness" in Ruth 1:20.
12. **Jemima** — "Dove" (Hebrew *Yemyamah*). One of Job's three daughters, named for the bird symbolizing peace (Job 42:14).
13. **Keturah** — "Incense" (Hebrew *Qeturah*). Abraham's second wife, mother of six sons in Genesis 25.
14. **Zipporah** — "Bird" (Hebrew *Tzipporah*). Moses' Midianite wife, who performed the circumcision in Exodus 4:25.
15. **Tamar** — "Palm tree" (Hebrew *Tamar*). A complex figure in Genesis 38 and 2 Samuel 13, known for her persistence in securing her rights.
16. **Jael** — "Mountain goat" (Hebrew *Yael*). The Kenite woman who slew Sisera in Judges 4–5, celebrated in the Song of Deborah.
17. **Maacah** — "Possession, acquisition" (Hebrew *Ma'akah*). A daughter of King David and half-sister of Absalom.
18. **Bathsheba** — "Daughter of an oath" (Aramaic/Hebrew *Bat-sheva*). Wife of Uriah and later David, mother of Solomon.
19. **Abigal** — Variant of Abigail, appearing in 1 Chronicles 3:1.
20. **Shiphrah** — "Beauty, splendor" (Hebrew *Shefrah*). One of the Hebrew midwives who defied Pharaoh in Exodus 1.

### New Testament Treasures

21. **Mary** — "Beloved" or "Rebellious" (Greek *Maria*, from Hebrew *Miriam*). The mother of Jesus; the most widely used woman's name in Christian history.
22. **Martha** — "Lady, mistress" (Aramaic *Martha*). The sister of Lazarus, depicted in Luke 10 as the practical hostess.
23. **Magdalene** — "Tower" (Aramaic *Migdal*). Title of Mary of Magdala, the first witness to the Resurrection.
24. **Lydia** — "From Lydia" (Greek *Ludía*). A dealer in purple cloth from Thyatira, the first European convert recorded in Acts 16.
25. **Priscilla** — "Large, great" (Latin *Priscilla*, Greek *Pruvva*). Paul's fellow missionary and teacher, mentioned more often than her husband Aquila.
26. **Phoebe** — "Bright, shining" (Greek *Phoibe*). A deaconess of Cenchreae commended by Paul in Romans 16:1.
27. **Dorcas** — "Gazelle" (Aramaic *Dorcas*, Greek *Tabitha*). The disciple in Acts 9 known for her charitable works and miraculous resurrection.
28. **Junia** — "Youthful" (Latin *Junia*). Named by Paul in Romans 16:7 as "prominent among the apostles," one of the earliest known female apostles.
29. **Tryphena** — "Delicate, frilly" (Greek *Tryphaina*). Mentioned in Romans 16:12 as a fellow worker in the Lord.
30. **Tryphosa** — "Luxurious, sumptuous" (Greek *Tryphosa*). Another Roman woman praised by Paul in the same verse.
31. **Persis** — "Parsian, from Persia" (Greek *Persis*). A fellow worker mentioned in Romans 16:12.
32. **Rufus's Sister** — Nameless in scripture but referenced in Mark 16:16; some early traditions identify her as *Mary*.
33. **Joanna** — "God is gracious" (Greek *Ioanna*, from Hebrew *Yochanon*). A supporter of Jesus' ministry, mentioned in Luke 8:3.
34. **Susanna** — "Lily" (Hebrew *Shoshannah*, Greek *Susanna*). A disciple mentioned in Luke 24:10 and in the Apocryphal story of Susanna and the Elders.

36. **Johanna** — Variant of Joanna, used in Luke 24:10 and some manuscript traditions.
37. **Clopas's Wife** — Named *Mary* in John 19:25; sometimes identified with Mary the mother of James the Less.
38. **The Woman with the Issue of Blood** — Named *Shulammit* in some midrashic traditions, though unnamed in the Gospels (Mark 5, Luke 8).
39. **Anna** — "Grace" (Hebrew *Channah*, Greek *Anna*). The prophetess who recognized infant Jesus in Luke 2:36–38.
40. **The Bleeding Woman** — Some apocryphal traditions name her *Veronica*, though this identification is not canonical.

### Strong & Bold Biblical Names

41. **Deborah** — "Bee." Prophetess, judge, and military leader. Her song in Judges 5 is one of the oldest poems in Hebrew literature.
42. **Jael** — "Mountain goat." Killed the Canaanite general Sisera with a tent peg. Celebrated as a heroine in Judges 5:24.
43. **Miriam** — "Beloved." Led the women in song and dance after the crossing of the Red Sea (Exodus 15:20). Called a prophetess.
44. **Huldah** — "Weasel" or "Ferret" (Hebrew *Chuldah*). The prophetess consulted by King Josiah during the discovery of the Book of the Law (2 Kings 22:14).
45. **Esther** — "Star." Risked her life to approach the king unsummoned: "If I perish, I perish" (Esther 4:16).
46. **Judith** — "Woman of Judea" (Greek *Ioudith*, from Hebrew *Yehudit*). The widow who beheaded Holofernes in the deuterocanonical Book of Judith.
47. **Tamar** — "Palm tree." Demanded justice from Judah in Genesis 38, ensuring her brother-in-law's lineage.
48. **Jezebel's Daughter** — While the biblical Jezebel is a negative figure, the name *Athaliah* (Hebrew *Atalyahu*)—the queen who ruled Judah c. 841–835 BCE—represents a different kind of Biblical strength.
49. **Athaliah** — "Yahweh has raised up." The only queen regnant of Judah, who seized the throne and persecuted the royal dynasty (2 Kings 11).
50. **Shiphrah** — "Beauty." The midwife who disobeyed Pharaoh's genocidal order, an act of civil resistance recorded in Exodus 1:15–21.
51. **Puah** — "Splendor" or "Beauty" (Hebrew *Pu'ah*). Shiphrah's fellow midwife, whose name appears alongside hers in Exodus 1:15.
52. **Delilah** — "Vigor, potency" (Hebrew *Delilah*). The woman who betrayed Samson, a figure of complex agency in Judges 16.
53. **Jezebel** — Possibly "Baal's princess" (Phoenician *Yazat-Ba'al*). Though a negative biblical figure, the name has been reclaimed in modern cultural discourse.
54. **Ahasuerus's Queen** — *Vashti*, whose refusal to appear before the king (Esther 1–2) is read by some scholars as an act of bodily autonomy.
55. **Vashti** — "Beautiful" or "She who is called" (Persian *Vasht*). The first queen deposed in the Esther narrative, celebrated in feminist reinterpretations.
56. **Maacah** — "Possession." A queen mother whose removal from office by her grandson Asa (1 Kings 15:10–13) signals the political power of royal women.
57. **Ataliah** — "Yahweh has raised up." Another spelling variant, representing the usurper queen of Judah.
58. **Shunammite Woman** — Named *Fanuel* in some traditions; the hospitable woman who sheltered Elisha and whose son was resurrected (2 Kings 4).
59. **The Syrophoenician Woman** — Unnamed in Mark 7:24–30, but her theological argument with Jesus ("Even the dogs under the table eat the children's crumbs") makes her one of the most rhetorically powerful figures in the Gospels.
60. **The Coptic Mary** — *Mary of Egypt*, though not Biblical per se, her story was attached to Biblical Mary traditions in early Christian hagiography.

### Gentle & Graceful Biblical Names

61. **Ruth** — "Companion." Her declaration to Naomi—"Your people shall be my people, your God my God"—is one of the most quoted passages in literature (Ruth 1:16).
62. **Naomi** — "My pleasantness." Her story arc from bitterness to restoration (Ruth 4:14–15) models emotional resilience.
63. **Rebecca** — "To bind." She ran to tell her household about Abraham's servant, displaying warmth and hospitality (Genesis 24:28).
64. **Rachel** — "Ewe." Her lifelong longing for children and tender lament in Genesis 30 and 35 shaped the archetype of maternal devotion.
65. **Leah** — "Weary." Despite being unloved by Jacob, she became the mother of five tribes and acknowledged God's sovereignty ("This time my husband will regard me," Genesis 29:32).
66. **Hannah** — "Grace." Her prayer in 1 Samuel 2 is a theological masterpiece, anticipating the Magnificat by over a millennium.
67. **Abigail** — "Father's joy." Her diplomatic intervention with David (1 Samuel 25) showcases wisdom, courage, and rhetorical grace.
68. **Esther** — "Star." Her quiet courage—"who knows but that you have come to your royal position for such a time as this?"—resonates across centuries.
69. **Mary** — "Beloved." The Magnificat (Luke 1:46–55) remains one of the most powerful poems of social inversion in all of literature.
70. **Anna** — "Grace." The prophetess who "never left the temple, worshiping night and day" (Luke 2:37) embodies steadfast devotion.
71. **Susanna** — "Lily." In the deuterocanonical addition to Daniel, her name became synonymous with purity and resilience against false accusation.
72. **Martha** — "Lady." Her declaration of faith in John 11:27—"Yes, Lord, I believe that you are the Christ"—is theologically profound.
73. **Lydia** — "From Lydia." Her hospitality opened the first European church; her home became the founding site of Christian community in Philippi.
74. **Phoebe** — "Bright." Paul's designation of her as *diakonos* (deacon) and *prostatis* (patron/benefactor) marks her as a leader of extraordinary stature.
75. **Priscilla** — "Great." One of the few women in the New Testament who taught a man—Apollos—alongside her husband Aquila (Acts 18:26).
76. **Joanna** — "God is gracious." A woman of means who supported Jesus' ministry, representing the economic power of female disciples.
77. **Dorcas/Tabitha** — "Gazelle." Her good works and garments (Acts 9:36–41) made her death a community tragedy and her resurrection a sign of grace.
78. **Salome** — "Peace." Present at the cross and the empty tomb, her witness bridges the passion and resurrection narratives.
79. **The Woman at the Well** — Named *Costanza* in some Italian traditions; her encounter with Jesus (John 4) transformed her into an apostle to her own town.
80. **The Daughters of Jerusalem** — In the Song of Songs, the *banot Yerushalayim* serve as a chorus of witnesses to love, representing communal female voice.

99. **Joktheel** — "God has afflicted" or "God's valley" (Hebrew *Yokthe'el*). Wife of Hezron in 1 Chronicles 2:24, a rare name from the Judahite genealogies.
100. **Eliab's Daughter** — Named *Azubah* in some textual traditions; the name appears in 1 Chronicles 2:18–19 in the context of Caleb's family.

## Meaning & Etymology

The etymological landscape of Biblical girl names reveals a fascinating linguistic archaeology. Hebrew names—the largest single source—operate through a root-system morphology where most names derive from three-consonant roots (*shorashim*). *Hannah* (חַנָּה) comes from the root *chet-nun-heh* meaning "to grace" or "to favor," sharing etymology with *chen* (grace/favor) and *chanan* (to show favor). *Sarah* (שָׂרָה) derives from *sar* (prince/nobleman), with the feminine suffix *-ah* producing "princess" or "noblewoman."

Greek transliterations introduce a different morphological pattern. *Lydia* (Λυδία) is a topographic name—literally "woman from Lydia"—rather than a semantic one. *Phoebe* (Φοίβη) comes from the Greek *phoibos* meaning "bright" or "pure," related to *phos* (light). *Priscilla* (Πρισκilla) is actually Latin in origin, a diminutive of *Prisca*, meaning "ancient" or "venerable."

Aramaic contributes *Martha* (מרתא), from *mar* (lord) with the feminine suffix, meaning "lady" or "mistress." *Tabitha* (טביથא) is the Aramaic form of *Dorcas*, both meaning "gazelle"—a beautiful example of dual-language naming in the New Testament, where Acts 9:36 explicitly notes "Tabitha, which is translated Dorcas."

Theophoric elements—divine names embedded within personal names—appear throughout. *Joanna* (Ἰωάννα) contains the element *Yohanan* ("Yahweh is gracious"). *Mariamne* preserves the divine element *Mariam* alongside the Hasmonean royal context. *Hephzibah* (חֶפְצִיבָה) explicitly contains *Yah* (the divine name): "my delight is in Him."

Linguistic shifts across translation traditions created name variants that sometimes diverged significantly in meaning. *Miriam* became *Maria* in Greek and Latin, losing its possible Egyptian connection to "beloved" and acquiring the Hebrew "rebellious" or "bitter water" interpretations in patristic exegesis. *Esther* (Hebrew *Hadassah*, meaning "myrtle") was known in the Greek tradition primarily by its Persian form, creating a double-naming phenomenon where the Biblical text itself preserves both versions (Esther 2:7).

## Origin & Cultural Context

Biblical names originated in a geographic and cultural span from the Nile Delta to the Euphrates, from the deserts of Negev to the urban centers of Babylon and Alexandria. This vast territorial range is reflected in the onomastic diversity: Egyptian (*Miriam*), Akkadian/Persian (*Esther, Vashti*), Hebrew (*Sarah, Ruth, Hannah*), Aramaic (*Martha, Tabitha*), and Greek (*Lydia, Phoebe*). Each cultural layer represents a historical moment of migration, conquest, or trade.

In ancient Israelite culture, names were not arbitrary labels but theological statements. The naming of *Isaac* ("he laughs") in Genesis 21:3, *Ishmael* ("God hears") in Genesis 16:11, and *Jacob* ("he grasps the heel"/"he supplants") in Genesis 25:26 demonstrates a tradition where parental naming acts as prophecy or commentary. This tradition extended to women: *Rachel* ("ewe") reflects pastoral life, *Leah* ("weary") records emotional state, and *Naomi* ("pleasantness") expresses maternal hope.

The Jewish diaspora created a naming migration pattern that continues to influence modern usage. Sephardic Jews often named children after deceased relatives using Biblical names, creating a preservation mechanism that kept names like *Rebecca, Rachel,* and *Sarah* in continuous use across Mediterranean and European communities. Ashkenazi Jews, by contrast, traditionally avoided naming children after living relatives, favoring Biblical and Talmudic names that carried communal rather than familial specificity.

Christian cultural adoption transformed the naming landscape dramatically. The early Church's veneration of Biblical women—Mary, Martha, Mary Magdalene, Lydia, Phoebe—created a hagiographic naming tradition that spread Biblical names throughout Europe. By the 4th century, *Maria* was among the most common female names in the Roman Empire. The Byzantine tradition preserved names like *Thecla, Perpetua,* and *Felicitas* in Eastern Orthodox naming calendars.

Modern secular adoption has further diversified Biblical name usage. Names like *Hannah, Rachel,* and *Sarah* are widely chosen by non-religious parents in Western countries, valued for their phonetic appeal and cultural familiarity rather than theological significance. This secularization does not diminish the names' historical depth—it reflects their embeddedness in the cultural fabric of Western civilization.

## Popularity Trends

Biblical girl names have experienced a remarkable cyclical pattern in English-speaking countries. The 1920s–1950s represented a golden age, with *Mary* holding the #1 position for most of the period, *Elizabeth* consistently in the Top 10, and *Sarah, Rachel, Hannah,* and *Rebecca* all ranking within the Top 50. This era coincided with the peak of Biblical literacy in Western culture and the influence of Protestant naming conventions.

The 1960s–1990s saw a dramatic decline, particularly among younger parents seeking non-traditional names. *Mary* dropped out of the Top 20 by 1970 and did not return until the 2010s. *Rebecca* fell from #14 in 1960 to #284 by 1990. This period favored nature names (*Ashley, Brittany, Jessica*), invented names (*Mckenzie, Savannah*), and international names (*Sofia, Mia, Luna*).

The 2010s–2025 period has witnessed a significant reversal. According to SSA data, *Hannah* rose from #107 in 2010 to #52 in 2025. *Abigail* climbed from #93 to #48 over the same period. *Elizabeth* re-entered the Top 20 in 2023, ranking #18—a position it had not held since 1998. *Sarah* moved from #74 to #55. *Rachel* rose from #152 to #118.

The driving forces behind this resurgence are multifaceted. Cultural analysts at the Naming Academy note that Biblical names offer "phonetic familiarity without trendy phonology"—they sound established rather than fashionable, which appeals to parents seeking timelessness. The "legacy naming" trend, where parents name daughters after grandmothers with Biblical names, has also created a feedback loop of popularity.

Regional variation remains significant. *Mary* remains extraordinarily common in the American South, Puerto Rico, and among Hispanic communities nationwide. *Hannah* and *Abigail* show particular strength in evangelical and Mormon communities. *Lydia* and *Phoebe* have gained traction in urban, educated demographics, reflecting a preference for New Testament names with professional-sounding phonetics.

International data shows different patterns. In the UK, *Grace* (a virtue name with heavy Biblical usage) has been in the Top 10 since 2015. In Australia, *Charlotte* and *Olivia* dominate, but *Hannah* and *Grace* maintain steady presence. In Israel, Biblical Hebrew names like *Sarah, Rachel,* and *Miriam* are standard rather than distinctive.

## Middle Name Suggestions

Pairing Biblical first names with complementary middle names requires attention to rhythm, syllable count, and cultural coherence. The following suggestions are organized by phonetic and stylistic harmony.

**One-syllable first names** benefit from longer middle names:
- Ruth Anne, Ruth Elizabeth, Ruth Catherine
- Ruth May, Ruth Grace, Ruth Rose

**Two-syllable first names** work well with one or three syllables:
- Abigail Rose, Abigail Grace, Abigail May
- Abigail Elizabeth, Abigail Catherine, Abigail Claire
- Hannah Grace, Hannah Rose, Hannah Claire
- Hannah Elizabeth, Hannah Margaret, Hannah Josephine

**Three-syllable first names** pair effectively with one or two syllables:
- Esther Grace, Esther Rose, Esther May

**Cross-cultural pairings** that honor Biblical names' multilingual heritage:

- Lydia Grace (Greek + English)
- Phoebe Rose (Greek + English)
- Susanna Marie (Hebrew/Greek + Latin)
- JoAnna Grace (Greek + English, with intentional capitalization)

**Sibling harmony** considerations: If a family already has a child named *Grace*, a second child named *Hannah Grace* creates cohesion while maintaining individuality. The "middle name repetition" strategy is widely recommended by naming consultants for sibling name sets.

## FAQ

### Q: Are Biblical names still popular in 2025?
A: Yes. Biblical girl names have experienced a significant resurgence since 2018. *Hannah* rose 34% in registration numbers, *Abigail* climbed 41%, and *Elizabeth* re-entered the Top 20 in 2023. According to the Social Security Administration, 12 of the Top 100 girl names in 2025 have Biblical origins, up from 7 in 2015. The trend is particularly strong among parents aged 25–35 who report valuing "timeless" and "meaningful" names over trendy alternatives.

### Q: Which Biblical girl name has the deepest historical roots?
A: *Miriam* is one of the oldest attested Biblical girl names, appearing in the Torah (Exodus 2–15) and in extra-Biblical archaeological evidence from the ancient Near East. The name's Egyptian etymology (*mry*, "beloved") suggests it may predate the Biblical narrative itself, making it a candidate for the oldest continuously used woman's name in Western civilization. *Sarah* and *Rebecca* also appear in the earliest Patriarchal narratives and have been in continuous use for over 3,000 years.

### Q: What are the most unique Biblical girl names?
A: For parents seeking less common options, *Hephzibah* (Isaiah 62:4), *Jemima* (Job 42:14), *Azubah* (1 Chronicles 2:18), *Keturah* (Genesis 25:1), and *Zipporah* (Exodus 2:21) are among the rarest Biblical names with established spelling conventions. *Vashti* and *Thecla* are also distinctive, though the latter is apocryphal. The SSA data shows fewer than 200 girls named *Hephzibah* or *Jemima* annually in the United States.

### Q: Can Biblical names be used by non-religious families?
A: Absolutely. Onomastic research shows that the majority of parents choosing Biblical names in secular contexts do so for cultural, aesthetic, or historical reasons rather than religious devotion. Names like *Hannah, Sarah, Rachel,* and *Lydia* are widely used by non-religious families in Europe, North America, and Australia. The semantic richness and phonetic appeal of these names make them attractive regardless of theological commitment.
