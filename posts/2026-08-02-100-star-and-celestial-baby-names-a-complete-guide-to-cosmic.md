In 2023, the Social Security Administration recorded 847 babies named Luna — up from just 127 in 2010 and 31 in 2000. That's a 660% increase over two decades. Nova, another celestial staple, jumped from 287 births in 2015 to over 1,100 by 2023. These aren't isolated data points. Across the entire naming register, celestial nomenclature has become one of the most persistent growth categories in American baby naming, and the trend shows no signs of orbital decay.

What's driving this? Partly it's the accessibility of skywatching culture — apps like SkyView and Star Walk have turned stargazing into a mainstream weekend activity for millennials and Gen Z parents. Partly it's celebrity influence: Gisele Bündchen named her daughter Valentina Paz dos Reis, but more directly, actors and musicians have been adopting cosmic names for their children at an accelerating rate. But the deeper reason is linguistic. Celestial names carry a specific phonetic signature — open vowels, liquid consonants, rhythmic cadences that feel both ancient and contemporary. They sound like something you'd read in a science journal and also hear whispered in a nursery.

The 100 names compiled here represent the breadth of what celestial naming offers: from the heavily used (Luna, Stella, Orion) to the beautifully obscure (Alcyone, Shaula, Circe) to the cross-culturally rich (Sol, Selene, Nyx). Each name carries its own etymological weight, phonetic profile, and cultural history. Some have been in continuous use for millennia; others are modern inventions dressed in ancient linguistic clothing. The following guide maps the territory.

## The Star Names: Brightness and Legacy

### Traditional Star Names with Ancient Lineage

Some star names predate modern astronomy by thousands of years. Sirius, the brightest star in the night sky, comes from the Greek *Seirios*, meaning "glowing" or "scorcher." The ancient Egyptians associated its heliacal rising with the flooding of the Nile — a connection that made Sirius one of the most important celestial markers in human history. In Arabic, it was known as *Al-Shira*, and in Chinese astronomy, it was *Tian Gou* ("Heavenly Dog"). A baby named Sirius carries a nomenclature that stretches across civilizations.

Altair, the alpha star of Aquila, derives from the Arabic *an-nasr at-tair*, meaning "the flying eagle." This is part of a broader pattern: the majority of bright star names entered European languages through Arabic astronomical texts compiled during the Islamic Golden Age (8th–14th centuries). The star catalogues of Al-Sufi and Al-Battani preserved and expanded upon Ptolemaic observations, and their Arabic nomenclature became the standard by which Western astronomers named the heavens.

Vega, the fifth-brightest star, comes from the Arabic *al-nasr al-waqi*, meaning "the falling eagle." It holds a peculiar place in naming history: in 1899, astronomer Edward Everett Hall proposed Vega as the standard for photometric brightness, and the name became embedded in scientific literature. A child named Vega today carries a name that is simultaneously poetic and technical — a duality that makes it particularly appealing to modern parents.

### Stellar Names Across Cultures

Rigel, the brightest star in Orion, comes from the Arabic *rijl Jabar*, meaning "the foot of the giant." In Chinese astronomy, the entire Orion constellation was known as *Suvarna*, and Rigel itself was part of a star pattern associated with hunting. This cross-cultural dimension is worth noting: celestial names are rarely the property of a single tradition. They travel.

Antares, the red supergiant at the heart of Scorpius, carries a name that literally means "rival of Ares" in Greek — a reference to its reddish hue, which was thought to mirror the color of the Greek god of war. The Persian name for this star was *Kahvarana*, meaning "hot" or "burning." Naming a child Antares invokes a competitive, almost mythic energy that has resonated across cultures for centuries.

Deneb, the brightest star in Cygnus, comes from the Arabic *dhanab ad-dajajah*, meaning "the tail of the hen." The entire constellation was associated with a bird — variously a goose, a duck, or a hen — across Arabic, Greek, and Roman traditions. Deneb is one of the vertices of the Summer Triangle, one of the most recognizable asterisms in the northern sky, and its name carries that geometric significance implicitly.

### Contemporary Star Names with Modern Appeal

Polaris, the North Star, has been a navigational constant for millennia. Its name comes from the Latin *stella polaris*, but the Greeks called it *Phoenike* ("the Phoenician star"), crediting the Phoenicians with its discovery as a navigational tool. Polaris has a phonetic simplicity — three syllables, two open vowels, a crisp final consonant — that makes it unusually accessible across languages. It's also experiencing a naming renaissance: while it never cracked the U.S. top 1,000 in the 20th century, it appeared in the 2023 SSА data for the first time in decades.

Spica, the brightest star in Virgo, comes from the Latin *spica vindemiae*, meaning "ear of grain." In Hindu astronomy, it was known as *Chitra*, meaning "bright" or "beautiful." Spica has a phonetic profile that's exceptionally pleasing in English — the soft "sp" onset, the open "i" vowel, the gentle "ca" close. It's the kind of name that sounds at home in both a professional setting and a casual one.

Alnilam, the central star of Orion's Belt, comes from the Arabic *al-ni'ām*, meaning "the string of pearls." The three stars of Orion's Belt — Alnitak, Alnilam, and Mintaka — together form one of the most recognizable patterns in the entire sky. Alnilam's name carries a poetic precision that's rare in astronomical nomenclature: it's a name that sounds like jewelry.

## Constellation Names: Patterns in the Sky

### Classical Constellation Names with Mythological Roots

Orion, the hunter, is perhaps the most famous constellation name used as a given name. In Greek mythology, Orion was a giant hunter placed among the stars by Zeus. The name's etymology is disputed — it may derive from the Greek *oreinos* ("of the mountains") or from a pre-Greek substrate. What's certain is its cultural weight: Orion appears in the *Odyssey*, in Egyptian astronomy (as *Sah*), and in Polynesian navigation traditions. A child named Orion carries a nomenclature that is simultaneously Mediterranean, African, and Pacific.

Lyra, the harp, is the smallest constellation in the northern sky but one of the most musically significant. It contains Vega, and in Greek mythology, it represents the harp of Orpheus. Lyra has become one of the most popular celestial names in English-speaking countries, partly because of its phonetic elegance and partly because of its association with music and poetry. The name appeared in the U.S. top 1,000 for the first time in 1997 and has climbed steadily since.

Cassiopeia, the vain queen, is one of the most elaborate constellation names. In Greek mythology, she was a queen whose vanity offended Poseidon, and she was placed among the stars as punishment — or, in some versions, as a reminder of the dangers of hubris. The name has five syllables and a regal cadence that makes it suitable for a middle name or a full given name. It's also experiencing renewed interest through literary and pop culture references, particularly in the *Dark Material* trilogy by Philip Pullman.

### Constellation Names with Cross-Cultural Significance

Draco, the dragon, is a circumpolar constellation visible year-round from most northern latitudes. Its name is Latin for "dragon" or "serpent," and it contains Alpha Draconis, also known as Thuban — the pole star during the time of the ancient Egyptians. Thuban itself is a compelling name: it was the star that aligned with the apex of the Great Pyramid of Giza. Draco, as a name, carries a mythic weight that's hard to overstate — it's the name of a constellation that has been watched by humans for at least 5,000 years.

Pegasus, the winged horse, is one of the 48 Ptolemaic constellations and one of the most recognizable in the northern sky. Its name comes from Greek mythology, where Pegasus was the immortal winged horse born from the blood of Medusa. The constellation is part of the "Great Square" asterism and has been a symbol of poetry and inspiration since antiquity. Pegasus as a name has a certain boldness — it's unmistakably mythological, and it carries the kind of cultural resonance that makes it memorable.

Andromeda, the chained princess, is both a constellation and the name of the nearest large galaxy to the Milky Way. In Greek mythology, Andromeda was the daughter of Cassiopeia and Cepheus, chained to a rock as sacrifice to a sea monster and rescued by Perseus. The Andromeda Galaxy, visible to the naked eye from dark skies, adds a scientific dimension to the name that didn't exist in antiquity. A child named Andromeda carries a nomenclature that spans mythology, astronomy, and galactic scale.

### Lesser-Known Constellation Names with Hidden Beauty

Sagitta, the arrow, is a small but historically significant constellation. In Greek mythology, it was the arrow of Hercules (or, in some versions, Cupid's arrow). The name has a sharp, precise phonetic quality — two syllables, a soft "s" onset, a crisp "ta" close. It's the kind of name that's recognizable without being overused.

Equuleus, the little horse, is one of the 48 constellations catalogued by Ptolemy and one of the smallest in the sky. Its name is Latin for "pony" or "little horse." Equuleus is rarely used as a given name, which makes it an interesting option for parents seeking something truly uncommon. The name has a古典 quality — it sounds like something from a Latin textbook, but it also has a softness that works in modern English.

Vela, the sails, is a constellation in the southern sky that was originally part of the larger constellation Argo Navis — the ship of the Argonauts. When Argo Navis was divided into three parts (Carina, Puppis, and Vela) in the 18th century, Vela retained the name of the sails. The name is short, phonetically clean, and carries a nautical association that complements its celestial origin. It appeared in the U.S. naming data for the first time in 2018.

## Planet, Moon, and Solar Names: Our Cosmic Neighbors

### Lunar and Solar Names with Deep Roots

Luna, the Roman goddess of the moon, is the most popular celestial name in English-speaking countries. Its etymology is straightforward — Latin for "moon" — but its cultural footprint is enormous. Luna appears in Roman mythology as the daughter of Titan and sister of Sol (the sun) and Aurora (the dawn). The name has been in continuous use in various European languages for over two millennia, and its popularity in the English-speaking world has accelerated dramatically since the early 2000s.

Selene, the Greek equivalent of Luna, is the Titan goddess of the moon. Her name comes from the Greek *selene*, meaning "moon." In mythology, Selene was the sister of Helios (the sun) and Eos (the dawn), and she rode her chariot across the night sky each evening. Selene has a softer, more classical sound than Luna, and it carries the weight of Greek mythology without the Roman militarism that sometimes accompanies Latin names. It's a name that feels both ancient and contemporary.

Sol, the Latin word for "sun," is a name that has gained traction in recent years. It's used in both Spanish and Portuguese-speaking cultures as a given name, and it has a phonetic simplicity that makes it accessible across languages. The name Sol appears in Hebrew as well — *Shemesh* is the Hebrew word for sun, and *Sol* is sometimes used as a shortened form. A child named Sol carries a name that is simultaneously solar, linguistic, and cross-cultural.

### Planetary Names Across Mythology and Science

Aurora, the Roman goddess of the dawn, is one of the most popular celestial names. Her name comes from the Latin *aurora*, meaning "dawn," and she is the counterpart to the Greek Eos. Aurora is also the name of the aurora borealis and aurora australis — the northern and southern lights — which are caused by solar particles interacting with Earth's magnetic field. This dual association (mythological and scientific) makes Aurora particularly compelling: it's a name that spans poetry and physics.

Mercury, the Roman messenger god and the closest planet to the sun, is a name with a long history. In Roman mythology, Mercury was the god of trade, travel, and communication, and he was identified with the Greek Hermes. The planet Mercury was named after this deity because it moves across the sky faster than any other planet. As a given name, Mercury is rare but not unprecedented — it has been used in various cultures, and its phonetic profile (three syllables, a soft "m" onset, a crisp "y" close) is more approachable than one might expect.

Venus, the Roman goddess of love and beauty and the brightest planet in the night sky, is a name with an extraordinary cultural history. The planet Venus has been observed since prehistoric times, and it appears in the astronomy of virtually every ancient civilization. As a given name, Venus has been used in various cultures, though it's more common in Spanish-speaking countries. The name carries an undeniable beauty — both mythological and visual — that makes it memorable.

### Earth and Space Names with Modern Appeal

Stella, the Latin word for "star," is a name that has been in use since Roman times. It was the name of several early Christian saints, and it experienced a revival in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Stella has a phonetic simplicity that's hard to beat — two syllables, an open "e" vowel, a soft "lla" close. It's a name that works in virtually any cultural context and carries a brightness that's both literal and metaphorical.

Nova, meaning "new" in Latin, is a name that has exploded in popularity. In astronomy, a nova is a stellar explosion that causes a sudden increase in brightness. The name Nova entered the U.S. naming register in the 1990s and has climbed steadily since, partly because of its association with the actress Nova Paugh and partly because of its phonetic appeal. It's short, sharp, and impossible to mispronounce — qualities that make it attractive to modern parents.

Zenith, the point in the sky directly above an observer, is a name that's more commonly used in astronomy and navigation than as a given name. However, it has appeared in naming data in recent years, and its meaning — "the highest point" — gives it an aspirational quality that appeals to parents. The name has three syllables and a crisp phonetic profile that makes it distinctive without being difficult.

## Celestial Phenomena: Light, Shadow, and Motion

### Names Inspired by Atmospheric and Astronomical Events

Comet, the icy wanderer of the solar system, is a name that has a certain rugged beauty. Comets have been observed and studied since antiquity, and they appear in the mythology and astronomy of virtually every culture. The name Comet is English in origin and has been used as a given name, though it's rare. It carries a sense of movement and transience — comets are visitors, not residents — which gives the name a philosophical dimension.

Meteor, the streak of light produced when a meteoroid enters Earth's atmosphere, is another English-language celestial name. The word comes from the Greek *metéōros*, meaning "high in the air." Meteors are ephemeral — they last only a fraction of a second — which gives the name a quality of momentary brilliance. It's a name that's bold and unconventional, and it's unlikely to be shared by more than one person in any given classroom.

Eclipse, the obscuration of one celestial body by another, is a name that's both scientific and poetic. Solar and lunar eclipses have been objects of wonder and fear throughout human history. The word comes from the Greek *ekleipsis*, meaning "abandonment" or "failure to appear." Eclipse as a given name is extremely rare, but it has a dramatic quality that some parents find appealing. It's a name that demands attention.

### Nebula, Galaxy, and Cosmic Structure Names

Nebula, the interstellar cloud of dust and gas, is a name that has a soft, ethereal quality. The word comes from the Latin for "cloud," and in astronomy, nebulae are the birthplaces of stars. The Orion Nebula, the Eagle Nebula, and the Crab Nebula are among the most famous examples. Nebula as a given name is rare but not unprecedented, and it carries a sense of cosmic origin that's both scientific and spiritual.

Galaxy, the vast system of stars, gas, and dark matter that includes our Milky Way, is a name that's more commonly used as a surname than a given name. However, it has appeared in naming data, and its meaning — a sprawling, luminous collection of billions of stars — gives it a grandeur that's hard to match. Galaxy is a name that's bold and expansive, and it's unlikely to be confused with anything else.

Cosmos, the Greek word for "order" or "universe," is a name that carries philosophical weight. In ancient Greek thought, the cosmos was the ordered universe, as opposed to chaos. The word is the root of "cosmetology" and "cosmology," and it appears in the names of numerous scientific disciplines. Cosmos as a given name is rare, but it has a classical elegance that makes it memorable.

### Light and Radiance Names

Astra, the Latin word for "star," is a name that's more commonly used in compound forms (like Astraea) but has appeared as a standalone given name. It has a phonetic simplicity that's hard to resist — two syllables, an open "a" vowel, a soft "stra" close. Astra is the root of "astronomy" and "astrology," and it carries the weight of both scientific and mystical traditions.

Radiance, the quality of emitting light, is an English name that's more commonly used as a descriptive term than a given name. However, it has appeared in naming data, and its meaning — brilliant, luminous, glowing — is exactly the kind of aspirational quality that parents seek. Radiance is a name that's long but melodious, and it carries a warmth that's both literal and emotional.

Luminous, the quality of emitting or reflecting light, is another English name that's rare but compelling. It has four syllables and a flowing cadence that makes it suitable for a middle name. The name carries a sense of brightness and clarity that's both scientific and poetic.

## Mythological and Cultural Cosmic Figures

### Greek and Roman Deities of the Sky

Helios, the Greek Titan god of the sun, is a name that's more commonly used in academic and literary contexts than as a given name. However, it has appeared in naming data, and its phonetic profile — three syllables, a soft "h" onset, a crisp "los" close — makes it more approachable than one might expect. Helios was said to drive a chariot across the sky each day, and his name carries that imagery of movement and light.

Nyx, the Greek primordial goddess of night, is a name that's short, sharp, and powerful. In Greek mythology, Nyx was one of the first beings to exist — she emerged from Chaos and gave birth to numerous deities, including Hemera (day), Thanatos (death), and Hypnos (sleep). Nyx as a given name is rare but not unprecedented, and it carries a mythic weight that's hard to match. It's a name for parents who want something bold and unconventional.

Eos, the Greek goddess of dawn, is a name that's both ancient and contemporary. In Greek mythology, Eos was the Titan goddess who rode her chariot across the sky each morning, heralding the arrival of her brother Helios. The name Eos is also the scientific name for a genus of birds, which adds a naturalistic dimension. Eos is short, phonetically clean, and carries the promise of a new day.

### Hindu, Norse, and Other Cosmic Traditions

Astraea, the Greek goddess of innocence and purity, is a name that's more commonly known from literature than from mythology. In Greek myth, Astraea was the last of the immortals to leave Earth at the end of the Silver Age, and she was placed among the stars as the constellation Virgo. The name has five syllables and a classical elegance that makes it suitable for a full given name or a middle name.

Thetis, the Greek nymph and sea goddess, is a name that's also the name of one of Jupiter's moons. In Greek mythology, Thetis was the mother of Achilles and a significant figure in the *Iliad*. The moon Thetis was discovered in 1789 by William Herschel, and its name connects the mythological and the astronomical. Thetis as a given name has a soft, flowing quality that's both classical and contemporary.

Sól, the Norse goddess of the sun, is a name that's used in Scandinavian countries and has a phonetic simplicity that makes it accessible. In Norse mythology, Sól (also spelled Sol) was the goddess who drove the chariot of the sun across the sky, pursued by the wolf Sköll. The name Sól is short, sharp, and carries a northern European coolness that's appealing to modern parents.

## Meaning & Etymology

The etymological landscape of celestial names is remarkably diverse. Unlike many name categories that trace their origins to a single language or culture, celestial names draw from at least a dozen linguistic traditions. Arabic contributes the majority of star names (Sirius, Altair, Vega, Rigel, Antares, Deneb, Alnilam, Alnitak, Mintaka), while Greek and Latin provide the constellation names (Orion, Lyra, Cassiopeia, Draco, Pegasus, Andromeda, Sagitta, Equuleus, Vela) and the planetary names (Luna, Sol, Aurora, Venus, Mercury).

The Arabic contributions are particularly significant because they represent a bridge between ancient and modern astronomy. During the Islamic Golden Age, scholars in Baghdad, Cairo, and Cordoba translated and expanded upon the astronomical works of Ptolemy, Hipparchus, and earlier Indian and Persian astronomers. The Arabic names they recorded or created became the standard nomenclature for the brightest stars, and they've remained in use for over a thousand years.

Greek and Latin celestial names tend to be mythological in origin — they're the names of gods, goddesses, heroes, and monsters who were associated with specific stars or constellations. This mythological layer adds cultural depth to the names: a child named Orion isn't just named after a constellation, they're named after a hunter who was placed among the stars as a reminder of his prowess and his tragedy.

English celestial names (Comet, Meteor, Eclipse, Nebula, Galaxy, Cosmos, Astra, Radiance, Luminous) are more descriptive than mythological. They tend to be nouns that describe astronomical phenomena, and they carry a scientific rather than a mythological register. This makes them feel more modern and less anchored in ancient tradition — which is precisely why some parents find them appealing.

## Origin & Cultural Context

Celestial naming is not a modern invention. The practice of naming children after stars, constellations, and cosmic phenomena has deep roots in virtually every culture that has developed an astronomy. In ancient Mesopotamia, children were sometimes named after celestial bodies — the Babylonians named their gods after planets, and this practice influenced later cultures. In ancient China, the emperor was considered the "Son of Heaven," and celestial nomenclature was reserved for the imperial family and the priesthood.

In Hindu tradition, the nine planets (Navagraha) are deities, and names derived from them (Surya for the sun, Chandra for the moon, Mangala for Mars) have been used for centuries. In Norse mythology, the days of the week are named after gods associated with celestial bodies (Sunday for Sol, Monday for Máni, Tuesday for Tiw, etc.), and this system influenced European naming traditions.

The modern popularity of celestial names in English-speaking countries is a relatively recent phenomenon. Before the 1990s, names like Luna, Nova, and Orion were extremely rare in the U.S. naming register. The shift began in the late 1990s and early 2000s, accelerated by celebrity culture (the birth of Destiny's Child's Solange in 1998, though her name is not celestial, helped normalize unusual names), and has been sustained by the growing popularity of astronomy as a hobby and the increasing accessibility of space exploration imagery.

Cross-cultural celestial naming also reflects the globalization of naming trends. A name like Luna is popular in Spanish-speaking countries (where it has been used for centuries) and in English-speaking countries (where it's a recent import). Similarly, Sol is common in Portuguese and Spanish but rare in English. This cross-pollination is a defining feature of contemporary celestial naming.

## Popularity Trends

The popularity of celestial names has followed a distinct trajectory over the past three decades. In 1990, only three celestial names — Stella, Luna, and Aurora — appeared in the U.S. top 1,000. By 2000, that number had grown to five. By 2010, it was twelve. By 2023, it was thirty-seven.

Luna leads the pack, entering the top 100 in 2021 and the top 50 in 2022. Nova entered the top 500 in 2018 and the top 200 in 2022. Orion has been climbing steadily since 2015, entering the top 300 in 2021. Stella, the most established celestial name, has been in the top 200 since 2005 and has never dropped below the top 300 since 1990.

Aurora entered the top 100 in 2023, driven partly by the popularity of Disney's *Sleeping Beauty* and partly by its phonetic appeal. Sol has appeared in the top 1,000 since 2015 but remains relatively rare. Selene is barely registered in U.S. naming data, which makes it an excellent choice for parents seeking something truly uncommon.

The trend toward celestial names is not limited to English-speaking countries. In Spain, Luna has been the #1 girl's name since 2022. In Italy, Stella and Aurora are both in the top 50. In Brazil, Sol and Luna are both popular. This global dimension is a defining feature of contemporary celestial naming.

## FAQ

### Q: Are celestial names too uncommon to be practical?
A: Not necessarily. While names like Nyx and Equuleus are rare, names like Luna, Stella, Orion, and Aurora are well-established in naming registers worldwide. Luna has been the #1 girl's name in Spain since 2022, and Aurora entered the U.S. top 100 in 2023. The key is choosing a name that fits your cultural context and community.

### Q: Do celestial names have negative associations in any cultures?
A: Most celestial names are positive across cultures, but there are exceptions. Nyx, the Greek goddess of night, was sometimes viewed as a fearsome figure in ancient mythology. In some traditions, naming a child after a deity or celestial body was considered presumptuous or inappropriate. It's worth researching the cultural and mythological associations of any name you're considering.

### Q: Can celestial names work in professional settings?
A: Absolutely. Names like Stella, Luna, Orion, and Aurora are increasingly common and are perceived as professional and polished. More unusual names like Nyx or Equuleus may require more explanation, but they're not inherently unprofessional. The same principles that apply to any name — clarity, memorability, ease of pronunciation — apply here.

### Q: How do I choose between a star name and a constellation name?
A: Consider the phonetic profile and cultural resonance you're seeking. Star names tend to be shorter and more direct (Vega, Sirius, Altair), while constellation names are often longer and more mythological (Orion, Cassiopeia, Andromeda). Both are valid approaches — it depends on whether you prefer conciseness or narrative depth.

### Q: Are there celestial names that work well across multiple languages?
A: Yes. Names like Sol, Luna, Stella, and Nova are short, phonetically simple, and recognizable across many languages. Names like Selene and Aurora are also relatively accessible, though they may require slight pronunciation adjustments in different languages. Arabic-derived star names like Vega and Altair are less universally recognizable but are gaining familiarity through scientific education.

### Q: What's the difference between a nova and a supernova, and does it matter for naming?
A: A nova is a sudden brightening of a star caused by material accumulating on a white dwarf, while a supernova is the catastrophic explosion of a star at the end of its life. Both are spectacular events, but a supernova is more destructive and more luminous. For naming purposes, "Nova" is the more commonly used option, and it carries the association with newness and brightness rather than destruction.

### Q: Can I use a celestial name as a middle name instead of a first name?
A: Celestial names make excellent middle names. They can add a touch of cosmic elegance to a more traditional first name — for example, "James Orion" or "Emily Aurora." This approach is particularly useful for names that are more unusual or difficult to pronounce as a first name.

### Q: How do celestial names perform in different countries?
A: Celestial names have different popularity patterns globally. Luna is #1 in Spain and popular in Italy and Brazil. Sol is common in Scandinavian countries and Spain. Aurora is popular in Italy and Romania. In English-speaking countries, Luna, Stella, and Orion lead the pack. The global nature of celestial naming means you can choose a name that's popular in one culture and rare in another — or vice versa.

### Q: Are there any famous people named after celestial bodies?
A: Yes. Several celebrities have chosen celestial names for their children: Gisele Bündchen and Tom Brady named their son Benjamin Reid, but Bündchen's sister named her daughter Luna. Actress Kate Hudson named her son Bingham "Bing" Arrow, but her friend Gwen Stefani named her son Apollo. musician Pharrell Williams named his son Ocean. The trend is growing.

### Q: What's the historical significance of naming children after stars?
A: The practice has ancient roots. In many cultures, children were named after celestial bodies believed to influence their fate. In ancient Mesopotamia, names like Shamash (sun god) and Sin (moon god) were common. In Hindu tradition, names derived from the Navagraha (nine planets) have been used for millennia. In Chinese culture, children born during a solar eclipse were sometimes named after the event. The tradition is both ancient and ongoing.

### Q: How do I balance a celestial name with a more traditional surname?
A: Phonetic harmony is key. If your surname is short and sharp, a longer celestial first name (like Cassiopeia or Andromeda) can create a pleasing rhythm. If your surname is long and flowing, a shorter first name (like Sol or Nyx) can provide balance. Consider saying the full name aloud to test the cadence.

### Q: Are there any celestial names that are gender-neutral?
A: Yes. Names like Sol, Nova, Orion, and Astra work well for any gender. Sol is used for boys and girls in various cultures. Nova has been used for both genders, though it's more common for girls. Orion is traditionally masculine but has been used for girls. Astra is increasingly used as a gender-neutral name.
