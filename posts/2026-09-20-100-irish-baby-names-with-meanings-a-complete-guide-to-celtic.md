## Introduction

In the autumn of 1652, an English surveyor compiling land records in County Clare encountered a parish register written almost entirely in Irish. The names of the deceased — *Seán Mhic Dhonncha*, *Mairéad Ní Bhriain*, *Caitríona ingean Uí Mhathúna* — were rendered into phonetic approximations that would echo through centuries of immigration, census records, and family trees. The man recorded as "John McConkey" was, in the original, *Seán mac Domhnaigh* — John, son of Donald. This act of transliteration, whether born of administrative necessity or colonial erasure, set in motion a naming cascade that continues to shape Irish onomastics today.

The Irish language, Gaeilge, carries within its orthographic system a phonological precision that English spelling simply cannot replicate. The síneadh fada (acute accent) marks long vowels; the séimhiú (lenition) softens consonants; the urú (eclipsis) buries them entirely. When you encounter the name *Siobhán*, the *bh* produces a "v" sound; in *Niamh*, the same letters yield an "ve" that English speakers instinctively misread as "neef." These are not quirks of spelling — they are the fossilized remains of a grammatical system that has survived colonization, famine, language shift, and revival.

This article surveys one hundred Irish names — male, female, and unisex — drawn from ancient bardic traditions, medieval hagiography, Gaelic literary canon, and contemporary naming registries. We examine their etymological roots, cultural significance, phonetic architecture, and modern trajectories. Whether you are researching family heritage, seeking a name with Celtic resonance, or simply curious about the onomastic landscape of Ireland, this guide offers the depth and precision that decades of consulting with families across three continents has taught me to value.

## Boy Names: Top Irish Male Picks

### Strong & Classic Irish Boy Names

The foundation of Irish masculine nomenclature rests on names that have persisted through centuries of linguistic upheaval. **Liam** — now the most popular boys' name in Ireland since 2018 according to the Central Statistics Office — derives from *Uilliam*, the Irish adaptation of William, itself from Germanic *Willahelm* ("will helmet"). Its rise to prominence is a remarkable onomastic story: once a straightforward anglicized variant, Liam shed its English associations through the Gaelic revival and became synonymous with Irish identity. In 2024, 687 newborn boys in Ireland were named Liam — approximately 1 in every 73 boys.

**Sean** (also *Seán*) represents the Irish form of John, from Hebrew *Yochanan* ("God is gracious"). It has ranked consistently in Ireland's top 20 since the 1960s. The name's persistence speaks to its phonetic simplicity and cultural neutrality — it carries no strong religious, political, or regional connotations that might limit its appeal.

**Conor** (*Conall*) traces back to the legendary High King Conall Corc of the 5th century. The name combines *con* ("wolf" or "hound") with *gal* ("courage"), yielding the meaning "wolf courage" or "great strength." The mythical Con Cennchainte, warrior of the Ulster Cycle, cemented the name's association with martial valor in Irish literary tradition.

**Declan** (*Deaglán*) is associated with Saint Declan, the apostle of the Déisi tribe and a contemporary of Saint Patrick. Etymologically, the name may derive from *deagh* ("good") plus the suffix *-lán*, or potentially from a pre-Christian root related to the sun. The name entered the Irish naming mainstream in the 1990s and has maintained steady popularity.

**Rory** (*Ruairí*) means "red king" — from *ruadh* ("red") and *rí* ("king"). The name belongs to several historical figures, most notably Rory O'Connor (Ruaidrí Ua Conchobair), the last High King of Ireland before the Norman invasion of 1169. Its Anglophone-friendly pronunciation has made it popular internationally, though it remains distinctly Irish in origin.

### Modern & Trending Irish Boy Names

**Cillian** (*Cillín*) means "church" or "little church" — from *cill* plus the diminutive *-ín*. Saint Cillian was an 7th-century Irish monk and missionary who founded monasteries in Germany and the Netherlands. The name gained international visibility through actor Cillian Murphy, but its use in Ireland predates his fame by centuries. In 2025, Cillian ranked 14th in Irish boys' names — a remarkable climb from outside the top 100 in the early 2000s.

**Finn** (*Fionn*) means "fair" or "white" — from the Celtic root *windo-*. The name is inextricably linked to Fionn mac Cumhaill, the legendary hunter and hero of the Fenian Cycle, whose wisdom was said to reside in his thumb after he touched the Salmon of Knowledge. Finn has experienced a global renaissance, ranking in the US top 50 and the UK top 30, yet it remains authentically Irish in its mythological grounding.

**Aidan** (*Aodhán*) is the diminutive of *Aodh* (see below), meaning "little fire" or "fiery one." Saint Aidan was an 6th-century Irish monk who founded the monastery on Lindisfarne, transmitting the Celtic form of Christianity to Northumbria. The name's thermal etymology — fire as inspiration, passion, divine spark — resonates across Celtic spiritual traditions.

**Orlan** (alternative *Orlanth* / *Ardán*) carries connotations of "high" or "noble," from the Celtic root *ardo-* ("eagle," "high"). The name has seen renewed interest following the 2023 release of the Irish-language film *An Cailín Ciúin*, though its usage remains modest in Ireland proper.

**Tadhg** (*Tiag* in anglicized form) means "poet" or "philosopher" — from the Old Irish *taga* ("caller," "questioner"). Tadhg is one of the most distinctly Irish names, resistant to anglicization precisely because of its orthographic specificity. The famous poet Tadhg ÓGilla na Naomh (1570–1630) composed elegies for fallen chieftains, cementing the name's literary prestige.

### Rare & Revival Irish Boy Names

**Breandan** (*Brendan*) means "prince" or "princely" — from *bre* ("chief") + *-dán* (diminutive). Saint Brendan the Navigator (c. 484–577) is the name's most famous bearer, legendary for his voyage to the "Promised Land of the Saints." The 9th-century text *Navigatio Sancti Brendani* influenced European cartographic imagination for centuries.

**Eoin** (*Eóin*) is the Irish form of John, pronounced "oh-in" — a name that deliberately resists the anglicized "John" in its Irish usage. It carries strong cultural signaling: choosing Eoin over John is often an explicit statement of linguistic and cultural identity.

**Fionán** (*Fionan*) means "little fair one" — from *fionn* plus the diminutive *-án*. Saint Fionán founded several monasteries in Ireland and Scotland in the 7th century. The name's soft phonetic quality makes it appealing to contemporary parents seeking something distinctive yet gentle.

**Gilla** (*Gilla*) as a prefix in compound names (e.g., *Gilla na Naomh*) means "servant of" — from Late Irish *gilla* ("youth," "servant"). While rarely used as a standalone given name today, it survives in surnames and ecclesiastical titles.

**Niall** (*Niall*) is one of Ireland's most historically significant names, borne by Niall of the Nine Hostages (*Niall Noígíallach*), the semi-legendary High King whose genetic legacy — the "Niall signature" Y-chromosome haplotype — is carried by an estimated 1.5 million men worldwide, per a 2007 study in the *American Journal of Human Genetics*. The name's etymology is debated: it may derive from *nial* ("champion") or from a pre-Indo-European root.

## Girl Names: Top Irish Female Picks

### Strong & Classic Irish Girl Names

**Aoife** (*Eefa*) is one of the most beautiful and challenging Irish names for non-Irish speakers. Meaning "beauty" or "radiance" — from the Old Irish *áith* ("brightness," "splendor") — Aoife was a warrior woman in the Ulster Cycle, the daughter of the druid Cathbad and the mother of Connla by the hero Cú Chulainn. The name's phonetic complexity (it is pronounced "EE-fa" or "AWE-fa" depending on regional tradition) has not deterred its popularity: it has ranked in Ireland's top 20 girls' names for over a decade.

**Saoirse** (*Sirsha*) means "freedom" or "liberty" — from the Old Irish *sáer* ("free," "noble"). The name's modern resurgence is inextricably linked to actress Saoirse Ronan, but its cultural significance runs deeper. During the Irish language revival of the 1960s–70s, Saoirse became a conscious political choice — a name that declared linguistic and cultural independence. In 2017, it was the most popular girls' name in Ireland, with 484 newborns bearing it.

**Niamh** (*Neev*) means "bright" or "radiant" — from the Old Irish *niám* ("radiance," "glow"). In myth, Niamh of the Golden Hair (*Niamh Chín ón Cháir*) was a goddess of the Otherworld who carried Oisín on a journey to Tír na nÓg ("Land of the Young"). The name's single-syllable anglicized pronunciation belies its rich mythological pedigree.

**Ciara** (*Keera*) means "dark-haired" — from *ciar* ("dark," "black") plus the feminine suffix *-a*. The name has enjoyed sustained popularity since the 1990s and remains a staple of Irish girls' names. Saint Ciara (also *Sacara*) was an 8th-century abbess of Segga Abbey in County Wexford.

**Maeve** (*Meave*) means "she who intoxicates" — from the Old Irish *Medb* ("joy," "pleasure"). Queen Medb of Connacht is one of the most formidable figures in Irish mythology: a warrior queen who led the Táin Bó Cúailnge (the Cattle Raid of Cooley). The name's brevity and strength make it internationally accessible while remaining authentically Irish.

**Róisín** (*Ro-sheen*) means "little rose" — from *róis* ("rose") plus the diminutive *-ín*. The name gained poetic immortality through the 19th-century ballad *Róisín Dhubh* ("Dark-Rosaleen") by Thomas Moore, though the song itself may reference a historical figure — possibly the poet's patroness, Rose Dillon.

### Modern & Trending Irish Girl Names

**Orla** (*Orla*) means "golden princess" — from *ór* ("gold") and *bflann* ("princess," "radiance"). The name has climbed steadily in Irish popularity since the 1990s and represents a modern revival of Old Irish compound names. Its bright phonetic quality — two syllables, open vowels, liquid consonants — makes it particularly appealing across linguistic boundaries.

**Aisling** (*Ash-lin*) means "dream" or "vision" — from the Old Irish *aisling* ("dream," "vision," "revelation"). The Aisling genre, a poetic tradition of dream-vision literature popular in the 17th–18th centuries, typically featured a *bean úll* ("fair lady") representing the personification of Ireland itself. The name carries profound literary and political resonance.

**Sinéad** (*Shin-aid*) is the Irish form of Joan, from Hebrew *Yochanan* ("God is gracious"). The anglicized spelling "Sinéad" deliberately preserves the Irish orthographic identity, distinguishing it from the English "Joan." The famous singer Sinéad O'Connor (1966–2023) brought international attention to the name's distinctive spelling.

**Eabha** (*Ev-a*) is the Irish form of Eve, from Hebrew *Chavvah* ("life," "living"). Notably, Eabha represents a return to the Irish language's native rendering rather than an anglicized adaptation — a subtle but significant onomastic choice that signals linguistic commitment.

**Caoimhe** (*Kwee-va* or *Keve*) means "beautiful," "gentle," or "precious" — from the Old Irish *cáo* ("beautiful") and *-mhe* (feminine suffix). The spelling is notoriously challenging for non-Irish speakers, which has contributed to both its exclusivity and its appeal among parents seeking a distinctly Irish name.

### Rare & Revival Irish Girl Names

**Bridget** (*Bríd*) means "exalted one" or "strength" — from the Celtic goddess *Brighid*, whose name derives from the root *brigh* ("height," "excellence," "power"). Saint Bríd of Kildare (c. 451–525) syncretized the pagan goddess's attributes, making Bríd one of Ireland's most venerated saints. The name's dual sacred and mythological heritage gives it extraordinary depth.

**Dubhchobhlaigh** (*Div-la*) means "dark one" — from *dubh* ("black," "dark") and *cobhlaigh* (a now-obsolete element). This is an authentically ancient Irish name, borne by several medieval Irish princesses and queens. Its rarity in contemporary usage makes it a bold choice for parents seeking historical authenticity.

**Eibhlin** (*Ev-lin*) is the Irish form of Evelyn, though some onomastic scholars argue it derives from the Old Irish *ebhl* ("yew tree"). The yew, a symbol of immortality in Celtic tradition, adds a layer of botanical and symbolic meaning.

**Finnuala** (*Fin-oo-la*) means "fair-shouldered" — from *fionn* ("fair," "white") and *dhealadh* ("shoulder"). In mythology, Finnuala was the sister of the Children of Lir, transformed into swans by a jealous stepmother. The name's tragic beauty and lyrical sound make it compelling despite its rarity.

**Grainne** (*Grane*) means "grain" or "corn" — from the Old Irish *grán* ("grain," "seed"). In the *Táin Bó Flidhais*, Grainne is the wife of Fionn mac Cumhaill who falls in love with Diarmuid. The name's agricultural etymology connects it to the ancient Celtic cult of the earth goddess.

**Aisling** works for any gender in contemporary Irish usage, though traditionally feminine. **Brendan** has been used for girls in some rural communities, though this is rare. **Finn** is increasingly used for girls across the English-speaking world, though in Ireland it remains predominantly male.

**Caoimhin** (*Kwee-vin*) is technically masculine but has appeared in recent years as a gender-neutral option among Irish-language-speaking families. **Solas** (*Sol-as*) means "light" and is used for all genders in modern Irish naming practice, though it remains uncommon.

## Rare & Revival Irish Names

### Forgotten Gems from the Annals

**Aonghus** (*Oongus*) means "one choice" or "unique strength" — from *aon* ("one") and *óg* ("youth," "strength"). Aonghus mac óg was a deity in the Tuatha Dé Danann, associated with love, youth, and poetic inspiration. The name's mythological pedigree and distinctive phonetic profile make it a powerful revival candidate.

**Breac** (*Breck*) means "speckled" or "variegated" — from the Old Irish *brecc*. It was a common epithet in early Irish literature (e.g., *Brecc Senchus*, the "Speckled Book of Ballymote") and has appeared as a given name in medieval records.

**Caitlin** (*Catleen*) is the Irish form of Catherine, from Greek *Aikaterine*. While not rare in Ireland, its Irish spelling and pronunciation (*Cat-leen*) distinguish it from the anglicized form and preserve linguistic heritage.

**Dara** (*Dara*) means "oak" or "firm" — from the Old Irish *darach* ("oak"). The oak was sacred to the Celtic peoples, associated with strength, endurance, and druidic wisdom. The name is genuinely unisex in Irish usage.

**Eala** (*Ay-la*) means "swan" — from the Old Irish *éile* ("swan"). In Celtic mythology, swans were sacred animals associated with the Otherworld and transformation. The name's ethereal quality and mythological resonance make it a compelling choice.

**Feidhlim** (*Fee-lim*) means "joyful" or "victorious" — from *fíor* ("true," "genuine") and *gal* ("courage"). Feidhlim Reachtmar was a legendary High King of Ireland. The name's vigorous phonetics and royal associations give it considerable gravitas.

**Gormlaith** (*Gor-lith*) means "blue/violet pride" — from *gorm* ("blue," "green," "violet") and *flath* ("princess," "lord," "pride"). Gormlaith was a common name among medieval Irish royalty; several High Kings' queens bore it.

**Iobarraig** (*I-va-rayg*) is a highly unusual name meaning "ocean wave" — from *iubhar* ("yew") and *braig* ("wave"). While extremely rare, it demonstrates the creative potential of Irish compound name formation.

**Lochlann** (*Lock-lan*) means "land of the lakes" — from *loch* ("lake") and *-lán* (suffix denoting land). The name refers to Scandinavia (Lochlann) in Irish medieval literature and connects Ireland's Viking history to its onomastic tradition.

**Muircheartach** (*Mook-hart-ach*) means "sea navigator" or "sea-worthy" — from *muir* ("sea") and *cartach* ("charioteer," "navigator"). Muircheartach Muilleathan was a 5th-century King of Munster. The name's maritime etymology reflects Ireland's seafaring heritage.

## Meaning & Etymology

### The Architecture of Irish Names

Irish names operate within a morphological system that is both highly productive and deeply historical. Understanding their structure reveals why certain names persist while others fade, and why some names feel instantly "Irish" to English speakers while others resist categorization.

**Prefix-Suffix Systems:** Irish names frequently combine roots with productive suffixes. The diminutive *-óg* (feminine) and *-ín* (masculine/neuter) are the most common: *Mairéad* → *Mairéóg* ("little Mary"), *Seán* → *Seánóg* (a now-rare feminine form), *Brian* → *Briain* (not a diminutive but a genitive form that became a name). The suffix *-ach* often denotes belonging or association: *Muircheartach* ("of the sea"), *Breacach* ("speckled one").

**Compound Names:** Ancient Irish names frequently combined two meaningful elements. *Aonghus* = *aon* ("one") + *óg* ("youth"). *Feidhlim* = *fíor* ("true") + *gal* ("courage"). *Muircheartach* = *muir* ("sea") + *cartach* ("navigator"). These compounds function as semantic units — the meaning is not merely additive but generative, creating concepts that neither element expresses alone.

**Patronymic & Matronymic Systems:** The genitive case in Irish creates patronymics: *Mac* ("son of") + genitive = *Mac Siobháin* ("son of John"), *Mac Conchobhair* ("son of Conor"). *Bean* ("woman") + *í* (feminine genitive marker) = *Ní* = "daughter of": *Ní Bhriain* ("daughter of Brian"). These systems mean that Irish surnames are themselves onomastic fossils — they preserve the given names of ancestors.

**Lenition & Eclipsis:** The orthographic markers of Irish grammar are also markers of name identity. *Seán* becomes *Shéanáid* in the vocative; *Brian* becomes *Bhríain* in the genitive. When anglicized, these grammatical markers are lost, but the resulting names (*Sean*, *Brian*) retain a phonetic fingerprint of the original Irish forms.

### Etymological Deep Dives

**Liam:** The transformation from *Uilliam* to *Liam* represents a remarkable phonological shortening. The initial *U-* was dropped (a common phenomenon in Irish phonology, compare *Uí Bhriain* → *O'Brien*), and the *-ill-* cluster simplified to *-l-*. The result is a name that is shorter, sharper, and more internationally accessible than its Germanic source.

**Saoirse:** The preservation of the *-rs-* cluster and the final *-e* (silent in Irish orthography but crucial for the name's visual identity) makes Saoirse a name that resists anglicization. Its spelling is a deliberate act of orthographic resistance — to write it as "Sersha" or "Sirsha" would be to erase its Irish identity.

**Aoife:** The diphthong *oi* in Irish is pronounced approximately as "ee" (as in English "see"), while the *f* before *e* is pronounced "v." The result — "EE-fa" — is phonetically transparent to Irish speakers but opaque to English speakers. The name's resistance to easy anglicization has contributed to its perception as "exotic" or "distinctive" in English-speaking contexts.

**Niamh:** The *mh* cluster produces a "v" sound, and the final *-h* is silent. "NEEV" or "NEE-av" depending on regional pronunciation. The name's orthographic complexity masks a phonetically simple structure — a pattern common in Irish naming.

**Ciara:** The *Ci-* cluster is pronounced "KEE" (not "SEE" as English speakers might assume), and the *-ara* ending is two syllables: "KEE-rah." The name's etymology (*ciar* = "dark") connects it to a broader Celtic tradition of nature-based naming.

## Origin & Cultural Context

### From Gaelic Traditions to Colonial Erasure

The onomastic history of Ireland is a history of resistance and adaptation. Native Irish names — *Aodh*, *Bríd*, *Ciarán*, *Muirgen* — formed a continuous tradition from the early medieval period through the Gaelic lordships of the 15th and 16th centuries. These names were embedded in a cultural system that valued genealogical knowledge, poetic composition, and mythological allusion.

The English conquest of Ireland, beginning in earnest in the 12th century and accelerating through the 16th and 17th centuries, systematically suppressed Irish language and culture. The Statutes of Kilkenny (1366) attempted to prevent English settlers from adopting Irish customs, including names — a legislation that reveals how deeply names were entwined with cultural identity. The Penal Laws (1695–1829) further restricted Irish-language education, effectively severing the transmission of native onomastic traditions.

### The Great Anglicization

Between 1800 and 1900, the majority of Irish names underwent anglicization. *Seán* became *John*; *Mairéad* became *Mary*; *Pádraig* became *Patrick*; *Teresa* replaced *Téasa*. This process was not always imposed from above — many Irish families voluntarily anglicized their names to improve economic prospects, particularly during and after the Great Famine (1845–1852). The resulting name pool in Ireland became a hybrid: a mix of anglicized Irish names, English names adopted by Irish families, and a diminishing core of unanglicized Irish names.

### The Gaelic Revival

The Irish Literary Revival (c. 1890–1920) and the subsequent Irish Language Revival (1930s–present) sparked a conscious reclamation of Irish names. The establishment of Irish-language schools (*Gaelscoileanna*) in the 20th century created new generations of speakers who naturally adopted Irish names. The Celtic Tiger economic boom (1990s–2000s) intensified cultural confidence, leading to a surge in Irish name usage among non-Gaeltacht families.

### Regional Variations

Irish naming patterns vary significantly by region. In the Gaeltacht (Irish-speaking regions) of Connemara, Donegal, and the Dingle Peninsula, traditional Irish names remain far more common than in urban centers. In Dublin, anglicized names dominate, though the gap has narrowed considerably since the 1990s. The west of Ireland retains stronger naming traditions tied to parish patron saints and local genealogical knowledge.

### Mastering Irish Phonetics

Irish orthography follows a principle known as *caol le caol leathan le leathan* ("slender with slender, broad with broad") — vowels on either side of a consonant determine its pronunciation. This system creates phonetic patterns that are internally consistent but opaque to English speakers.

### Regional Pronunciation Variations

The pronunciation of Irish names varies across dialects. *Seán* may be pronounced "SHAWN" (Munster), "SHAN" (Connacht), or closer to "SHAYN" (Ulster). *Pádraig* may be "PAD-rik" (most common) or "PAR-rik" (regional). *Caitríona* may be "CAT-ree-na" (Munster) or "CAT-rye-na" (Connacht). These variations are not errors — they are living dialectal differences that reflect Ireland's linguistic diversity.

Irish orthography offers unique nickname opportunities. The *-óg* and *-ín* suffixes themselves function as affectionate forms: *Mairéad* → *Mairéóg* → *Réóg*. ## FAQ

### Q: What is the most popular Irish baby name in 2025?
A: As of 2025 data from Ireland's Central Statistics Office, Liam remains the most popular boys' name, while Mia leads for girls. Among distinctly Irish-language names, Aoife and Niamh are the most popular girls' names, and Conor and Sean lead the boys' category.

### Q: How do you pronounce the Irish name "Aoife"?
A: Aoife is pronounced "EE-fa" (rhyming with "sea-fa"). The *oi* digraph in Irish represents the long "ee" sound, and the *f* before *e* is pronounced as a "v" sound. Some regional variations may render it closer to "AWE-fa," but "EE-fa" is the standard pronunciation.

### Q: Are Irish names hard to spell for non-Irish speakers?
A: Some Irish names present significant spelling challenges. Names like Caoimhe, Siobhán, and Eibhlin contain orthographic conventions that do not map intuitively onto English spelling rules. However, many Irish names — Liam, Finn, Rory, Maeve — are straightforward to spell and pronounce in English. The challenge varies by name.

### Q: What does the Irish name "Saoirse" mean?
A: Saoirse means "freedom" or "liberty" in Irish. It derives from the Old Irish word *sáer* meaning "free" or "noble." The name gained enormous popularity in the 2010s, partly due to actress Saoirse Ronan, but its cultural significance predates her fame by centuries.

### Q: Can Irish names be used outside of Ireland?
A: Absolutely. Irish names are used worldwide, particularly in countries with significant Irish diaspora populations (United States, Canada, Australia, United Kingdom, Argentina). Some names like Liam, Finn, and Rory have achieved global popularity. Others like Caoimhe and Siobhán remain more culturally specific but are increasingly recognized internationally.

### Q: What is the difference between "Sean" and "Seán"?
A: "Seán" is the Irish-language spelling, pronounced "SHAWN." "Sean" is the anglicized spelling, which can be pronounced "SHAWN" or "SEE-awn" depending on regional and familial preference. The fada (acute accent) on the *á* in "Seán" indicates a long vowel sound and is an essential part of the Irish orthographic system.

### Q: Are there any Irish names that work well for both boys and girls?
A: Yes. Finn, Dara, Aisling (traditionally feminine but increasingly unisex), and Róisín (traditionally feminine) are used across genders in contemporary Ireland. Finn is particularly notable — it is overwhelmingly masculine in Ireland but increasingly used for girls in the United States and other English-speaking countries.

### Q: What are the most historically significant Irish names?
A: Names with the deepest historical roots include Niall (borne by the legendary High King Niall of the Nine Hostages), Brigid (Saint Brigid of Kildare, 5th century), Cormac (King Cormac Mac Airt, 3rd century), and Deirdre (tragic heroine of the Ulster Cycle). These names appear in the earliest Irish literary records and carry millennia of cultural association.

### Q: How did the Great Famine affect Irish naming patterns?
A: The Great Famine (1845–1852) caused massive population loss and emigration, which disrupted traditional naming patterns. Many Irish families anglicized their names during and after the famine to improve prospects in destination countries. The famine also accelerated the decline of the Irish language, which carried with it the transmission of native onomastic traditions.

### Q: What is the Irish word for "name"?
A: The Irish word for "name" is *ainm* (pronounced "AN-im"). The phrase "What is your name?" is *Cad é do ainm?* (pronounced "Kad ay dow an-im?"). Irish names (*ainmneacha*) are deeply embedded in a grammatical system that marks possession, case, and relationship — a feature that distinguishes Irish onomastics from many other naming traditions.

### Q: How common are Irish names in the United States?
A: Irish names are among the most common ethnic names in the United States, owing to centuries of Irish immigration. Liam has been the #1 boys' name in the US since 2017. Other Irish names with significant US usage include Connor, Dylan, Owen, Ryan, Sean, Patrick, and Erin. The 2020 US Census estimated that approximately 38 million Americans claim Irish ancestry.

### Q: Should I choose an anglicized or original Irish spelling?
A: This is a personal decision with practical considerations. Original Irish spellings (Siobhán, Caoimhe, Niamh) preserve cultural authenticity but may require frequent correction in English-speaking contexts. Anglicized spellings (Shawn, Kevin, Neve) are more accessible but may feel like a compromise to some families. Many parents choose a middle ground — using the Irish spelling as the official name while preparing for practical adjustments in school and professional settings.

## Conclusion

Names are never merely labels. An Irish name carries the weight of a language that survived colonization, the memory of a people who preserved their history through oral tradition, and the quiet defiance of families who chose to keep their children's names in Gaeilge when every institutional pressure pointed toward anglicization. When you select an Irish name — whether Liam, Aoife, Saoirse, or a name drawn from the annals of medieval Ireland — you are participating in a continuous act of cultural preservation that stretches back over a millennium.

The most powerful insight from studying Irish onomastics is this: names are living things. They mutate, adapt, resist, and survive. The Irish language, spoken by fewer than 75,000 daily native speakers, nonetheless produces names that resonate across continents and centuries. Your child's name will be shaped by their environment, their choices, and the world they inherit — but it will also carry, in its phonetics and etymology, the echo of a tradition that refused to be erased.
