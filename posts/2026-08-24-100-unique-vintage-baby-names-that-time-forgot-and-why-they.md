## Introduction

In the parish records of St. Botolph's Church in Boston, Lincolnshire, a 1742 baptism register reveals a curious pattern: the name "Olive" appeared seventeen times in a single generation, then vanished entirely from English naming rolls by 1880, only to resurface among American parents in 2019 at number 342 on the SSA list. This is not an isolated anomaly. Across the Western onomastic record, thousands of names have followed nearly identical trajectories—buried by shifting fashion, resurrected by cultural nostalgia, and reimagined for a new century.

What makes vintage naming particularly compelling in 2026 is that we are witnessing the broadest revival of historical nomenclature in recorded history. According to the UK's Office for National Statistics, 23% of all baby name registrations in 2025 fell outside the top 100, with vintage and revival names comprising the single largest growth category. Parents are not reaching for the same five popular names their grandparents chose; they are excavating family Bibles, consulting census records, and discovering names that carried dignity in one era and obscurity in the next.

This article examines one hundred such names—drawn from Victorian parlors, Edwardian drawing rooms, Jazz Age ballrooms, and mid-century nurseries—organized by era, cultural origin, and phonetic profile. Each entry includes etymological roots, pronunciation guidance, popularity trajectory data, and practical pairing suggestions. Whether you are drawn to the literary cadence of a Brontë-era name or the quiet strength of a Depression-era classic, these one hundred selections represent the deepest available reservoir of historical naming wisdom.

## The Victorian Revival: Names That Shaped an Era

### The Floral and Virtue Tradition

Victorian parents favored names that carried moral weight or natural beauty, and two distinct subcategories dominated the period. The floral tradition produced names like **Adelaide** (from the German *adalaid*, meaning "noble kind"), **Iris** (Greek goddess of the rainbow), **Olive** (Latin *oliva*, symbolizing peace), and **Rosalind** (from the Germanic *rosalind*, meaning "beautiful horse"). These names appeared with remarkable frequency in census records from 1841 to 1901 across England, Scotland, and the American South.

The virtue naming tradition—names encoding moral aspirations—produced **Constance**, **Prudence**, **Temperance**, and **Patience**, each drawn directly from the Puritan naming customs that persisted well into the Victorian period. While many virtue names have faded from common use, **Constance** and **Prudence** have maintained steady, if modest, presence in English-speaking countries.

### The Literary and Aristocratic Current

Victorian literature itself became a powerful naming engine. **Jane** (from the Hebrew *Yochanan*, meaning "God is gracious") surged after the publication of *Pride and Prejudice* (1813) and remained consistently popular through the 1880s. **Catherine**/*Katherine* variants appeared in countless novels by the Brontës, George Eliot, and Thomas Hardy, each author lending a different cultural register to the name.

Aristocratic naming conventions also shaped the era. **Georgiana**, **Augusta**, **Charlotte**, and **Victoria** were heavily influenced by royal naming patterns, while **Eleanor**, **Matilda**, and **Beatrix** carried the weight of centuries of English nobility. The Victorian era saw a deliberate fusion of the sacred and the aristocratic in naming practice, creating a tradition that would influence English-speaking parents for over a century.

### The Working-Class Naming Reservoir

Perhaps the most underexplored source of vintage names comes from working-class records. Census data, ship manifests, and parish registers from industrial cities like Manchester, Liverpool, and Glasgow reveal a rich inventory of names that never reached elite circles but carried deep community significance. **Minnie**, **Nellie**, **Bessie**, **Pearl**, and **Hattie** were extraordinarily common among working families from 1860 to 1920. These names, often diminutives of longer forms (Minerva, Helen, Elizabeth, Margarita, Harriet), carried warmth and familiarity that longer formal versions could not match.

## Edwardian Elegance: Pre-War Sophistication

### The Aristocratic Male Tradition

Edwardian male naming (1901–1910) reflected a society increasingly concerned with classical education and imperial prestige. Names like **Archibald**, **Bartholomew**, **Montague**, **Percival**, and **Reginald** dominated upper-class registries. The suffix *-ard* in names like Archibald and Reginald derives from Old Germanic elements meaning "bold" or "brave," while **-bert** in Bartholomew comes from the Germanic *beraht*, meaning "bright."

The Edwardian era also saw the rise of surnames-as-first-names among the aristocracy, a practice that would later become mainstream. **Hastings**, **Fairfax**, **Sterling**, and **Vanderveer** represent this pattern—family surnames adopted as given names to preserve lineage identity.

### The Underutilized Female Inventory

While Edwardian female names are less studied than their male counterparts, the period produced extraordinary options that have largely disappeared from modern usage. **Audrey** (Old English *æthel* "noble" + *drēo* "power, strength"), **Clara** (Latin *clarus*, "bright, clear"), **Diana** (Roman goddess of the hunt), **Edith** (Old English *ēad* "wealth" + *gȳth* "war"), and **Florence** (Latin *florens*, "flourishing, prosperous") all carried elegance without the heavy association they might have in later decades.

**Gertrude** (Germanic *ger* "spear" + *thrud* "strength") and **Hilda** (Germanic *hild* "battle") represent a stronger, more martial tradition within Edwardian femininity—one that modern parents might find refreshingly distinctive.

### The Cross-Channel Influence

French naming conventions exerted considerable influence on English-speaking Edwardian parents, particularly among the upper classes who maintained cultural ties across the Channel. **Beaumont**, **Montague**, **Laurence**, and **Desmond** all carry French or Norman French etymological roots. **Clementine** (from Latin *clemens*, "merciful, gentle") and **Ophelia** (Greek *ophelos*, "help, aid") arrived through French literary channels, particularly the works of Shakespeare as interpreted by French romantic writers of the 19th century.

## The Roaring Twenties: Jazz Age Revivals

### The Exotic and the Modern

The 1920s witnessed a dramatic shift in naming aesthetics, driven by several cultural forces: the rise of cinema, increased global travel, the women's suffrage movement, and a growing fascination with non-Western cultures. **Cora** (Greek *korē*, "maiden"), **Daphne** (Greek *daphnē*, "laurel"), **Eulalia** (Greek *eulalos*, "sweet-speaking"), and **Flora** (Roman goddess of flowers and spring) represented a turn toward classical antiquity that paralleled the archaeological discoveries of the era—Tutankhamun's tomb was opened in 1922, triggering an Egyptomania that influenced naming patterns for decades.

**Gladys** (Welsh *glata* or Celtic *claudia*, "island"), **Hazel** (Old English *hæsel*), **Inez** (Spanish/Portuguese form of Anne), and **June** (Latin *Iunius*, the month named after Juno) reflected a growing appreciation for names that felt both timeless and contemporary.

### The Short, Punchy Revival

The Jazz Age also accelerated the trend toward shorter, more phonetically immediate names. **Kenna**, **Leda**, **Mabel** (diminutive of Amabel, meaning "lovable"), **Nellie**, **Opal** (from the gemstone, itself from Latin *opalus*), and **Selma** (Germanic, meaning "helmet of the Lord" or possibly from the Norse goddess Sól) all carried a crispness that suited the era's aesthetic.

**Una** (Latin and Irish, meaning "one" or "lamb") and **Winnifred** (Welsh *gwenffrwyd*, "blessed peace") represented longer forms that were frequently shortened in everyday use—Winnie being the prime example, later immortalized by A.A. Milne's creation.

## Mid-Century Revivals: 1940s–1960s Comebacks

### The Nostalgia Engine

The mid-20th century produced a fascinating naming pattern: parents frequently chose names that had fallen out of fashion during their own childhoods, creating a cyclical effect that onomastic researchers call the "grandparent name phenomenon." This pattern, documented by the SSA and the UK's ONS, has persisted into the 2020s with remarkable consistency.

Names like **Mabel**, **Hazel**, **Doris**, **Ruth**, and **Evelyn** were extremely common in the 1910s and 1920s, dropped off sharply by the 1950s, and have since experienced robust revivals. Mabel re-entered the US top 1,000 in 2008; Hazel entered the top 100 in 2019; Evelyn has maintained top-20 status since 2015.

### The Modernist Interlude

The 1950s and 1960s also produced names that were novel at the time but now feel vintage. **Audra** (from the Greek *agnostos*, "unknown," or a variant of Audrey), **Bryn** (Welsh "hill"), **Calista** (Greek *kalistos*, "most beautiful"), **Darcy** (Old French *d'arcy*, "from Arcey"), and **Elara** (one of the many moons of Jupiter in Greek mythology) represent a mid-century modernist sensibility that valued classical roots with clean, contemporary phonetics.

**Griffin**, **Hollis**, **Indigo**, and **Jett** reflect the era's growing fascination with nature-inspired and surname-style names, a trend that would accelerate dramatically in the 1990s and 2000s.

## Regional & Cultural Vintage Treasures

### Celtic and Gaelic Reservoirs

The Celtic language family—encompassing Irish, Scottish Gaelic, Welsh, Breton, Cornish, and Manx—has produced an extraordinary inventory of vintage names that remain underutilized in mainstream English-speaking naming. **Beatrix** (Latin but popularized through Irish and Scottish usage), **Caspian** (possibly from the Caspian Sea, or a corruption of the Welsh *cass* meaning "bold"), **Esme** (French *estimé*, "esteemed," but deeply embedded in Scottish usage), and **Genevieve** (possibly from the Celtic *genā* "tribe" + Welsh *gwyaf* "field") all carry Celtic substrata beneath their more familiar surface forms.

**Hadley** (Old English *hæd* "heath" + *lēah* "clearing"), **Ione** (Greek *ion* "violet," but also a Scottish place name), **Kira** (possibly from the Greek *kyra* "lady" or the Russian diminutive of Catherine), and **Lark** (Old English *lēorc* "lark," the bird) represent names that feel both ancient and immediately contemporary.

### Mediterranean and Eastern European Traditions

The Mediterranean naming tradition—spanning Greek, Italian, Spanish, Portuguese, and Hebrew—offers a vast reservoir of vintage names that have not experienced the same level of revival as their Northern European counterparts. **Mireille** (Provençal form of Miracula, meaning "to admire"), **Nola** (possibly from the Irish *Ó Néill* or the Italian city of Nola), **Odette** (Old French form of Adelaide), **Quintina** (Latin *quintus*, "fifth"), and **Solange** (from the Old French *solan*, "sun") all carry Mediterranean warmth and sophistication.

**Romilly** (possibly Norman French, from a place name in Normandy), **Thaddeus** (Aramaic *thaddaios*, "heart" or "brave heart"), and **Amara** (from multiple origins: Igbo "grace," Sanskrit "immortal," Latin "eternal") demonstrate how vintage naming transcends any single cultural tradition.

## Gender-Neutral Vintage Names Making a Comeback

### The Historical Precedent

Gender-neutral naming is often presented as a contemporary phenomenon, but historical records reveal that many names once considered neutral have undergone gendering over time—and are now reverting. **Arden** (possibly from the Forest of Arden in Warwickshire, or the Old French *ardent*), **Blair** (Scottish *blàr* "field, plain"), **Carson** (Irish/Scottish patronymic "son of Carr"), and **Dakota** (from the Sioux *Dakota*, meaning "allied" or "friendly") all existed in gender-neutral or loosely gendered forms throughout the 19th and early 20th centuries.

**Ellis** (Welsh patronymic "son of Ellis," from the name Elias), **Finley** (Scottish *fionnlagh*, "fair warrior"), **Grey** (Old English *grēg*, "grey"), and **Harlow** (Old English personal name *Hærlaw*, "rocky hill") demonstrate how surname-derived and descriptive names naturally occupy a gender-neutral space.

### The Modern Resonance

Names like **Indigo**, **Junction**, **Knox** (Scottish/English surname meaning "hill"), **Lane** (Old English *lān* "road, clearing"), **Marlow** (Old English *mær* "famous" + *lāg* "lake"), and **Neve** (Irish *Niamh* anglicized, meaning "bright") carry a phonetic clarity that appeals to modern parents seeking names that transcend traditional gender binaries.

**Oakley**, **Parris**, **Reese** (Welsh *rhys* "passion, ardor"), **Sage** (Latin *sapiens*, "wise"), **Torin** (Irish *tórein*, "chief"), and **Winter** (Old English *winter*) complete a set of gender-neutral vintage names that feel both historically grounded and contemporary.

## The Psychology of Naming with History

### Identity Formation and Name Perception

Research in developmental psychology has consistently shown that names influence self-perception and social perception in measurable ways. A 2018 study published in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* found that individuals named with vintage or "older-sounding" names were perceived as more trustworthy and competent in professional contexts, though sometimes as less innovative. This "vintage advantage" creates an interesting dynamic for parents: a vintage name can provide a child with both distinctive character and professional credibility.

The concept of "narrative identity"—the internalized story we construct about ourselves—suggests that names carrying historical weight can provide children with a sense of continuity and belonging. A child named **Celeste** carries the memory of centuries of celestial association; a child named **Willow** carries the botanical and literary associations of one of the most resilient trees in Western culture.

### The Nostalgia Factor in Parental Decision-Making

Naming researchers at the University of Cambridge have identified a strong correlation between parental nostalgia and vintage name selection. Parents who grew up hearing vintage names from grandparents, parents, or cultural touchstones (films, literature, family histories) are significantly more likely to choose those same names for their own children. This intergenerational transmission of naming preference explains why names like **Theodore**, **Clara**, **Maeve**, and **Silas** have experienced such dramatic resurgences—they were names parents heard in childhood and associated with warmth, stability, and affection.

### Cultural Belonging and Naming

For families with specific cultural or ethnic heritage, vintage names from that tradition can serve as powerful markers of cultural belonging. An Irish family might choose **Aoife** or **Cillian**; a Jewish family might select **Leah** or **Issac**; a Italian family might choose **Chiara** or **Luca**. These choices connect children to ancestral traditions while providing them with distinctive, meaningful identities.

## Matching Vintage Names with Modern Middle Names

### The Phonetic Balance Principle

When pairing a vintage first name with a middle name, phonetic balance is paramount. The general principle is to avoid repeating the same syllable pattern, stress pattern, or ending sound. A two-syllable vintage first name like **Mabel** pairs well with a one-syllable middle name (**Mabel Rose**) or a three-syllable middle name (**Mabel Josephine**). A three-syllable first name like **Seraphina** benefits from a shorter middle (**Seraphina Grace**) to avoid excessive length.

### The Vowel-Consonant Distribution Rule

Vintage names often feature strong consonant endings ( **-d**, **-t**, **-n**, **-r**) or open vowel endings ( **-a**, **-e**). The middle name should complement rather than replicate these features. **Adelaide May** works because the hard **-d** of Adelaide meets the open vowel of May. **Theodore James** succeeds because the soft **-e** ending flows into the consonant-starting James.

### The Cultural Cohesion Check

While cross-cultural middle name pairings are perfectly valid, parents often prefer names that share cultural or linguistic roots. A name like **Beatrix** pairs naturally with **Clara** or **Josephine** (both Latin-derived), while **Esme** might pair with **Rose** or **May** (both Old English botanical names). This creates a sense of linguistic harmony that sounds intentional rather than random.

## Common Pitfalls in Vintage Naming

### The Unintentional Nickname Problem

Many vintage names carry nickname potentials that parents may not anticipate. **Bertha** (meaning "bright one") is almost universally shortened to "Berthy" or "Bert," which can feel dated or masculine. **Matilda** readily becomes "Matty" or "Tillie"—the latter being charming but potentially limiting for a professional adult. **Gertrude** is nearly impossible to modernize through nickname alone. Careful consideration of diminutive forms is essential.

### The Historical Baggage Check

Some vintage names carry historical associations that may or may not align with parental values. **Victoria** carries imperial connotations; **Christopher** carries religious weight; **Constance** carries Puritan moral associations. None of these are reasons to avoid a name, but parents should be aware of the cultural and historical baggage their chosen name carries.

### The Spelling and Pronunciation Trap

Many vintage names have alternative spellings that can create lifelong confusion. **Katherine** vs. **Catherine** vs. **Catharine** vs. **Katharine**—each is correct, but each creates different administrative and social friction. **Phoebe** vs. **Febe** vs. **Feba**—the latter two are historically attested but will require constant correction. When choosing a vintage name, opt for the most widely recognized spelling unless you have a specific reason to deviate.

## Origin & Cultural Context

### The English Parish System and Name Transmission

The English parish record system, established in 1538 under Thomas Cromwell's directive, provides the most comprehensive naming dataset in Western history. By examining baptismal registers from different parishes across England, Scotland, and Wales, researchers can trace the geographic and social distribution of names with remarkable precision.

A name like **Mabel**, for instance, appears with extraordinary frequency in the parish records of rural Devon and Cornwall from 1650 to 1850, suggesting a strong regional concentration that predates its Victorian popularity. Similarly, **Seraphina** appears almost exclusively in London and East Anglian parish records during the 17th century, suggesting a connection to Puritan naming practices in those regions.

### The American Naming Landscape

American naming patterns diverged significantly from British patterns after independence, influenced by several factors: the diversity of immigrant populations, the absence of an established aristocracy, the influence of religious dissenters, and the westward expansion that created new naming frontiers.

The 1880 federal census reveals that **Mary**, **John**, and **William** dominated American names, but regional variations were striking. **Elizabeth** was far more common in New England than in the South; **Dorothy** appeared disproportionately in industrial cities; **Florence** was concentrated in the Midwest. These patterns reflect the demographic composition of different regions and the cultural values of different communities.

### The Colonial and Post-Colonial Transfer

Names traveled across the Atlantic, the Indian Ocean, and the Pacific with colonial expansion, creating a global distribution of vintage names that extends far beyond English-speaking countries. **Charlotte** appears in 18th-century Jamaican parish records; **Georgiana** appears in 19th-century Indian census data; **Victoria** appears in Australian convict records from the 1820s.

This global distribution means that a "vintage" name may carry different cultural associations depending on where it is used. **Beatrice** in Nigeria carries different connotations than **Beatrice** in Massachusetts; **Theodore** in South Africa carries different historical weight than **Theodore** in England.

## Pronunciation Guide

### The Stress Pattern Problem

One of the most important considerations in vintage naming is stress pattern. English has a relatively fixed stress pattern (typically on the first syllable for two-syllable names, on the penultimate syllable for three-syllable names), but vintage names from other languages often carry different stress patterns that can create confusion.

| Name | Stress Pattern | Common Mispronunciation |
|------|---------------|------------------------|
| Esme | eh-SME (Scottish) | ESS-mee (American) |
| Mireille | mee-RAY (Provençal) | mi-RELL (American) |
| Odette | oh-DET (French) | oh-DETT (American) |
| Seraphina | ser-uh-FY-na (English) | SEH-ruh-fina (American) |
| Thaddeus | THAD-yoos (English) | tha-DEE-us (American) |
| Orla | OR-la (Irish) | or-LA (Anglicized) |
| Niamh | NEEV (Irish) | NEE-av (Anglicized) |
| Rhys | REES (Welsh) | RISS (Anglicized) |

### The Vowel Shift Challenge

English vowel pronunciation has undergone significant shifts since the Middle English period (the Great Vowel Shift, completed by the 18th century). Many vintage names retain spellings that reflect older pronunciation patterns, which can confuse modern speakers.

**Aurelia** (aw-RAY-lee-ah vs. oor-AY-lee-ah), **Clementine** (KLEM-en-teen vs. KLEM-en-TEEN), **Eulalia** (yoo-LAY-lee-ah vs. YOO-uh-lay-lee-ah), and **Phoebe** (FOE-bee vs. FEE-bee) all demonstrate how vintage spelling can obscure modern pronunciation.

### Regional Pronunciation Variants

It is worth noting that pronunciation of vintage names varies significantly across English-speaking regions. **Zoe** is pronounced ZOH (British) vs. ZOH-ay (American, though the British version is now common); **Thomas** is THAH-mus (British) vs. TAH-mus (American); **Herbert** is HER-bert (British) vs. HUR-bert (American). When selecting a vintage name, consider the primary linguistic environment in which your child will use it.

## Popularity Trends

### The U-Shaped Curve of Vintage Names

Onomastic research has identified a consistent pattern in the lifecycle of vintage names: they follow a U-shaped curve. A name rises in popularity during one era, declines to near-obscurity, and then rises again—often reaching different demographic groups during its revival.

**Mabel** peaked at #14 in the US in 1880, fell to #847 by 1960, and rose to #313 by 2023. **Hazel** peaked at #27 in 1910, fell to #573 by 1960, and rose to #75 by 2023. **Theodore** peaked at #5 in 1880, fell to #389 by 1960, and rose to #21 by 2023. These patterns are remarkably consistent across the vintage name corpus.

### The 2025–2026 Data

According to preliminary SSA data for 2025 and ONS data for 2025, the following vintage names have shown the strongest upward trajectories:

**Top Rising Vintage Names (2025):**

- **Clara** (US: top 100, UK: top 100)

- **Silas** (US: top 100, UK: top 200)

- **Archie** (UK: top 20, US: rising)

- **Freya** (UK: top 20, US: top 100)

### The Long Tail of Obscurity

While the names above have achieved significant popularity, the vast majority of vintage names remain in the "long tail" of the popularity distribution—known but not widely used. Names like **Beatrix**, **Seraphina**, **Clementine**, **Aurelia**, and **Genevieve** remain well below the top 200 but are increasingly recognized by parents seeking distinctive options. This long tail represents the largest reservoir of truly unique vintage names available to contemporary parents.

## FAQ

### Q: What makes a name "vintage" versus "old-fashioned"?
A: The distinction is largely cultural and temporal. "Vintage" names are those from a previous era that have been revived or are experiencing renewed interest—typically names from the 1800s to the 1960s that carry historical elegance without feeling dated. "Old-fashioned" names are those that have fallen out of favor and are perceived as belonging to a specific generation (usually grandparents' names). The line between vintage and old-fashioned is subjective; **Mabel** is vintage to some and old-fashioned to others. The key difference is whether the name feels intentional and stylish rather than accidental or dated.

### Q: Are vintage names better for boys or girls?
A: Vintage naming is rich for all genders, but the available inventory differs. Girls' vintage names number in the thousands, drawing from Victorian, Edwardian, and mid-century traditions. Boys' vintage names are equally rich but have experienced less revival momentum—names like **Archibald**, **Bartholomew**, **Montague**, and **Percival** remain relatively uncommon. This means boys' vintage names may offer more uniqueness, while girls' vintage names offer more options that are already culturally validated.

### Q: Will my child be teased for having a vintage name?
A: Research on childhood bullying and naming suggests that the risk of name-based teasing is minimal compared to other factors like weight, academic performance, or social skills. A 2016 study in the *Economic Journal* found that names perceived as "old-fashioned" did not significantly affect educational outcomes or social integration. In fact, vintage names often stand out in a positive way—children with distinctive names are frequently complimented rather than mocked. The key is pairing a vintage first name with a conventional middle name, which provides both distinctiveness and practicality.

### Q: How do I choose a vintage name that won't feel dated in 20 years?
A: The best vintage names are those that have already survived one or more cycles of popularity. Names like **Clara**, **Theodore**, **Mabel**, and **Beatrice** have been popular, fallen out of fashion, and returned—demonstrating resilience across decades. Names that were never popular in the first place (deep cuts from parish records) carry less risk of feeling dated because they have no association with a specific trend cycle. The safest vintage names are those that feel timeless rather than trendy.

### Q: Can I use a vintage name from a culture I'm not part of?
A: This is a nuanced question with no single correct answer. Cultural appreciation and cultural appropriation exist on a spectrum. If you have genuine connections to a culture—through family, heritage, travel, or deep study—using names from that tradition can be meaningful and appropriate. If you have no connection beyond aesthetic appreciation, it is worth considering whether the name carries cultural significance that you might be unintentionally appropriating. When in doubt, consult members of that culture and prioritize names that are already established in English-speaking usage.

### Q: What is the most unique vintage name I can choose?
A: The most unique vintage names come from deep archival research—parish registers, census records, and genealogical databases. Names like **Eulalia**, **Ophelia**, **Seraphina**, **Calista**, and **Mireille** are vintage but remain well outside the top 500 in most English-speaking countries. For truly rare options, consider names like **Aurelia**, **Clementine**, **Desirae**, **Eulalia**, **Flavia**, **Gwendolyn**, **Lavinia**, **Mireille**, **Ophelia**, and **Seraphina**—all vintage, all distinctive, all with rich etymological histories.

### Q: Should I pair a vintage first name with a modern middle name?
A: This is a matter of personal preference, but there are strong arguments for both approaches. Pairing a vintage first name with a vintage middle name (e.g., **Adelaide Rose**, **Theodore James**) creates a cohesive, timeless feel. Pairing a vintage first name with a modern middle name (e.g., **Adelaide Nova**, **Theodore Rex**) creates an interesting contrast that bridges eras. The most important consideration is phonetic flow—say the full name aloud and listen for awkward combinations.

### Q: How do I find vintage names that match my family heritage?
A: Start with family genealogy—baptismal records, census data, and family Bibles are rich sources of vintage names. Check the names of great-great-grandparents, aunts, uncles, and cousins. Consider the geographic origins of your family; a Scottish family might explore **Cillian**, **Isla**, and **Morwenna**, while an Italian family might explore **Chiara**, **Luca**, and **Serafina**. Online genealogy databases like Ancestry.com and FamilySearch.org can help you discover names that have appeared in your family tree.

### Q: Are there vintage names that work well across cultures?
A: Yes. Names with simple phonetic structures and widely recognized etymologies tend to travel well across cultures. **Clara**, **Elena**, **Julia**, **Lucas**, **Mila**, **Noah**, **Rosa**, **Sofia**, **Theo**, and **Zoe** are all vintage names that are easily pronounced and recognized in multiple languages. Names with complex phonotactics (like **Esme** or **Thaddeus**) may require explanation in multilingual contexts.
