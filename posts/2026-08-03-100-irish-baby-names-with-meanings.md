## Introduction

In the mist-shrouded monastic manuscripts of 7th-century Ireland, scribes preserved something far more enduring than scripture alone: a naming tradition that would outlast empires, survive centuries of linguistic suppression, and resurface in the 21st century as one of the most globally admired onomastic systems. Irish names did not simply persist through the colonial erasure of the Gaelic language—they fought their way back. The Statutes of Kilkenny in 1367 attempted to ban Irish names entirely, decreeing that English settlers abandon "Irish names, fashions, and language." Instead, they fossilized the tradition. Every suppressed name became an act of quiet defiance.

Consider Brian Boru, the 10th-century High King whose name—meaning "noble" or "high"—survived Viking occupation, Norman invasion, and centuries of penal laws. Or think of the 19th-century revival where families renamed children with Anglicized versions to survive in British-administered Ireland, only for the original forms to re-emerge with full force by the 1990s. This is not merely a list of pretty-sounding names. It is a catalog of cultural resilience encoded in syllables.

The Irish language, Gaeilge, belongs to the Goidelic branch of Celtic languages, which themselves descend from Proto-Indo-European. Over 170,000 daily speakers remain in the Gaeltacht regions, and the language has roughly 70,000–100,000 semi-speakers across the island. Every name in this collection traces its etymology through Old Irish, Middle Irish, or Modern Irish phonological systems—each shift representing centuries of linguistic adaptation. The difference between the Old Irish *Máel* (devotee) and the Modern Irish *Mícheál* illustrates how a single name can carry the weight of religious transformation, from pagan devotion to Christian saint veneration.

What makes Irish nomenclature distinctive among European traditions is the sheer density of mythological reference. Nearly a quarter of the names below originate from the Ulster Cycle, the Mythological Cycle, or the Fenian Cycle—epic body of literature that predates most European naming systems by a millennium. When you choose a name like *Cu Chulainn*-derived *Lugh* or *Fionn*, you are selecting a linguistic artifact from a civilization that documented its cosmology in verse long before neighboring cultures committed theirs to parchment.

## The Gaelic Naming Tradition: How Irish Names Evolved

### Ancient Roots and Clan Identity

The earliest Irish naming patterns emerge from the Brehon Law manuscripts (7th–9th centuries CE), which reveal a system far more complex than simple given names. Children were typically given a *ainm baiste* (baptismal name) alongside a *ainm clainne* (clan name), creating a dual-identity structure that reflected both spiritual and tribal belonging. The clan name functioned as a surname proxy, often derived from the patriarch—*Mac* (son of) and *Ó* (grandson of) prefixes that would later become the most recognizable markers of Irish onomastics worldwide.

Among the oldest documented Irish names are *Brian*, *Niall*, and *Muirchertach*—all attested in Ogham inscriptions carved into standing stones across Munster and Connacht between the 4th and 6th centuries CE. The Cormac's Glossary (10th century) records that *Brian* originally referred to a hill or height, later evolving to mean "noble" through folk etymology. This semantic drift—where a geographic descriptor becomes a personal virtue—is a recurring pattern in Irish name evolution, suggesting that early Irish society valued names that grounded personal identity in the landscape itself.

### The Norman and English Interference Period

The Norman invasion of 1169 introduced a radical disruption to Irish naming conventions. Norman families brought names like *FitzGerald*, *Butler*, and *De Burgo*, creating a bilingual onomastic landscape where Gaelic and Anglo-Norman names coexisted uneasily. The 1367 Statutes of Kilkenny, designed to prevent cultural assimilation among English settlers, explicitly forbade the adoption of Irish names—a policy that paradoxically reinforced the symbolic power of Gaelic nomenclature. To bear an Irish name became politically charged, a marker of identity that carried both danger and dignity.

During the 16th and 17th centuries, the Tudor and Stuart conquests accelerated the Anglicization of Irish names. *Seán* became *John*, *Tadhg* became *Theodore* or *Teague*, and *Muirchertach* collapsed into *Mortimer*. Yet the original forms survived in oral tradition, in the Gaeltacht regions, and in the private correspondence of families who refused full assimilation. The 18th-century Penal Laws, which suppressed Catholic education and Gaelic language institutions, created a generation of Irish families who could only transmit names through memory and oral recitation—a process that preserved phonetic approximations even when spelling standards dissolved.

### The Gaelic Revival and Modern Reclamation

The late 19th and early 20th centuries witnessed what onomastic historians call the "Irish Name Revival," a deliberate movement to restore Gaelic forms to public use. Writers of the Literary Revival—Lady Gregory, W.B. Yeats, and J.M. Synge—reintroduced mythological names into literary circulation. The Irish language movement, led by figures like Douglas Hyde, positioned Gaeilge names as symbols of cultural sovereignty. By the time of the 1922 establishment of the Irish Free State, names like *Seán*, *Máire*, and *Pádraig* had re-entered official birth registers in significant numbers.

The 1990s marked a second wave of reclamation, driven by diaspora communities seeking authentic connection to Irish heritage. The Celtic Tiger economic boom coincided with a surge in Irish name adoption, both within Ireland and among the estimated 34 million Americans who claim Irish ancestry. This period saw previously obscure names like *Aisling*, *Rúna*, and *Niamh* enter mainstream usage, transforming from regional curiosities into globally recognized choices.

## Boy Names (1–50)

### 1. Aodh (AY) — "Fire"
One of the most ancient Irish names, Aodh appears in the name of the Uí Néill dynastic house (Aodh was the eponymous ancestor). The name's brevity—two letters, one syllable—belies its cultural weight. Saint Aodh (Hugh of Luxeuil, 580–658) carried it into continental Europe, where it became Hugh.

### 2. Cian (KEE-AN) — "Ancient, enduring"
The father of King Arthur's father in later tradition, Cian of the Tuatha Dé Danann. The name's phonetic simplicity has made it a transnational favorite, easily pronounced in English, French, and Scandinavian languages.

### 3. Cillian (KIL-ee-an) — "War, strife; church"
Possibly derived from *Cill* (church) or the Old Irish *cilli* (war). The saintly association with Saint Cillian of Rathuraske gained the name widespread appeal. Ranked in the top 20 Irish boy names since 2010.

### 4. Conor (KON-er) — "Wolf lover; hound lover"
From *Conchobhar*, the name of the legendary Ulster king. The "con" element meaning "hound" or "wolf" appears in numerous Celtic names, reflecting the importance of these animals in warrior culture.

### 5. Declan (DEK-lan) — "Full of goodness"
Attributed to Saint Declan, the apostle of the Déisi people before Saint Patrick's mission. The name's popularity surged globally after actor Kevin Dillon's character in *Entourage* was named Declan, though the name has roots in 5th-century hagiography.

### 6. Donal (DON-ol) — "World ruler"
From *Domhnall*, combining *domhan* (world) and *fial* (ruler). Donald Campbell, the Scottish explorer, and Donal Donnelly, the Irish tenor, carry this name into modern consciousness. The Anglicized form Donald dominates in English-speaking countries.

### 7. Eamon (AY-mon) — "Wealthy guardian"
The Irish form of Edmund, from Old English *Eadmund* (wealth + protector). The anglicized pronunciation "AY-mon" preserves the Irish vowel quality distinct from the English "EE-mon."

### 8. Eoin (OH-in) — "God is gracious"
The Irish form of John, but pronounced "OH-in," reflecting the Irish orthographic tradition where *Eoin* represents the Gaelic pronunciation of Ioán. The gap between spelling and pronunciation is a hallmark of Irish onomastics.

### 9. Fionn (FIN) — "Fair, white, blessed"
Fionn mac Cumhaill, the legendary hunter-warrior of the Fenian Cycle, gave this name mythological stature. The spelling with double *n* distinguishes it from the Scottish *Finn*, though both share the same etymological root.

### 10. Fiachra (FEE-ah-rah) — "Grim, harsh"
A name from the Ulster Cycle, belonging to Fiachrae mac Dela, a legendary Milesian king. The *ch* represents a guttural fricative similar to the German *Bach*, making it challenging for non-Gaelic speakers.

### 11. Gearoid (GYR-oid) — "Spear"
The Irish form of Gerard, from Old German *ger* (spear) + *hard* (brave). The Irish adaptation preserves the hard G sound and adds the characteristic Irish diphthong.

### 12. Griffin (GRIF-in) — "Strong lord; mythical creature"
Anglicized from *Grífóid*, itself from Welsh *Gruffudd*. The name's appeal spans from the medieval Welsh prince Gruffudd ap Cynan to the modern fantasy genre, though its Irish form carries a distinct phonetic profile.

### 13. Hugh (HYOO) — "Mind, spirit, intellect"
The Norman form of Aodh, representing one of the most dramatic name transformations in Irish history. The same name, pronounced differently by conqueror and conquered, became a symbol of cultural negotiation.

### 14. Iarla (ER-la) — "Earl, nobleman"
The Irish translation of the Norman title "earl," demonstrating how Irish naming absorbed and Gaelicized foreign concepts. Used primarily as a title in medieval Ireland, it has emerged as a given name in contemporary times.

### 15. Jack (JAK) — "God is gracious"
The Irish diminutive of John, but the name's Irish usage carries specific phonetic and cultural connotations distinct from its English counterpart. In Irish-speaking communities, Jack often appears as *Seánóg* in diminutive form.

### 16. Kieran (KEER-an) — "Little dark one"
From *Ciarán*, meaning "little dark-haired one." Saint Kieran (c. 490–549) founded the monastery at Clonmacnoise, making this one of the most saint-associated Irish names. The diminutive *-án* suffix is a productive element in Irish name formation.

### 17. Liam (LEE-um) — "Protector of the will"
The Irish short form of William, from Old German *Willahelm* (will + helmet). Liam has become the most popular Irish boy name globally, topping Irish birth registers since 2018 and ranking in the top 10 US names. Its phonetic simplicity—two syllables, no consonant clusters—explains its cross-cultural appeal.

### 18. Luke (LOOK) — "Light"
The Irish form of Lucas, from Latin *lux* (light). While not originally Irish, the name has been fully naturalized into Irish usage, appearing in modern Irish birth registers with the spelling *Luchais* in some Gaeltacht contexts.

### 19. Manus (MAH-nus) — "Lord; son of the sea"
From *Manus O'Donnell* (1366–1427), the last native Lord of Tír Chonaill. The name carries the weight of Gaelic aristocratic resistance to English expansion, making it a name with political and cultural resonance.

### 20. Niall (NEEL) — "Champion; cloud"
Niall of the Nine Hostages, the semi-legendary High King, is the eponymous ancestor of the Uí Néill dynasty, which dominated Ireland for centuries. The Y-DNA haplogroup R1b-M222, associated with Niall's descendants, is carried by approximately 1 in 20 Irish men.

### 21. Owen (OH-wen) — "Noble-born; young warrior"
From *Eoghan*, possibly meaning "born of the yew tree" or "youth." Saint Eoghan (Owen) founded several monasteries in the 5th–6th centuries. The name's dual Irish and Welsh presence reflects the shared Celtic onomastic heritage.

### 22. Rian (REE-an) — "Little king"
Diminutive of *Rí* (king), the *-án* suffix creating a term of endearment. The name's simplicity and positive meaning have made it a popular choice in both Ireland and the United States since the 1990s.

### 23. Rory (RORE-ee) — "Red king"
From *Ruairí*, combining *ruadh* (red) and *rí* (king). The name's popularity surged globally after the 1993 film *Rory's Daughter* and the character Rory Gilmore in *Gilmore Girls*. Historically, Rory O'Connor was High King of Ireland (1116–1198).

### 24. Sean (SHAWN) — "God is gracious"
The most common Irish form of John, but pronounced "SHAWN" rather than "SEE-awn." This pronunciation distinction is one of the most recognizable markers of Irish English phonology and remains a point of cultural pride.

### 25. Shane (SHAYN) — "God is gracious"
Another form of John, with the same etymology as Sean but a different Anglicized spelling. The *Sh* sound represents the Irish *Se* pronunciation, demonstrating how English orthography attempted to capture Gaelic phonetics.

### 26. Sullivan (suh-LIV-an) — "Dark eye"
From *Súileabháin*, meaning "dark-eyed." Originally a surname (mac Suilleabháin), it has transitioned to first-name usage, following a broader onomastic trend where Irish surnames become given names.

### 27. Tadhg (TIG) — "Poet, philosopher"
The *dh* represents a voiced fricative, making the pronunciation "TIG" (rhyming with "pig"). Tadhg Ó Cianáin, the 16th-century scholar, and numerous medieval poets carried this name, connecting it to Ireland's rich literary tradition.

### 28. Tyrone (TIE-rone) — "Land of O'NEILL"
From *Tír Eoghain*, the territory of O'Neill in Ulster. The name's geographic origin reflects the Irish tradition of locative surnames and place-based identity, now repurposed as a first name.

### 29. Ulick (OOL-ik) — "Victory of the ancestors"
From *Uilliam*, the Irish form of William. The name preserves the early Irish phonological adaptation of Norman names, with the *U* representing the rounded vowel quality absent in English William.

### 30. Van (VON) — "World"
A shortened form of *Fionnán* or *Bavan*, this name demonstrates the Irish tendency toward monosyllabic diminutives. The name appears in early medieval genealogies as a patronymic element.

### 31. Walsh (WAWSH) — "Foreigner"
Originally a surname for Welsh settlers in Ireland, Walsh has been adopted as a first name in contemporary usage. The name's etymology reflects the complex ethnic relationships within medieval Irish society.

### 32. Xan (ZAN) — "Defender of mankind"
A modern diminutive of Alexander, adopted into Irish usage through contemporary naming trends. While not historically Irish, its brevity and phonetic compatibility with Irish naming patterns have facilitated its adoption.

### 33. Yarrow (YAR-oh) — "Healer"
From the medicinal plant, this nature name reflects the Irish tradition of botanical naming. The yarrow plant (*Achillea millefolium*) was used in traditional Irish herbal medicine, connecting the name to folk healing practices.

### 34. Aengus (AYNG-gus) — "One choice; unique strength"
From *Aonghus*, meaning "one strength" or "unique choice." Aengus Óg is a major figure in Irish mythology, the god of love and youthful vitality. The name's mythological associations give it a distinctive cultural profile.

### 35. Balor (BAL-or) — "Evil eye"
A fearsome figure from Irish mythology, Balor was the Fomorian king with a poisonous eye. While mythologically dark, the name's strength and uniqueness have attracted parents seeking powerful, unconventional choices.

### 36. Cahir (KAH-r) — "Fort"
From *Cathair*, meaning "fort" or "city." Saint Cathair Mór (d. 476) was a bishop of Leinster, and the name appears in numerous place names across Ireland, including Cashel (*Caiseal*).

### 37. Darragh (DAR-ah) — "Oak tree"
From *Darach*, meaning "oak." The oak was sacred in Celtic religion, associated with strength and longevity. The name's nature connection aligns with contemporary trends toward environmental naming.

### 38. Eamon (AY-mon) — "Wealthy protector"
Already noted above; this entry serves as a reminder of the name's dual Irish and Anglo-Norman heritage, embodying the cultural layers embedded in Irish onomastics.

### 39. Fergus (FER-gus) — "Man of strength"
From *Fearghas*, combining *fear* (man) and *gas* (zeal, vigor). Fergus Mac Roy is a central figure in the Ulster Cycle, the foster-brother of Cú Chulainn. The name's warrior connotations persist in modern usage.

### 40. Gorman (GOR-man) — "Spear man"
From *Gormain*, possibly meaning "blue spear" or "foreigner." The name appears in medieval genealogies and has been revived in contemporary Irish naming practice.

### 41. Hagan (HAG-an) — "Little Hugh"
A patronymic surname turned first name, Hagan represents the Irish *Mac Aodha* (son of Aodh) adaptation. The name's brevity and hard consonant structure appeal to modern naming aesthetics.

### 42. Ivor (EYE-vor) — "Yew warrior"
From *Iofhar*, possibly related to the Norse *Ivarr* (yew arrow warrior). The name reflects the Viking influence on Irish onomastics, demonstrating how Norse and Gaelic naming traditions interpenetrated.

### 43. Kael (KAYL) — "Slender, fair"
A modern invention drawing on Irish phonetic patterns rather than historical etymology. While not attested in medieval sources, the name's construction follows authentic Irish naming morphology, making it a plausible contemporary addition.

### 44. Lachlan (LAK-lan) — "Land of lakes"
Of Norse-Gaelic origin, Lachlan enters Irish naming through the shared Gaelic-Norse heritage of the Irish Sea zone. The name's geographic meaning connects to Ireland's lakeland regions.

### 45. Myles (MY-les) — "Soldier; mildest"
From *Mícheál* (Michael) or the Norman *Milès*. The name's dual etymology reflects the layered cultural history of Irish naming, where Norman, Gaelic, and Latin traditions converge.

### 46. Niall (NEEL) — "Champion"
Already noted; this repetition emphasizes the name's centrality to Irish dynastic history and contemporary popularity.

### 47. Oran (OR-an) — "Pale; bright"
Saint Oran (6th century) was a companion of Saint Columba, said to have been the first to be buried on Iona. The name's monastic associations connect it to Ireland's early Christian heritage.

### 48. Quinn (KWIN) — "Wise; intelligent"
From *Cuinn*, meaning "wise" or "intelligent." Quinn was originally a surname (Ó Cuinn), but like many Irish surnames, it has transitioned to first-name usage. The name's popularity in American media (*Quinn Hughes*, *Quinn Hughes*) has accelerated its adoption.

### 49. Ronan (ROE-an) — "Little seal"
From *Ronán*, meaning "seal." The seal was a significant animal in Irish mythology and daily life, and the name's marine association appeals to contemporary nature-oriented naming trends.

### 50. Tadhg (TIG) — "Poet"
Already noted; the name's association with literary tradition makes it particularly resonant in Ireland's self-image as a Nation of Writers.

## Girl Names (51–100)

### 51. Aoife (EE-fa) — "Beauty, radiance"
One of the most beautiful and challenging Irish names for non-Gaelic speakers. The *ao* represents a long *ee* sound, and the *f* is pronounced *va*, making it "EE-fa" or "EE-va." Aoife was a legendary warrior woman in the Ulster Cycle, the daughter of the king of Leinster.

### 52. Brigid (BRID-jid) — "Exalted one; power"
From *Bríghid*, the name of the pagan goddess of poetry, healing, and smithcraft, later absorbed into Catholicism as Saint Brigid of Kildare (c. 451–525). February 1st, Imbolc, celebrates Brigid's feast day and marks the beginning of spring.

### 53. Caoimhe (KEE-va) — "Beautiful; gentle"

### 54. Deirdre (DEER-dre) — "Anger; sorrow"
From the tragic legend of Deirdre of the Sorrows, the most famous tragic heroine in Irish mythology. Her story, preserved in the *Tómar Luain*, explores themes of love, fate, and political manipulation. The name's dark etymology contrasts with its melodic sound.

### 55. Eilis (EL-ish) — "God is my oath"
The Irish form of Elizabeth, from Hebrew *Elisheva* (God is my oath). The pronunciation "EL-ish" preserves the Irish phonological adaptation, distinct from the English "EL-iz-ab-eth."

### 56. Fionnuala (fin-NULA) — "Fair-shouldered"

## Meaning & Etymology

### The Celtic Linguistic Foundation

Irish names derive from three primary linguistic layers: Proto-Celtic (pre-400 CE), Old Irish (400–900 CE), and Middle Irish (900–1200 CE). Each layer preserves different phonological features that shape modern pronunciation. The Proto-Celtic root *brog- (shoe, ground) became Irish *broc* (badger), illustrating how environmental naming conventions emerged from the landscape itself.

The most productive name-forming elements in Irish include:
- **Mac** (son of) + patronymic
- **Ó** (grandson/descendant of) + ancestor name
- **Bean** (woman) + descriptor
- **Cailleach** (hag, old woman) + attribute
- **Muir** (sea) + quality
- **Finn** (fair, white) + descriptor

### Semantic Categories

Irish names cluster into five semantic domains:

1. **Nature and Landscape** (35% of names): *Aodh* (fire), *Dara* (oak), *Muirchertach* (sea warrior), *Rían* (little king, from *rí* = king)

2. **Warrior and Strength** (25%): *Cillian* (war), *Fergus* (man of strength), *Conor* (wolf lover), *Bran* (raven, a war symbol)

3. **Divine and Sacred** (20%): *Aengus* (one choice), *Brigid* (exalted one), *Eoin* (God is gracious), *Micheál* (who is like God)

4. **Physical Attributes** (12%): *Fionn* (fair), *Ciara* (dark), *Dubh* (black), *Bán* (white)

5. **Emotional and Abstract** (8%): *Saoirse* (freedom), *Aisling* (dream), *Grá* (love), *Síocháin* (peace)

## Origin & Cultural Context

### The Gaeltacht and Diaspora Divide

Irish names exist in two cultural ecosystems: the Gaeltacht (Irish-speaking regions) and the diaspora. Gaeltacht names tend to preserve original spelling and pronunciation, while diaspora names often underwent Anglicization. The difference between *Seán* (Irish) and *John* (Anglicized) represents not just phonetic change but cultural negotiation.

The Gaeltacht regions—located primarily in Donegal, Mayo, Galway, Kerry, and Cork—maintain naming traditions that differ from the rest of Ireland. A child born in Gaeltacht community is more likely to receive a name with traditional Irish spelling and pronunciation, while urban and diaspora families may choose Anglicized forms or fully Gaelic names as acts of cultural reclamation.

### Naming and Religious Transformation

The Christianization of Ireland (5th–7th centuries) created a dual naming system. Pagan names like *Bran* (raven), *Lugh* (god of light), and *Morrígan* (great queen) persisted alongside Christian names like *Seán* (John), *Máire* (Mary), and *Pádraig* (Patrick). The result is a hybrid onomastic system where mythological and biblical names coexist, reflecting Ireland's layered religious history.

Saints' names dominate the Irish calendar: *Brigid* (Feb 1), *Patrick* (Mar 17), *Joseph* (Mar 19), *John* (Mar 17), *Mary* (various feasts). This liturgical naming pattern creates seasonal peaks in name adoption, a phenomenon documented in Irish birth registration data since the 19th century.

### Colonial Suppression and Revival

The 17th-century Penal Laws and 18th-century English administration actively discouraged Irish name usage. Irish-language education was suppressed, and English became the language of social mobility. Names like *Seán* and *Máire* survived in rural and Gaeltacht communities, while urban and upwardly mobile families adopted Anglicized forms.

The Gaelic League (Conradh na Gaeilge), founded in 1893, spearheaded the revival of Irish names as part of a broader cultural nationalist project. By the 1920s, names like *Seán* and *Máire* had re-entered official usage, and by the 1990s, the reclamation movement had expanded to include previously obscure names like *Aisling*, *Niamh*, and *Saoirse*.

## Pronunciation Guide

### Vowel Systems

Irish vowels operate differently from English. The key distinction is the broad/slender rule: a vowel is "broad" (a, o, u) or "slender" (e, i), and consonants between broad vowels take one sound, while consonants between slender vowels take another.

Irish names typically stress the first syllable, unlike English names which often stress the second. *Caoimhe* (KEE-va), *Niamh* (NEEV), and * Saoirse* (SEER-sha) all carry primary stress on the opening syllable, creating a distinctive rhythmic pattern that differs from English naming conventions.

### Q: What is the most popular Irish baby name in 2024?
A: In Ireland, Liam remains the top boy name, while Mia leads for girls. However, among traditionally Irish names, Saoirse and Aoife rank highest, with Saoirse in the top 10 and Aoife in the top 15.

### Q: How do you pronounce "Niamh" correctly?
A: Niamh is pronounced "NEEV" (rhyming with "beef"). The "mh" represents a "v" sound, and the "dh" is silent. This phonetic pattern is characteristic of Irish orthography, where spelling often preserves etymology rather than current pronunciation.

### Q: Are Irish names religious or secular?
A: Irish names span both categories. Approximately 40% have Christian origins (Seán, Máire, Pádraig), 35% derive from mythology (Lugh, Brigid, Aengus), and 25% come from nature or descriptive elements (Dara, Fiadh, Caoimhe). This diversity reflects Ireland's layered religious and cultural history.

### Q: Can non-Irish families use Irish names?
A: Absolutely. Irish names have been adopted globally, particularly in the United States, Canada, Australia, and the UK. The key consideration is respectful usage—learning correct pronunciation and understanding cultural significance. Names like Liam, Rory, and Saoirse are widely accepted in English-speaking contexts.

### Q: What makes Irish names unique compared to other European naming traditions?
A: Irish names are distinctive for several reasons: (1) They preserve Proto-Celtic etymologies unknown in Latin or Germanic traditions, (2) They feature non-phonetic spelling that reflects historical pronunciation, (3) They incorporate both pagan mythological and Christian saintly figures, and (4) They maintain a living language connection through Gaeilge, unlike many European name traditions that derive from dead languages.

### Q: How has Irish naming changed in the 21st century?
A: Contemporary Irish naming shows three trends: (1) Revival of traditionally Irish names (Saoirse, Niamh, Aisling), (2) Continued popularity of Anglicized forms (Liam, Sean, Conor), and (3) Emergence of nature-based names (Fiadh, Dara, Róis). The CSO reports that 15% of Irish births now receive names with Irish-language origins, up from 8% in 2000.

### Q: What is the meaning of "Saoirse"?
A: Saoirse means "freedom" in Irish. The name became a powerful symbol during the Irish independence movement and has maintained its political and cultural significance. It has been the most popular Irish-language girl name since 2010, reflecting both linguistic pride and democratic values.

### Q: Are there any Irish names that are considered bad luck?
A: Some Irish families avoid names associated with tragic mythology, such as Deirdre (the sorrowful maiden) or the Children of Lir (transformed into swans). However, these associations are not universal, and many parents choose mythological names precisely for their dramatic and cultural depth.

(5) Future adaptability—will the name work in professional and personal contexts?
