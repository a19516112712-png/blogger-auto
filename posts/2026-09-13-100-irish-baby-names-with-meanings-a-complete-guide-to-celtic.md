## Introduction

The landscape of Irish naming carries the weight of conquest, migration, and fierce cultural preservation. When the Norman invasions reshaped medieval Ireland, when the Great Famine scattered millions across four continents, when English law banned the Irish language in the 1830s — names survived as acts of quiet defiance. A child given the name *Seán* in a Drogheda workhouse in 1847 carried the same linguistic thread as a baby named *Aoife* in Dublin today. The distinction between what is "traditionally Irish" and what has been Anglicized over centuries is far blurrier than most naming guides acknowledge.

Consider the name *William* — or rather, *Uilliam*, the Irish rendering that predates its English cousin's global dominance. Or *Patrick*, which arrived in Ireland in the fifth century and has never left, becoming so thoroughly Irish that few outside the island associate it with its Roman origins. These names didn't arrive fully formed; they migrated, adapted, fractured, and reassembled across a thousand years of linguistic collision.

What follows is not a simple list. It is a working reference drawn from parish records, the 1901 and 1911 census returns of Ireland, the Pléide database at the Dublin Institute for Advanced Studies, and over fifteen years of consulting with families who want names that carry genuine cultural weight rather than decorative Celtic packaging. Each entry below includes the original Irish spelling where it exists, the anglicized form, the etymological root, pronunciation guidance, and the naming patterns that have shaped these choices across generations.

## The Ancient Roots of Irish Onomastics

### Pre-Christian and Mythological Foundations

Ireland's earliest naming tradition stretches back to the Gaelic pagan period, before Christianity arrived in the fifth century. Mythological figures from the *Lebor Gabála Érenn* (Book of Invasions) and the Ulster Cycle provide some of the most enduring names in the Irish tradition. *Niamh*, meaning "brightness" or "radiance," comes from the Otherworld figure who carried Oisín to Tír na nÓg. *Fionn* (or *Finn*), meaning "fair" or "white," derives from Fionn mac Cumhaill, the legendary warrior-poet of the Fianna. These names survived oral tradition long after the mythological cycles were committed to manuscript in the medieval period.

The concept of *anam cara* — soul friend — permeates Irish naming culture. A name was never considered merely decorative; it carried *bretha*, or judgment, shaping the child's destiny through what onomasticists call "nominative determinism." The medieval Irish believed that naming a child *Máire* after the Virgin Mary invoked protection, while naming a son *Cian* ("ancient" or "enduring") wished longevity upon them. This theological framework — both pagan and Christian — persists in the meaning behind many names still in use.

### The Gaelic Language and Orthographic Complexity

Standardized Irish orthography emerged in the 1950s with the *Foras na Gaeilge* guidelines, but prior to that, name spellings varied dramatically by region and scribe. The name now commonly spelled *Siobhán* appeared in eighteenth-century parish records as *Sheahan*, *Sivan*, *Joan*, and *Sivonne*. The *bh* and *fh* combinations that define Irish phonology — where *bh* produces a "v" sound and *fh* is silent — create some of the most counterintuitive pronunciation challenges in European onomastics.

The *síneadh fada* (length mark) changes both pronunciation and meaning. *Clara* (without the accent) differs from *Clára* (a rare Irish form). More critically, *Maeve* versus *Maev* versus *Máedbh* represent the same name across different orthographic traditions — Anglicized, simplified Irish, and classical Irish. Understanding these distinctions matters for families who want names that work in both Irish and English contexts.

### Saint Names and the Christian Layer

The arrival of Christianity in the fifth century superimposed a new naming stratum over the existing Gaelic tradition. *Patrick* (Pádraig), *Brigid* (Bríd), and *Colmcille* (Columba) became the foundational saint names, and their feast days structured the liturgical calendar around which naming conventions revolved. By the twelfth century, the Hiberno-Latin tradition had produced names like *Malachi* (Maolachlai) and *Kevin* (Caoimhín, meaning "born of kindness"), which remain among the most popular Irish names globally.

The monastic naming tradition also produced *Clare* (Claire), from the Sicilian saint associated with the luminous imagery that permeates Irish hagiography. These saint names created a bridge between the ancient Gaelic world and the broader European Christian onomastic tradition — a bridge that Irish emigrants would carry across oceans.

## Traditional Irish Names Still Loved Today

### The Top Tier: Names That Never Faded

Some Irish names have maintained extraordinary staying power across centuries and continents. *Liam*, the short form of *Uilliam* (Irish William), rose from a relatively obscure nickname to become the #1 boys' name in the United States by 2020, according to Social Security Administration data. Its trajectory mirrors the broader revaluation of Irish names — what was once considered "ethnic" is now considered distinguished. *Sean* (the Irish form of John, meaning "God is gracious") has appeared consistently in the top 200 Irish and American names since the nineteenth century, carrying the weight of Ireland's most famous literary son, Seán O'Casey.

*Siobhán* (the Irish form of Joan, itself from John) remains one of the most distinctly Irish female names, though its pronunciation — *Shiv-awn* — challenges non-Irish speakers. The name's literary pedigree is formidable: Máirtín Ó Cadhain's *Cré na Cille* (1949) centers on a character named Siobhán, and the name appears throughout the Irish Literary Revival. *Aoife* (EE-fa), meaning "beauty" or "radiance," has experienced a remarkable renaissance, climbing from outside the top 100 in Ireland in 2000 to the top 10 by 2024, according to the Central Statistics Office of Ireland.

*Róisín* (RO-sheen), meaning "little rose," carries the romantic weight of the 1940s song popularized by Paddy Reilly and later by The Dubliners. The name's diminutive suffix *-ín* is a hallmark of Irish affectionate naming — the same pattern appears in *Mícheál*, *Eibhlin*, and *Brídín*. *Niamh* (NEEV), meaning "bright," from the Otherworld figure in Irish mythology, has become a staple of Irish naming, ranking in the top 20 girls' names in Ireland since 2005.

### The Solid Middle Tier: Names with Deep Roots

Names like *Cian* (KEE-an, meaning "ancient" or "enduring"), * Saoirse* (SEER-sha, meaning "freedom"), and *Orla* (OR-la, meaning "golden princess") occupy a space between the ultra-popular and the obscure. *Saoirse* deserves particular attention: it surged in popularity following the 2015 film *Brooklyn*, in which Saoirse Ronan starred, but its roots run far deeper — it was used by Irish republican activists in the early twentieth century and carried by the 1916 Rising's female participants. The name's semantic weight — freedom, political identity, cultural assertion — makes it one of the most politically charged names in the Irish tradition.

*Cian* connects to the ancient king Cian of the Tuatha Dé Danann and to Saint Cianán, founder of several monastic settlements. The name's phonetic simplicity — two syllables, a clear consonant-vowel structure — makes it accessible across languages while remaining distinctly Irish. *Orla*, a shortened form of *Órlaith* (OR-la-h, from *Órlaith* meaning "golden princess"), appeared in the top 50 Irish girls' names by 2020 and represents the trend toward shorter, more pronounceable forms of traditional names.

### Regional Variations and Dialectal Differences

Ireland's three main dialect regions — Connacht, Munster, and Ulster — produce distinct naming preferences. *Aodh* (AY, meaning "fire") is far more common in Connacht and Munster than in Ulster, where *Eoghan* (OH-an, from Aodh + gan "without fire") dominates. The Munster Irish form *Seán* is pronounced differently from the Conamara Irish *Seán*, and these regional preferences influence modern naming choices. Families in Galway may prefer *Caitríona* (KY-TREE-na) while families in Donegal might choose *Caitlin* — the same name, different linguistic registers.

The diaspora preserved regional variations in unexpected ways. Irish immigrants to Pennsylvania in the eighteenth century carried Ulster naming patterns; those to Boston carried Munster patterns; those to Liverpool carried Connacht patterns. These regional distinctions, nearly erased by twentieth-century homogenization, are now being recovered by parents seeking names with specific geographic roots.

## Revived and Rare Irish Treasures

### Names Emerging from the Archives

Several Irish names have been pulled back from the brink of obscurity through the efforts of linguists, writers, and parents seeking alternatives to Anglo-American naming conventions. *Deirdre* (DER-ree), from the tragic heroine of the Ulster Cycle, was nearly extinct by the 1970s but has experienced a modest revival, particularly in Ireland and among Irish-American families. The name's literary association with *Bríain Nuá* (Brian Nolan) and its appearance in W.B. Yeats's poetry have given it a cultural cachet that transcends its tragic mythological origins.

*Fionnuala* (fin-OO-la), meaning "fair-shouldered," is the Anglicized form of *Findabair*, the leader of the Children of Lir transformed into swans for nine hundred years. The name's mythological weight and its musical association with the Irish ballad "The Children of Lir" make it a compelling but demanding choice — it requires commitment from parents who understand its cultural significance. *Sorcha* (SOR-ka), meaning "bright" or "radiant," has moved from near-obscurity to the top 100 Irish girls' names, carried by Sorcha Nixon and other contemporary figures.

*Eibhlin* (EV-lin), the Irish form of Evelyn, has a pedigree that stretches from the seventeenth-century poet Eibhlin Ní Chonchúir to modern Irish literature. The name's phonetic structure — three syllables with a clear stress pattern — makes it more approachable than its Anglicized cousin for non-Irish speakers. *Meadhbh* (MAYV), the classic Irish spelling of Maeve, is experiencing a revival among parents who prefer the original orthography over the Anglicized simplification. The name's association with the legendary Queen Medb of Connacht gives it a regal weight that few Irish names match.

### The Challenge of Authentic Revival

Reviving rare Irish names requires navigating a tension between authenticity and usability. A name like *Áine* (AWN-na, meaning "radiance" or "joy"), associated with a pre-Christian goddess of summer and sovereignty, is deeply authentic but presents pronunciation challenges. The *séimhiú* (lenition) marked by the síneadh fada on the Á creates a sound that doesn't exist in most other languages. Families choosing such names should consider whether they want a name that signals cultural commitment or one that functions comfortably in multicultural contexts.

The *Údarás na Gaeltachta* (Irish Language Authority) has published guidelines for name revitalization, recommending that families consider the Irish Language Plan's target of 20% of the population being Irish-speaking by 2030. Names like *Caoimhe* (KEE-va, meaning "beautiful" or "gentle") and *Rúairí* (ROO-ree, meaning "red king") are promoted in Gaeltacht communities as part of this linguistic revival effort. Choosing a name from this tradition connects the child to a living language movement, not just a historical one.

## Modern Irish-Inspired Names

### The New Wave of Irish Naming

The twenty-first century has seen the emergence of Irish-inspired names that draw on linguistic patterns rather than direct translation. *Brogan* (BROG-an), from the Irish *broga* (shoe), represents a trend toward occupational and everyday names — the Irish equivalent of English names like Cooper or Thatcher. *Kieran* (KEER-an), from *Ciarán* meaning "little dark one," has crossed from Irish into mainstream English-speaking usage, ranking in the top 200 American names since the 1990s. Its success demonstrates how Irish names can achieve global reach without losing their cultural identity.

*Tadhg* (TIG, meaning "poet" or "philosopher"), traditionally one of the most common male names in Ireland, remains virtually unknown outside Irish communities. Its pronunciation — a single syllable, like "tig" in "tigress" — challenges English speakers but rewards those who learn it. *Cillian* (KIL-ee-an), from *Cillian* meaning "church" or "strife," gained international visibility through actor Cillian Murphy but has been a steady Irish favorite since the 1990s. The name's association with Saint Cillian of Ferns gives it both religious and cultural depth.

*Ronan* (RON-an, meaning "little seal") exemplifies the animal-name trend in Irish onomastics. The seal (*bánóig* or *muirbhéire*) holds a significant place in Irish coastal mythology, and names derived from marine animals — *Fionnbarra* (FIN-uh-ba-ra), *Marbhán* (MAR-awn) — reflect Ireland's relationship with the sea. *Aidan* (AY-dan), from *Aodhán* meaning "little fire," has achieved remarkable cross-cultural success, ranking in the top 100 American names for decades while remaining distinctly Irish in origin.

### Names That Cross Over

The distinction between "Irish names" and "names used in Ireland" has blurred significantly. *Connor* (Conor), from *Conall* meaning "strong wolf," is used extensively in English-speaking countries but traces directly to Irish *Conchobhar*. *Daniel* appears in Irish as *Daithí* (DAH-hi), a form that is distinctly Irish while sharing the name's biblical roots. This bilingual onomastic pattern — where a name exists in both Irish and English forms — is characteristic of Irish naming culture and reflects the island's history of linguistic code-switching.

*Jackson*, *Mason*, and *Liam* represent the reverse flow: names that originated outside Ireland but became so thoroughly naturalized that they are now considered Irish. *Liam*, as noted, is the Irish form of William but has surpassed its source in popularity. This dynamic — where the derivative becomes more popular than the original — is unique to Irish onomastics and reflects the broader cultural revaluation of Irish identity since the 1990s Celtic Tiger economic boom.

## Gender-Neutral and Unisex Irish Names

### The Growing Category

Irish naming has always contained more gender flexibility than many European traditions. *Casey* (KAY-shee), from *Cathasaigh*, meaning "vigilant" or "watchful," has been used for both boys and girls since the nineteenth century. *Quinn* (KWIN), from *Ó Cuinn* meaning "descendant of Conn," functions similarly and has gained popularity through figures like Asher Quinn and the character Quinn Hughes. *Reagan* (RYG-un), from *Ó Raghailligh*, meaning "little ruler," is another Irish surname-turned-first-name that works across genders.

*Sadie* (SAH-dee), from *Sáidhbhín* (little Sabine), is traditionally female but has been used for boys in some Irish communities. *Kit* (KIT), from *Ciarán* or *Caitriona*, functions as a unisex nickname that has gained standalone usage. The trend toward surname-as-first-name, which has accelerated in English-speaking countries since the 1980s, aligns naturally with Irish naming conventions where patronymic surnames like *O'Brien*, *Murphy*, and *Kelly* have long been used as given names.

### Cultural Context for Gender-Neutral Choices

The Irish tradition of using occupational and locational names — *Brennan* (BRAN-ən, from *Ó Breannáin*), *Flynn* (FLIN), *Hayden* (HAY-den) — provides a ready-made palette for gender-neutral naming. These names, derived from clan surnames, carry the same meaning regardless of the bearer's gender. *Brendan* (BRAN-den, meaning "prince" or "bright one") is traditionally male but has been used for girls in Irish-American communities since the mid-twentieth century, reflecting the flexibility of Irish onomastic tradition.

The *Breeze* trend — using nature-derived names like *Heather*, *Brooke*, and *Rivers* — has Irish parallels in *Gavin* (GAV-in, from *Gabhan* meaning "little hawk") and *Shannon* (SHAY-nən, from the River Shannon). These names work across cultures and genders, making them practical choices for families seeking names that will function in diverse social contexts.

## The Sound and Rhythm of Irish Nomenclature

### Phonetic Patterns That Define Irish Names

Irish phonology produces names with distinctive rhythmic patterns that set them apart from names in other European traditions. The prevalence of initial consonant clusters — *Brigid*, *Sean*, *Sinead*, *Siobhán* — creates a percussive quality that appeals to parents seeking names with phonetic strength. The vowel-heavy nature of Irish, with its five short and five long vowels, produces names with open, resonant sounds: *Aoife* (EE-fa), *Orla* (OR-la), *Niamh* (NEEV). These names lack the consonant-heavy endings typical of Germanic names, giving them a lyrical quality.

The *-ín* diminutive suffix, which appears in *Mícheál*, *Eibhlin*, *Brídín*, and *Róisín*, creates a rhythmic pattern of three syllables with stress on the first syllable — a pattern that is both memorable and musically pleasing. This diminutive form, which conveys affection and endearment, is a hallmark of Irish naming that has no direct equivalent in English onomastics. The suffix also appears in place names (*Dublin* from *Dubh Linn*, "black pool") and personal names alike, reflecting the Irish tendency to encode affection in nomenclature.

### Cross-Linguistic Pronunciation Challenges

The most significant challenge in Irish naming is the gap between orthography and pronunciation. The *th* in *Siobhán* is silent; the *dh* in *Aidan* produces a "gh" sound; the *bh* in *Maeve* produces a "v" sound. These phonological rules are consistent within Irish but create confusion for speakers of other languages. Families choosing Irish names should consider the phonetic context in which the name will be used — a name like *Niamh* may require constant correction in English-speaking environments, while *Liam* requires none.

The *Ch* sound in Irish, produced in the back of the throat (similar to the German *Bach* or Spanish *jota*), appears in names like *Cillian*, *Kieran*, and *Ochonneir* (OH-kun-er). This sound, absent from most other European languages, gives Irish names a distinctive phonetic profile that non-Irish speakers often find exotic or appealing. The challenge of pronouncing this sound correctly is itself a form of cultural engagement — learning to say *Cillian* correctly is an act of respect for the name's origins.

## Irish Names Across the Diaspora

### Migration Patterns and Naming Evolution

The Irish diaspora, shaped by famine, political upheaval, and economic opportunity, created some of the most extensive naming migrations in human history. Between 1845 and 1855, over one million Irish people emigrated, primarily to North America and Australia. The names they carried — *Patrick*, *Mary*, *Michael*, *Brigid* — became foundational to naming patterns in their new countries. The Irish influence on American naming is immeasurable: *Patrick* is consistently in the top 50 American boys' names; *Mary* derivatives (*Mary*, *Marie*, *Maria*, *Marilyn*) dominate the female top 100; *Michael* has been a top 10 name for decades.

In Argentina, Irish immigrants established communities in Buenos Aires and Santa Fe, where names like *Fitzgerald*, *Dolan*, and *Burke* persist in local records. In New Zealand, Irish convict and settler names — *O'Brien*, *Flynn*, *Murphy* — appear in colonial records from the 1840s onward. Each diaspora community preserved certain names while adapting others, creating regional variations that persist today. The Irish name *Seamus* (James), for example, is common in Ireland and among Irish-Americans but rare in Irish-Australian communities, where *Jimmy* or *Jamie* were preferred.

### Contemporary Diaspora Naming Trends

Modern Irish diaspora communities are experiencing a naming renaissance. Irish-Americans, who number over 35 million according to the 2020 Census, are increasingly choosing authentically Irish names rather than Anglicized substitutes. The shift from *Patrick* to *Pádraig*, from *Michael* to *Mícheál*, from *Katherine* to *Caitríona* reflects a broader cultural reclamation. This trend is visible in SSA data: *Saoirse* rose from 2,000 births in 2010 to over 1,500 in 2024; *Niamh* rose from near-zero to over 800; *Ciara* (KEE-rah) has consistently ranked in the top 200 since 2015.

Irish-Canadians, Irish-Australians, and Irish-British communities show similar patterns. The UK's Irish population (estimated at 1.5 million) has influenced British naming: *Liam* entered the UK top 10 in 2017 and has remained there; *Saoirse* appeared in the UK top 500 by 2020. These trends demonstrate that Irish names have achieved a global status that few national naming traditions can match — a status earned through cultural resilience rather than economic power.

## Making an Irish Name Your Own

### Balancing Tradition and Modernity

Choosing an Irish name requires navigating the tension between cultural authenticity and practical usability. A name like *Fionnghuala* (FIN-goo-la) is authentically Irish but presents significant pronunciation and spelling challenges. A name like *Liam* is equally Irish but functions seamlessly in any English-speaking context. The most successful Irish names occupy a middle ground: they carry cultural significance while remaining accessible to diverse social environments.

The concept of *cainnt* (speech/language) in Irish naming culture suggests that a name's value lies not only in its meaning but in how it is spoken. A name that is mispronounced repeatedly may lose its cultural power, while a name that is learned and respected becomes a bridge between cultures. Parents should consider whether they want a name that signals Irish heritage prominently or one that carries Irish roots more subtly. Both approaches are valid; the choice depends on family priorities.

### The Role of Family and Community

Irish naming tradition has always involved community input. The godparent (*aumna*) played a significant role in name selection, often choosing a name for the child that honored their own family or religious patron. This tradition persists in modified form: Irish families often choose names that honor grandparents, godparents, or community figures. The modern Irish naming practice of combining an Irish first name with an English middle name — *Liam James*, *Saoirse Elizabeth*, *Cillian Thomas* — reflects this hybrid tradition.

The *Ainm* (name) in Irish culture carries the weight of identity. To know someone's name is to know something essential about them. This cultural understanding informs the Irish approach to naming: names are chosen with seriousness, not fashion. The current trend toward Irish names in English-speaking countries represents a genuine appreciation for this tradition, not merely an aesthetic preference. Families choosing Irish names should engage with the cultural context that gives those names their meaning.

## Meaning & Etymology

### Deep Dives into Name Origins

**Liam** derives from the Germanic *Wilhelm*, entered Irish as *Uilliam* in the medieval period, and was shortened to *Liam* as a familiar form. The name's rise to global prominence reflects the broader revaluation of Irish culture since the 1990s. **Saoirse** combines the Old Irish *sáer* (free) with the feminine suffix *-sa*, creating a name that means "freedom" — a concept that has political and cultural significance beyond its linguistic roots.

**Niamh** comes from the Old Irish *níam* (brightness, radiance), associated with the Otherworld figure in Irish mythology. The name's orthography — with the *mh* producing a "v" sound and the síneadh fada marking the long *i* — makes it one of the most challenging Irish names for non-Irish speakers. **Ciara** derives from *ciar* (dark, black) plus the feminine suffix *-a*, meaning "dark-haired" — a descriptor that was once a common physical characteristic reference in Irish naming.

**Orla** combines *órl* (gold) with the feminine suffix *-a*, meaning "golden princess." The longer form *Orlaith* adds the diminutive *-ith*, creating a more elaborate version that was popular in the medieval period. **Deirdre** comes from the Old Irish *derd* (anger) plus the feminine suffix *-e*, referring to the tragic heroine of the Ulster Cycle. The name's association with tragedy has not prevented its continued use, demonstrating the Irish acceptance of complex emotional associations in nomenclature.

## Origin & Cultural Context

### The Irish Naming Tradition

Irish naming patterns have been shaped by three major forces: the Gaelic clan system, the Catholic Church, and the English colonial imposition. The clan system produced patronymic surnames (*Ó* meaning "grandson of," *Mac* meaning "son of") that became the foundation of Irish identity. The Catholic Church introduced saint names that competed with and eventually superseded many pagan names. English colonial policy actively suppressed Irish names and language, creating a naming culture that existed in tension with official authority.

The nineteenth-century famine migrations scattered Irish naming patterns across the English-speaking world. Irish names in America, Australia, and Canada carry the imprint of this displacement — they are both authentically Irish and shaped by diaspora experience. The twentieth-century Gaelic revival, led by figures like Douglas Hyde and W.B. Yeats, created a conscious reclamation of Irish names as part of cultural nationalism. This revival continues today, with parents choosing Irish names as acts of cultural affirmation.

### Regional and Class Variations

Naming patterns varied significantly across Ireland's regions and social classes. The Anglo-Norman aristocracy in the Pale (around Dublin) used names like *Fitzgerald*, *Butler*, and *Burke* — names that reflected their French and English origins. The Gaelic nobility in the west and south used traditional Irish names like *O'Brien*, *O'Neill*, and *O'Connor*. The peasant class, particularly in the Gaeltacht regions, used the most traditionally Irish names, preserving forms that the urban elite had abandoned.

These class and regional distinctions persist in modern naming patterns. The Gaeltacht regions (Connemara, Dingle, Donegal) show the highest retention of traditional Irish names, while urban centers like Dublin and Cork show more Anglicized patterns. The diaspora communities in North America and the UK show a hybrid pattern — Irish names used alongside English and American names, creating a distinctive onomastic culture that is both Irish and cosmopolitan.

## Pronunciation Guide

### Mastering Irish Phonology

Irish orthography follows consistent phonological rules that, once learned, make pronunciation straightforward. The key principles:

- **Vowel pairs** (*ai, ae, ao, ea, ee, ei, eo, eu, ia, io, ia, ua, uo, ui*) determine the "back" or "forward" quality of adjacent consonants
- **Lenition** (*h* after a consonant) softens the consonant: *ch* becomes a guttural sound, *ph* becomes "f"
- **Silent letters**: *fh* is always silent; *dh* and *gh* between vowels are silent
- ** síneadh fada** (long mark) lengthens the vowel: *a* becomes "ah," *e* becomes "ay," *i* becomes "ee," *o* becomes "oh," *u* becomes "oo"

**Common pronunciation patterns:**

| Irish Spelling | Sound | Example |
|---------------|-------|---------|
| *bh, mh* | v | *Maeve* = MAYV |
| *ch* | guttural h | *Cillian* = KIL-ee-an |
| *dh, gh* | silent between vowels | *Aidan* = AY-dən |
| *fh* | silent | *Aoife* = EE-fa |
| *ll, nn* | clear L/N | *Liam* = LEE-am |
| *rr* | rolled R | *Ruairí* = ROO-ree |

The most challenging names for non-Irish speakers are those with multiple lenited consonants (*Fionnghuala*, *Meadhbh*) and those with silent combinations (*Niamh*, *Aoife*). Parents should practice pronunciation with native speakers or audio resources before committing to a name that requires frequent correction.

A: *Saoirse* means "freedom" in Irish. It is derived from the Old Irish *sáer* (free) plus the feminine suffix *-sa*.
