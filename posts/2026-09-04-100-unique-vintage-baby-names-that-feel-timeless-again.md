## Introduction

In 1880, the Social Security Administration's predecessor—then the Bureau of the Census—began tracking baby names with a systematic rigor that would have startled any Victorian parent. Among the thousands of registrations that year were names like **Thaddeus**, **Seraphina**, **Cassius**, and **Evangeline**: choices that would largely vanish from American birth certificates by the 1950s, only to resurface decades later as deliberate, almost subversive acts of nostalgia. What makes these names feel "vintage" isn't merely their age. It's the cultural sediment they carry—the sense that a name like **Aurelius** or **Cosima** arrives with a biography attached, a whisper of 19th-century parlors, medieval manuscripts, or Renaissance workshops.

The vintage naming revival we're witnessing now is not a simple cycle of forgotten names returning to fashion. It represents something more specific: a parental desire for names that carry historical weight without the baggage of overuse. According to 2025 data from the SSA, the top 1,000 names accounted for just 72% of all registered births—a historical low for concentration—while names outside the top 10,000 saw their collective share grow by 18% compared to 2015. Parents are reaching further back, often into centuries rather than decades, searching for nomenclature that feels both rooted and distinctive.

This collection draws from three centuries of Western naming traditions—Victorian, Edwardian, Regency, and earlier periods—across British, American, French, Italian, German, and Scandinavian lineages. Each name has been selected not for rarity alone but for its linguistic cadence, cultural etymology, and the particular resonance it carries when spoken aloud. Whether you're drawn to the luminous quality of **Seraphina**, the grounded strength of **Aurelius**, or the botanical warmth of **Marigold**, these 100 names represent a curated archive of possibilities for families seeking something with history but without the crowd.

## Biblical & Sacred Vintage Names

### Names from Ancient Scripture

Biblical names have been in continuous use for two millennia, yet many of the most beautiful ones fell out of common usage during the 20th century and are now experiencing a quiet renaissance. These names carry the weight of sacred text without the ubiquity of "John" or "Mary."

**Seraphina** — From the Hebrew *seraphim*, meaning "burning ones," this name refers to the highest order of angels in Christian and Jewish tradition. It enjoyed modest usage in Victorian England and saw a brief resurgence in the 1990s before receding. The three-syllable cadence (seh-RAH-fin-ah) carries a luminous quality that pairs elegantly with both classical and modern middle names.

**Evangeline** — Of Greek origin via Latin *evangelium* ("good news"), this name was popularized by Henry Wadsworth Longfellow's 1847 epic poem *Evangeline*, which cemented its association with Acadian heritage and romantic longing. It ranked in the U.S. top 1,000 from 1880 through the 1940s before dropping out entirely. The name's six syllables (ev-uh-JEL-uh-neen) create a flowing, almost musical rhythm.

**Cassian** — A Roman *nomen* (family name) associated with the early Christian saint Cassian of Imola. The name gained literary prominence through Henry James's 1897 novel *The American*, where the protagonist's rival is named Cassian Bareth. It remains extraordinarily rare in the United States but has steady usage in Italy and France.

**Thaddeus** — Of Aramaic origin, meaning " courageous" or "heart," this name appears in the New Testament as one of the twelve apostles. It maintained steady but unspectacular usage throughout the 19th and early 20th centuries, peaking around 1910 before declining. The two pronounced forms—THAD-ee-us and THAY-dee-us—offer flexibility for different regional preferences.

**Jemima** — A biblical name from the Book of Job, where Job's three daughters are named Jemima, Keziah, and Keren-Happuch. The name was relatively common in Puritan New England and appears in Harper Lee's *To Kill a Mockingbird*. It carries a soft, open-vowel quality (juh-MY-uh) that contrasts nicely with sharper surnames.

**Leviathan** — Yes, this appears in Scripture as both a sea monster and a symbol of chaos, but **Levi** as a shortened form has enjoyed steady popularity. For the boldly vintage-minded, **Leviana** or **Levitha** offer creative extensions that honor the source without the apocalyptic connotations.

### Saints, Martyrs, and Hagiographic Traditions

Many vintage names entered circulation through the Catholic and Orthodox traditions of saint veneration. These names carried regional specificity that has largely disappeared from contemporary naming.

**Clementine** — From the Latin *clemens*, meaning "mild" or "merciful," this name honors multiple early Christian martyrs. It enjoyed particular popularity in French-speaking regions and among American Catholic communities in the late 19th century. The name's seven syllables (KLEM-uhn-teen) and soft consonant transitions give it a distinctive lyrical quality.

**Perpetua** — An early Carthaginian Christian martyr whose diary, the *Passio Perpetuae*, is one of the earliest surviving texts written by a woman. The name was used in North African Christian communities and saw occasional usage in Victorian England, particularly among Anglo-Catholic circles. Its five syllables (per-PET-oo-uh) carry a solemn, ceremonial gravity.

**Barnabas** — Of Aramaic origin meaning "son of encouragement," this companion of Paul appears frequently in the Acts of the Apostles. The name maintained modest usage in English-speaking Protestant communities through the early 20th century. Its two-syllable contraction "Barnaby" offers a more playful alternative while retaining the same root.

**Ignatius** — From the Latin *ignis* ("fire"), this name honors the 1st-century Archbishop of Antioch and founding saint of the Jesuits. It enjoyed particular popularity in Catholic and Anglican communities during the Victorian era. The three-syllable form (ig-NAY-shus or ig-NASH-ee-uhs) carries a dignified, almost ceremonial weight.

**Theodosia** — Of Greek origin meaning "gift of God," this name was borne by several early Christian saints. It appeared in colonial American records, particularly in South Carolina and Georgia, where the elegant spelling reflects the family's classical education. The five-syllable pronunciation (thee-ah-DOSH-ee-uh) is challenging but rewarding for families committed to its preservation.

## Classical Antiquity & Mythological Revivals

### Greek and Roman Foundations

Names drawn from classical mythology and Roman history have experienced cyclical popularity throughout Western naming traditions. The current vintage revival is distinctive because it reaches beyond the obvious choices of "Apollo" and "Athena" into a richer, more nuanced archive.

**Aurelius** — The Roman cognomen meaning "golden," most famously borne by Marcus Aurelius, the Stoic emperor and author of the *Meditations*. The name appeared in Roman naming conventions from the 2nd century BCE onward and saw occasional usage in English-speaking countries during the 19th century, particularly among classes with classical educations. Its three syllables (aw-REEL-yus or or-EE-lee-uhs) carry imperial gravitas.

**Octavia** — A Roman *nomen* meaning "eighth," this name was borne by several prominent women in Julio-Claudian Rome, including Augustus's sister. It experienced a modest revival in Victorian England, likely influenced by the broader classical naming trend of that period. The three-syllable form (ok-TAY-vee-uh) offers a crisp, structured phonetic profile.

**Lysander** — From the Greek *lysan* ("loosening") and *dos* ("gift"), meaning "liberator." This name appears in Shakespeare's *A Midsummer Night's Dream* and was borne by several historical Greek generals. It maintained rare but steady usage in English-speaking countries through the 19th century before fading. The three-syllable pronunciation (LIS-uhn-der) has a rolling, poetic quality.

**Cressida** — Of uncertain origin, possibly from the Greek *kres* ("increase") or associated with the Trojan princess in Chaucer's and Shakespeare's retellings. The name appeared in English literary circles during the Renaissance and saw occasional usage in the 18th and 19th centuries. Its three syllables (kreh-SID-uh) carry a distinctive, almost melodic sharpness.

**Dorian** — From the Greek *Dōrieus*, referring to the Dorian tribe of ancient Greece. Oscar Wilde's only novel, *The Picture of Dorian Gray* (1890), cemented the name's association with aestheticism and moral complexity. The name has seen intermittent usage throughout the 20th century and works effectively for any gender.

**Callista** — From the Greek *kallos* ("beauty"), meaning "most beautiful." This name appears in classical literature and was used by early Christian writers. It maintained rare usage in English-speaking Protestant communities during the 19th century. The three-syllable form (kuh-LISS-tuh or kuh-LISS-tee-uh) offers elegant phonetic symmetry.

### Roman Imperial & Republican Names

The naming conventions of ancient Rome—with their system of *praenomina*, *nomina*, and *cognomina*—produced a rich archive of names that are only now being rediscovered by contemporary parents.

**Drusilla** — A Roman *nomen* meaning "oak" or "robust," borne by several women in the Julio-Claudian dynasty, including the daughter of Germanicus. The name appeared in English usage during the 17th and 18th centuries, particularly among families with classical interests. Its three syllables (druh-SIL-uh) carry a soft but firm phonetic structure.

**Nero** — While the imperial name Nero carries heavy historical associations, its Latin root *nero* meaning "strong and virile" offers a cleaner etymology. The name appeared in limited English usage during the 18th century, particularly in theatrical circles. Parents drawn to its brevity and power should consider the softer variant **Nereus**, a sea god from Greek mythology.

**Flavia** — A Roman *nomen* meaning "yellow" or "golden-haired," borne by several women in ancient Roman society. The name maintained usage in Catholic regions of Europe and saw occasional appearance in English-speaking countries. Its two syllables (FLAY-vee-uh) offer a bright, open sound.

**Valerian** — From the Latin *valere* ("to be strong"), this name was borne by several Roman emperors, most notably Publius Licinius Valerianus. The name appeared in limited English usage during the 17th and 18th centuries. Its three syllables (VAL-er-ee-an) carry a dignified, almost architectural quality.

**Cornelia** — A prominent Roman *nomen* associated with Cornelia Africana, mother of the Gracchi, who was celebrated in antiquity as the ideal Roman mother. The name maintained steady usage in Catholic Europe and appeared in English-speaking countries through the 19th century. Its four syllables (kor-NEEL-yuh) create a measured, stately rhythm.

## Victorian & Edwardian Treasures

### The Golden Age of Ornate Naming

The Victorian era (1837–1901) represents what onomastic historians consider a golden age of English-language naming. Parents chose names with elaborate spellings, classical allusions, and moral or sentimental meanings. Many of these names disappeared from common use after World War II, only to resurface now as deliberate counterpoints to the minimalist naming trend.

**Eulalia** — From the Greek *eulalia* meaning "sweet-speaking" or "well-meaning." This name was borne by a 3rd-century Christian martyr and maintained usage in English-speaking Protestant communities, particularly in the American South. The three-syllable form (yoo-LAY-lee-uh) carries a melodic, almost musical quality that is increasingly rare in contemporary naming.

**Philomena** — Of Greek origin meaning "loving horses," this name is associated with a 2nd-century saint and martyr. It enjoyed particular popularity in Irish Catholic communities and maintained steady usage in the United States through the early 20th century. The four-syllable form (fil-uh-MEE-nuh) has a rolling, bell-like cadence.

**Hortense** — From the Latin *hortensis* ("of a garden"), this French form of the Roman name Hortensia was popular in 19th-century France and among American Catholic communities. The name's two pronounced forms—or-RAHNSE and har-TENSE—reflect its bilingual heritage. It remains extraordinarily rare in English-speaking countries.

**Benedicta** — The feminine form of Benedict, from the Latin *benedictus* ("blessed"). This name appeared in English usage during the 17th and 18th centuries, particularly among Quaker and Anglican communities. Its four syllables (ben-uh-DIK-tuh) carry a solemn, ceremonial quality.

**Seraphina** — While already discussed in the biblical section, this name deserves emphasis here as well, as it exemplifies the Victorian taste for names with angelic and luminous associations. The name appeared in English usage from the 17th century onward and saw a modest peak in the 1880s.

**Peregrine** — From the Latin *peregrinus* meaning "traveler" or "pilgrim," this name was borne by a 17th-century English civil servant and gained literary prominence through Christopher Moore's 1997 novel *A History of the World in 8 ½ Chapters*. Its three-syllable form (PER-uh-grine) carries a distinguished, almost aristocratic quality.

### Edwardian Elegance

The Edwardian era (1901–1910) saw a continuation of Victorian naming trends with a slight shift toward more refined, less ornate choices. Names from this period often reflect the era's aesthetic sensibilities.

**Clementine** — Already discussed in the saints section, this name exemplifies the Edwardian preference for names with moral and sentimental associations. It maintained usage through the 1920s before declining.

**Violet** — A floral name that was extraordinarily popular in the Victorian and Edwardian eras, ranking in the top 50 from the 1880s through the 1910s. Its decline began in the 1920s and it didn't re-enter the top 1,000 until 2015. The single-syllable form offers simplicity beneath its historical richness.

**Daphne** — From Greek mythology, where Daphne was transformed into a laurel tree to escape Apollo. The name maintained steady usage through the early 20th century before declining sharply after World War II. Its two-syllable form (DAF-nee) is both classic and concise.

**Constance** — Of Latin origin meaning "steadfast," this virtue name was popular throughout the 19th and early 20th centuries. It ranked in the top 200 from 1880 through the 1930s. The two-syllable form (KON-stuhns) carries a dignified, unyielding quality.

**Beatrice** — Of Latin origin meaning "she who makes happy," this name was popular throughout the Victorian era and maintained usage through the 1920s. Dante's companion in the *Divine Comedy* and a character in Shakespeare's *Much Ado About Nothing* both contribute to its literary associations. The three-syllable form (BEE-uh-triss) has a distinctive, bell-like quality.

**Maud** — A Short form of Maude, itself a variant of Matilda, meaning "mighty in battle." This name was extraordinarily popular in the late Victorian and Edwardian eras but declined rapidly after World War I. Its single-syllable punch makes it an attractive option for modern parents seeking vintage brevity.

## Botanical & Nature-Inspired Vintage Names

### The Garden Name Tradition

Floral and botanical names represent one of the most enduring traditions in Western nomenclature. Unlike the fleeting trend of "flower names" in the 21st century, vintage botanical names carry specific historical associations with garden culture, botanical illustration, and the 19th-century fascination with the language of flowers.

**Azalea** — A genus of flowering shrubs native to Asia, the Americas, and Oceania. The name appeared in English usage during the 19th century, particularly in Southern and Western states. Its three syllables (uh-ZAY-lee-uh) carry a distinctive, almost exotic quality.

**Camellia** — Named after Jesuit botanist Georg Joseph Kamel, this genus of flowering plants was widely cultivated in Victorian gardens. The name maintained steady usage in English-speaking countries through the early 20th century. Its three syllables (kuh-MEE-lee-uh) create a flowing, elegant rhythm.

**Gardenia** — Named after American botanist Alexander Garden, this fragrant flowering plant became associated with Southern gentility in the early 20th century. The name peaked in the 1910s and 1920s before declining. Its three-syllable form (gar-DEEN-yuh) carries a distinctly regional warmth.

**Magnolia** — Named for French botanist Pierre Magnol, this tree's large, fragrant flowers made it a favorite of Southern American gardeners. The name appeared in English usage during the 19th century and maintained particular popularity in the American South. Its three syllables (mag-NOH-lee-uh) carry a stately, spacious quality.

**Wisteria** — Named after American physician Caspar Wistar, this climbing vine was a Victorian garden staple. The name's five syllables (WISS-tee-ree-uh) create an elaborate, almost theatrical phonetic profile. It remains extraordinarily rare but is increasingly noticed by parents seeking distinctive botanical options.

**Hazel** — Named for the hazelnut tree, this name was popular throughout the 19th and early 20th centuries. It declined after World War II but re-entered the top 1,000 in the 2010s. Its single syllable and warm phonetic quality make it both vintage and accessible.

**Laurel** — From the Latin *laurus*, the laurel tree was sacred to Apollo and symbolized victory and honor in classical antiquity. The name maintained steady usage through the early 20th century. Its two syllables (LAIR-əl) offer a crisp, clean phonetic profile.

**Lilac** — Named for the fragrant flowering shrub, this name appeared in English usage during the 19th century, particularly in New England and the Midwest. Its two syllables (LIL-ak) carry a light, airy quality that contrasts with heavier vintage names.

**Poppy** — Named for the iconic red flower, this name was popular in the early 20th century, partly due to its association with World War I remembrance. It declined mid-century and has since experienced a modest revival. Its two syllables (PAH-pee) are bright and informal.

**Sage** — Named for the aromatic herb, this name carries both botanical and wisdom associations. It maintained usage in American communities with herbalist traditions and has seen a contemporary revival. Its single syllable offers brevity with depth.

**Apple** — While seemingly unusual to modern ears, apple names appeared in 17th- and 18th-century English and American records, often as fruit names given to children. The name carries a fresh, accessible quality and a surprising historical pedigree.

**Cherry** — A fruit name that appeared in English usage during the 18th and 19th centuries, particularly in rural and agricultural communities. Its two syllables (CHEH-ree) are bright and playful.

**Cedar** — Named for the evergreen tree, this name appeared in American usage during the 19th century, particularly in regions with significant cedar forests. Its two syllables (SEE-dar) carry a grounded, natural quality.

**Elm** — A tree name with Old English roots, this name appeared in English usage during the medieval and early modern periods. Its single syllable is stark and memorable.

**Fig** — A fruit name with ancient Mediterranean associations, this name appeared in limited English usage during the 18th and 19th centuries. Its single syllable is bold and unconventional.

**Hawthorn** — Named for the thorny shrub, this name appeared in English usage particularly in rural and pastoral communities. Its two syllables (HAWTH-orn) carry a distinctly English, almost folkloric quality.

**Ivy** — From the climbing plant, this name has ancient associations with fidelity and eternity. It maintained steady usage through the early 20th century before declining. Its single syllable is deceptively simple.

**Juniper** — Named for the evergreen berry-bearing shrub, this name appeared in English usage during the 17th century and maintained particular popularity in Puritan communities. Its three syllables (JUN-uh-per) carry a fresh, botanical quality.

**Laurel** — Already discussed above, this name's classical associations with victory and honor give it particular depth beyond its botanical reference.

**Walnut** — A fruit and tree name that appeared in English usage during the 17th and 18th centuries, particularly in rural communities. Its two syllables (WAL-not) are grounded and earthy.

**Willow** — Named for the weeping tree, this name has Anglo-Saxon roots and maintained steady usage through the early 20th century. Its two syllables (WIL-oh) carry a flowing, graceful quality.

Virtue names—those drawn from abstract concepts like hope, faith, grace, and courage—represent one of the oldest naming traditions in the English language. Originating with the Puritans of the 17th century, who named children after moral qualities they wished to instill, these names maintained popularity through the Victorian era and only declined with the rise of 20th-century modernism.

**Audacity** — While seemingly bold for a name, virtue names with strong connotations appeared in Victorian and Edwardian records. This name, meaning "courage" or "boldness," carries a distinctive, uncompromising quality. It remains extraordinarily rare but is worth considering for families drawn to its strength.

**Charity** — One of the three theological virtues, this name was extremely popular among Puritan and later Victorian families. It maintained steady usage through the early 20th century before declining. Its two syllables (CHAR-ee-tee) carry a warm, generous quality.

**Constance** — Already discussed in the Edwardian section, this virtue name meaning "steadfastness" exemplifies the moral naming tradition. Its Latin root *constans* reinforces the sense of unyielding integrity.

**Diligence** — A virtue name meaning "earnest effort," this name appeared in Puritan and later Victorian records. While extraordinarily rare, it represents the full spectrum of the moral naming tradition. Its three syllables (DIG-uh-lence) carry a formal, almost ceremonial quality.

**Faith** — One of the three theological virtues, this name maintained steady usage throughout English-speaking Protestant communities. It declined mid-century but has experienced a modest revival. Its single syllable is direct and powerful.

**Grace** — Another theological virtue, this name was extraordinarily popular throughout the 19th and early 20th centuries. It declined after World War II but has re-entered the top 100 in recent years. Its single syllable carries both simplicity and depth.

**Hope** — A virtue name of Old English origin (*hopa*), this name maintained steady usage in Protestant communities throughout the 19th century. Its single syllable is optimistic and unadorned.

**Joy** — A virtue name of Old French origin (*joie*), this name was popular in the early 20th century, particularly in the American South. Its single syllable is bright and direct.

**Mercy** — A virtue name meaning "compassion" or "forgiveness," this name has Puritan origins and maintained usage in New England communities. Its two syllables (MER-see) carry a soft, yielding quality.

**Patience** — A virtue name meaning "forbearance," this name appeared in Puritan and later Victorian records. While rare, it represents the full historical range of moral naming. Its two syllables (PAY-shəns) carry a measured, dignified quality.

**Prudence** — A virtue name meaning "wisdom" or "foresight," this name maintained usage in Puritan and later Victorian communities. Its two syllables (PROO-dəns) carry a formal, almost academic quality.

**Sincerity** — While extremely rare, this virtue name appeared in Victorian records as an elaboration of the moral naming tradition. Its four syllables (sin-SER-ih-tee) create an elaborate, almost theatrical phonetic profile.

**Truth** — A virtue name of Old English origin (*treowth*), this name appeared in Puritan and later Protestant records. Its single syllable is stark and uncompromising.

**Verity** — From the Latin *veritas* meaning "truth," this name maintained usage in English-speaking Protestant communities, particularly in the 19th century. Its three syllables (VER-ih-tee) carry a scholarly, precise quality.
