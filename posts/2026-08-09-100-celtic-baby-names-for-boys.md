## Introduction

The Ogham inscriptions carved into standing stones across Ireland and western Britain, dating to roughly the 4th century CE, represent the earliest surviving record of a naming tradition that would echo through a millennium and a half. Those angular marks—still decipherable by specialists—bear names like *Cormac*, *Conall*, and *Eogan*, each carrying a weight of lineage that went far beyond mere identification. In Celtic societies, a name was a claim to ancestry, a declaration of tribal belonging, and often a prayer for the child's future. To name a boy was to inscribe him into a living chain stretching back to mythic kings and warlords.

That tradition has not died. It has merely been suppressed, translated, and reshaped. The Norman conquests, the Anglicization policies of the 17th and 18th centuries, and the Great Famine's linguistic devastation drove countless Gaelic names into hiding—or into anglicized forms that stripped them of their original phonetic architecture. A *Seán* became a *John*. A *Domhnall* was flattened into a *Donald*. But the underlying linguistic DNA persisted, and across the Atlantic, in Australia, and in the urban centers of modern Europe, a quiet reclamation has been underway since the 1990s.

What makes Celtic male names particularly resonant in 2026 is their structural diversity. The Brythonic branch—Welsh, Cornish, Breton—offers hard consonant clusters and distinctive phonemes like the Welsh *ll* and the gutteral *ch* found in Breton. The Goidelic branch—Irish and Scottish Gaelic—provides a softer, vowel-forward cadence with its slender consonants and broad consonants (*leathanaigh* and *caol*). Together, these two branches produce a naming landscape of extraordinary variety: names that feel ancient without feeling archaic, strong without being aggressive, distinctive without being difficult to pronounce.

This collection examines 100 names drawn from across the full Celtic linguistic spectrum, organized for parents who want more than a list—they want context, etymology, pronunciation guidance, and the kind of onomastic analysis that comes from understanding why certain names carry certain energies.

## Why Celtic Names Are Resurging

### The Data Behind the Trend

Celtic baby name registrations in the United States increased by 47% between 2018 and 2025, according to Social Security Administration data cross-referenced with naming database aggregators. Irish names led the growth at +52%, followed by Welsh names at +38% and Scottish Gaelic names at +41%. Notably, names that had been declining since the 1960s—such as *Cian*, *Declan*, and *Rhys*—reversed trajectory entirely, with *Cian* climbing from rank #587 in 2015 to #214 in 2025.

This isn't a superficial trend. The names driving this growth share specific phonetic and semantic characteristics: they tend to be two syllables, they carry transparent meanings (often nature or warrior-related), and they resist easy categorization into existing naming "tribes." A parent choosing *Bran* isn't selecting a name that sounds like every other trending name—*Liam*, *Noah*, *Oliver*—but isn't reaching so far into obscurity that the child faces constant correction.

### The Celebrity and Media Effect

The 2023–2025 period saw a cluster of high-profile name introductions that accelerated Celtic adoption. Actor Cillian Murphy's sustained visibility, combined with the cultural phenomenon of *Peaky Blinders* (which featured multiple Celtic-named characters), drove a measurable spike in *Cillian* registrations—up 63% in the UK and 41% in the US between 2019 and 2022. Welsh names benefited from the global success of *Mythic Quest* and the continued cultural footprint of *Game of Thrones*, whose creator George R.R. Martin drew heavily from Celtic mythology for character naming.

Perhaps more significantly, the rise of Celtic-inspired fantasy literature and media has normalized these names for a generation of parents who grew up consuming that content. Unlike the mythological-name trend of the 1990s (which peaked with *Thor* and *Loki* and then collapsed), the Celtic resurgence is anchored in real linguistic traditions, not fictional constructs.

### The Identity and Heritage Factor

For families with Celtic ancestry, these names serve a different function than they do for parents with no particular heritage connection. In a 2024 survey of 2,400 new parents conducted by the Naming Institute, 68% of respondents with Irish or Scottish heritage cited " reconnecting with family roots" as a primary motivation for choosing a Celtic name. Among parents without Celtic heritage, the top motivations were "unique sound" (71%) and "meaningful etymology" (58%).

This dual appeal—heritage reclamation for some, aesthetic and semantic preference for others—explains why the trend has broader staying power than typical naming fads. It's not driven by a single demographic or a single cultural moment.

## The Celtic Naming Tradition: A Living History

### From Ogham to Anglicization

The earliest Celtic names survive in three primary sources: Ogham inscriptions on stone monuments, Latin and Greek accounts of Celtic tribes (notably Julius Caesar's *Commentarii de Bello Gallico* and Ptolemy's *Geography*), and later medieval Irish manuscripts such as the *Lebor Gabála Érenn* and the *Annals of the Four Masters*. These sources reveal a naming system built on patronymics, descriptive epithets, and divine associations.

A 9th-century Irish manuscript records the name *Ailill* with the epithet *Mánet* ("the Handsome"), suggesting that personal names were often augmented with descriptive elements that functioned almost like surnames. The famous Irish king *Niall* carries the epithet *Noígíallach* ("of the Nine Hostages"), a name that functions as both personal identifier and historical record.

The Anglicization period (16th–19th centuries) represents the most traumatic disruption to Celtic onomastic traditions. The Statutes of Ireland (1541–1695) explicitly discouraged the use of Gaelic names, and the establishment of the National School system in 1831 enforced English-language instruction that penalized Gaelic speech. Children were literally beaten for speaking Irish in school—a policy that accelerated the shift from *Seán* to *John*, *Pádraig* to *Patrick*, *Séamus* to *James*.

### The Mythological Substrate

Many Celtic names preserve traces of pre-Christian mythology. *Aengus* derives from the god *Aonghus* (Angus), whose name likely means "one choice" or "unique strength." *Bran* means "raven" and connects to the mythological figure Bran mac Febal, a giant whose decapitated head continues to speak after death—a motif that appears in the *Mabinogion* and the Irish *Branch* cycles. *Llew* (or *Lluellyn*) derives from the Welsh god *Lleu Llaw Gyffes*, whose name means "the bright-faced one" or "the skillful-handed one."

These mythological connections don't require parents to adopt a pagan worldview. They do, however, add a layer of cultural depth that most modern names lack. When you name a child *Cernunnos*, you're invoking the horned god of Celtic polytheism—a name that carries theological weight. When you name him *Cian*, you're invoking a figure from the *Táin Bó Cúailnge* whose very name (meaning "ancient" or "enduring") becomes a narrative device in the epic.

### Regional Variations Across the Celtic World

The six Celtic nations each contribute distinct naming traditions:

- **Ireland (Gaeilge):** The largest reservoir of Celtic names, with a written tradition dating to the 5th century. Irish names tend toward the soft, vowel-forward sounds characteristic of Goidelic phonology.
- **Scotland (Gàidhlig):** Scottish Gaelic names share etymological roots with Irish names but have undergone distinct phonological developments. *Domhnall* (Irish: *Domhnall*) becomes *Donald* in English; *Eòghann* (Irish: *Eoghan*) becomes *Ewan* or *Ewen*.
- **Wales (Cymraeg):** Welsh names feature the distinctive *dd* (voiced th), *ll* (voiceless lateral fricative), and *ch* (voiceless velar fricative) phonemes. Names like *Rhys*, *Gareth*, and *Owain* have achieved international recognition, while names like *Cynan*, *Llewellyn*, and *Tegid* remain more regionally concentrated.
- **Cornwall (Kernowek):** Revived Cornish names are a 20th-century phenomenon, drawn from medieval records and the *Buchedd Pyb* tradition. *Terry* (from *Trevelyan*), *Keziah*, and *Annedd* represent the modern revival effort.
- **Brittany (Brezhoneg):** Breton names share etymological roots with Welsh names but have developed distinct forms. *Yann* (equivalent to *John*), *Jark* (equivalent to *Jack*), and *Malo* are traditionally Breton.
- **Isle of Man (Gaelg):** Manx names are the rarest of the Celtic tradition, with only a handful surviving into modern usage. *Cronyn*, *Kione*, and *Mooinjer* reflect the island's unique linguistic position between Gaelic and Norse traditions.

## The 100 Celtic Names for Boys

### Irish (Gaelic) Names

**1. Aodh** (AY) — "fire," "fiery one." One of the most historically significant Irish names, borne by numerous High Kings of Ireland. The anglicized form *Hugh* derives from this name.

**2. Ailred** (AIL-rede) — "noble friend." An Anglo-Saxon name that entered Irish tradition through medieval monastic records.

**3. Ailill** (A-LIL) — A name shared by multiple figures in Irish mythology, including Ailill mac Máta, king of Connacht. The meaning is uncertain but may relate to "noble" or "exalted."

**4. Aonghus** (OW-us) — "one choice," "unique strength." The Irish form of *Angus*, derived from the Celtic god *Ogme*.

**5. Brian** (BRI-an) — "noble," "high," "virtue." Possibly derived from the Celtic word *brīwo* meaning "height" or "excellence." Borne by Brian Boru, the 11th-century High King who defeated the Vikings at Clontarf.

**6. Cian** (KEE-an) — "ancient," "enduring," "life." A name of enormous historical weight, borne by the father of Lugh in the *Táin* cycle. The god *Cian* was killed by his own son Lugh, a mythological narrative that echoes across Indo-European traditions.

**7. Cormac** (KOR-ak) — "son of a hound" or possibly "charioteer." Borne by Cormac mac Airt, the legendary High King of Ireland whose court at Tara is the setting for many Irish tales.

**8. Cillian** (KIL-ee-an) — "church," "war," or "strife." The meaning is debated; the name may derive from *Cill* (church) or from an older root meaning "battle." Saint Cillian was a 7th-century Irish missionary who preached in Bavaria.

**9. Declan** (DEK-lan) — "full of goodness" or possibly "the good one." The name of a 5th-century saint, though its etymology is uncertain—it may be pre-Christian in origin.

**10. Diarmuid** (DEER-mud) — "free from envy" or "faultless." A hero of the Fenian Cycle, Diarmuid is the tragic lover of Gráinne in the tale *Diarmuid and Gráinne*. The anglicized form *Dermot* is far more common in English-speaking countries.

**11. Donall** (DON-al) — "world ruler" or "world-powered." The Gaelic form of *Donald*, derived from *Domhnall* meaning "world victory."

**12. Eamon** (AY-mon) — "wealthy protector." The Irish form of *Edmund*, from the Old English *Eadmund* meaning "wealthy protector."

**13. Eoghan** (OH-an) — "born of the yew tree" or "youth." A name of enormous dynastic significance: the *Uí Einne* (O'Neill) and *Eóghanacht* clans traced their lineage to Eóghan, a son of Niall of the Nine Hostages. The anglicized forms *Eugene* and * Owen* both derive from this name.

**14. Fergal** (FAR-gal) — "man of vigor" or "fierce man." Derived from *fear* (man) and *gal* (valor). Borne by multiple Irish kings and saints.

**15. Fionn** (FIN) — "fair," "white," "bright." The most famous bearer is Fionn mac Cumhaill, the legendary hunter and leader of the Fianna. The anglicized form *Finch* derives from this name.

**16. Flann** (FLAN) — "red," "ruby." A name borne by several Irish kings, including Flann Sinna, the 10th-century High King who died at the Battle of Belach Mugna.

**17. Gearoid** (GAIR-oid) — " Spear." The Irish form of *Gerard*, from the Germanic *ger* (spear) and *hard* (brave).

**18. Gormlaith** (GORM-la) — "fair and gentle" or possibly "blue-flecked." Though traditionally a female name, it has been used for boys in certain regional traditions.

**19. Huw** (HYOO) — The Welsh form of *Hugh*, itself derived from the Irish *Aodh*. Demonstrates the cross-branch flow of names within the Celtic family.

**20. Iarla** (EAR-la) — "earl" or "count." A title-name that entered Irish onomastics through the Anglo-Norman period.

**21. Keelan** (KEE-lan) — "slender" or "fair." A modern Irish name that has gained popularity in the 21st century.

**22. Kieran** (KEER-an) — "little dark one" or "dark-haired." The most common anglicized form is *Ciaran*, derived from *ciar* meaning "dark" or "black." Saint Ciaran was one of the Twelve Apostles of Ireland.

**23. Lorcan** (LOR-k-an) — "little fierce one." The diminutive of *Lochar*, meaning "fierce" or "passionate." Borne by Saint Lorcan, the 12th-century Archbishop of Dublin.

**24. Niall** (NEEL) — "champion" or "cloud." The most significant dynastic name in Irish history: the Uí Néill dynasty, which dominated Irish politics for a millennium, traced its lineage to Niall Noígíallach.

**25. Oisin** (OS-heen) — "little deer" or "deer." The most famous literary bearer is Oisín, the legendary poet and warrior son of Fionn mac Cumhaill, who features in one of the most beloved tales of Irish mythology. The name's phonetic structure—ending in the slender *-ín* diminutive suffix—makes it distinctly Irish.

**26. Ronan** (RON-an) — "little seal." A name that combines a nature element with the diminutive suffix, typical of Irish naming patterns. Borne by several Irish saints.

**27. Rory** (RORE-ee) — "red king" or "king of red hair." Derived from *Ruairí*, the anglicized form of *Ruadhri*, from *ruadh* (red) and *ri* (king).

**28. Seamus** (SHAY-mus) — The Irish form of *James*, derived from the Hebrew *Ya'akov* via Latin *Iacomus*. One of the most common Irish names, with a pronunciation that is distinctly different from its English counterpart.

**29. Shane** (SHAYN) — The Irish form of *John*, derived from the Hebrew *Yochanan* ("God is gracious"). The anglicized spelling *Shane* became standard in the 20th century, though the traditional Irish spelling is *Seán*.

**30. Tadg** (TIG) — "poet" or "philosopher." A name of considerable historical prestige, borne by multiple Irish kings and saints. The anglicized form *Tadgh* is increasingly used to preserve the original spelling.

### Scottish (Gaelic) Names

**31. Alasdair** (AL-az-dar) — "Defender of the people." The Scottish Gaelic form of *Alexander*. The anglicized *Alasdair* is used in Scotland to distinguish the Gaelic pronunciation from the English *Alexander*.

**32. Angus** (ANG-gus) — The anglicized form of *Aonghus*. While now widely used across the English-speaking world, its Gaelic roots are unmistakable.

**33. Bran** (BRAN) — "raven." A name shared across the Celtic world, appearing in both Irish and Welsh traditions. In Irish mythology, Bran mac Febal is a giant whose head continues to speak after decapitation. In Welsh tradition, *Bran* appears in the *Mabinogion* as a legendary king of Britain.

**34. Callum** (KAL-um) — "dove." The Scottish Gaelic form of *Colm*, derived from the Latin * Columba* ("dove"). Saint Columba (Colum Cille) was the 6th-century monk who founded Iona Abbey and played a pivotal role in spreading Christianity throughout Scotland.

**35. Calum** (KAL-um) — The alternative spelling of *Callum*, used in both Scottish and Irish contexts.

**36. Colin** (KOL-in) — "pup" or "young dog." Possibly derived from the Celtic *Caelicus* or from the Gaelic *Coilean*. The name gained popularity in the English-speaking world in the 20th century.

**37. Connor** (KON-or) — "lover of hounds" or "wolf-lover." Derived from the Irish *Conchobhar*, meaning "hound lover" or "passionate about hounds." A name borne by Connor O'Brien, the last High King of Ireland before the Norman invasion.

**38. Declan** (DEK-lan) — Also used in Scottish Gaelic tradition, though more commonly associated with Irish names. The uncertainty of its etymology makes it a fascinating case study in onomastic transmission.

**39. Dugald** (DOUG-ald) — "blind one." The anglicized form of the Gaelic *Dòghall*, from *dubh* (dark/black) and *ghall* (stranger). A name with a darkly poetic etymology.

**40. Duncan** (DUHN-kən) — "dark chief" or "brown warrior." Derived from the Gaelic *Donnchadh*, from *donn* (brown/dark) and *cath* (war/battle). A name with royal associations: Duncan I was King of Scotland in the 11th century, the figure Shakespeare dramatized in *Macbeth*.

**41. Ewan** (YOO-an) — "born of the yew tree" or "youth." The Scottish Gaelic form of *Eòghann*, cognate with the Irish *Eoghan*. The yew tree, long-lived and evergreen, was associated with immortality in Celtic tradition.

**42. Finlay** (FIN-lay) — "fair warrior" or "beautiful warrior." Derived from the Old Norse *Finngál* through Gaelic *Fionnghal*, combining *fionn* (fair) and *gall* (stranger/Norseman). A name that reflects the Viking-Gaelic cultural fusion of medieval Scotland.

**43. Hamish** (HAY-mish) — The Scottish Gaelic form of *James*, derived from the Hebrew *Ya'akov* via the Irish *Seamus* and the Gaelic *Séamas*. A name that demonstrates the complex transmission chain of biblical names through Celtic languages.

**44. Iain** (AYN) — The Scottish Gaelic form of *John*. Pronounced differently from the Irish *Seán*—the Scottish version lacks the sh sound, reflecting the different phonological evolution of Scottish Gaelic.

**45. Lachlan** (LAK-lən) — "land of lakes" or "from the fjord." Derived from the Old Norse *Lóðlǫnd* through Gaelic *Lachlann*, referring to the Norse-controlled territories of Scotland and Ireland. A name that encodes the Viking history of the Scottish islands.

**46. Malcom** (MAL-kəm) — "devotee of Saint Colmcille." The anglicized form of the Gaelic *Maol Eòlaig*, meaning "devotee of Colmcille." Saint Columba (Colmcille) was the patron saint of Scotland.

**47. Murdoch** (MER-dok) — "sea lord" or "lord of the sea." Derived from the Gaelic *Murchadh*, from *muir* (sea) and *cath* (lord/battle). A name with strong maritime associations.

**48. Ranald** (RA-nald) — "ruler's advisor" or "counsel of the chief." The Gaelic form of *Ronald*, derived from the Old Norse *Rögnvaldr*. A name that reflects the Norse-Gaelic cultural synthesis of the Hebrides.

**49. Rodger** (ROJ-er) — The Scottish form of *Roger*, derived from the Old Norse *Hroðgeirr* ("famous spear"). The Scottish pronunciation and spelling distinguish it from the English variant.

**50. Rory** (RORE-ee) — Also used in Scottish tradition, though of Irish origin. The cross-border usage of Celtic names is a common feature of the tradition.

### Welsh Names

**51. Arwel** (AR-wel) — "great" or "prominent." A name borne by several Welsh princes, including Arwel ap Gruffydd, a 12th-century ruler of Deheubarth.

**52. Bleddyn** (BLEH-din) — "bear leader" or "wolf leader." A name from Welsh mythology, borne by Bleddyn ap Cynfyn, a 11th-century king of Gwynedd and Powys. The animal-related etymology connects to the Celtic tradition of animal totems and nickname-based naming.

**53. Branwen** (BRAN-ben) — Traditionally a female name meaning "white raven," but occasionally used for boys in modern Welsh naming practice. The name connects to the *Branwen ferch Llŷr*, a figure in the *Mabinogion*.

**54. Cadwallader** (kad-wal-AY-der) — "battle leader" or "leader of the host." Derived from the Welsh *Cadwallydder*, from *cad* (battle) and *gwallter* (leader). Cadwalladr ap Cadfan was a 7th-century king of Gwynedd, remembered in Welsh tradition as a legendary warrior.

**55. Caradog** (kar-AD-og) — "beloved" or "dear." A name borne by Caratacus, the British chieftain who resisted the Roman invasion and was captured but spared by Emperor Claudius. The Welsh form *Caradog* preserves the original Brythonic pronunciation.

**56. Ceredig** (ker-ED-ig) — Possibly derived from the Roman name *Ceretic*, borne by the 5th-century King of Alt Clut (Dumbarton Rock). The name connects Welsh royal lineage to the post-Roman British kingdoms.

**57. Cynan** (KIN-an) — "chief dog" or "leader." A name borne by multiple Welsh princes, including Cynan Dindaethwy, a 10th-century ruler of Anglesey. The *-an* suffix is a common Welsh diminutive or patronymic marker.

**58. Gethin** (GEH-thin) — "dark-skinned" or "swarthy." Derived from the Welsh *gwydd*, meaning "dark" or "black." A descriptive name that follows the Celtic tradition of epithet-based naming.

**59. Gwydion** (gwid-EE-on) — "born of trees" or "tree-born." A major figure in the *Mabinogion*, Gwydion is a magician and trickster who shapes the narrative of the Fourth Branch. The name's etymology—possibly from *gwydd* (tree) and the suffix *-ion*—connects to Celtic tree symbolism.

**60. Iolo** (EE-loh) — A diminutive of *Owain*, but used as an independent name. Made famous by Iolo Morganwg (1747–1826), the Welsh antiquarian and forger who invented the Gorsedd of the Bards. A name that carries the weight of Welsh cultural revival.

**61. Llewelyn** (LOO-uh-LIN) — "lion-like" or "lion chief." Derived from the Welsh *Llywelyn*, possibly from *llyw* (leader) and *lyn* (lion). Llywelyn ap Gruffudd, known as Llywelyn the Last, was the last native Prince of Wales before the English conquest. The name's phonetic complexity makes it distinctive.

**62. Madog** (MAH-dog) — "fortunate" or "blessed." A name borne by Madog ap Gruffydd Maelor, the 13th-century rebel who claimed to be the son of Gruffydd ap Llywelyn. The name has a bright, open vowel structure.

**63. Meilir** (MY-leer) — Possibly derived from the Welsh *meilir* meaning "polished" or "bright." A name with literary associations, borne by the 12th-century Welsh poet Meilir Brydydd.

**64. Myrddin** (MER-din) — The Welsh form of *Merlin*. Derived from the Brittonic *Marontinos*, possibly meaning "sea-born" or connected to the word for "marine." The legendary figure of Merlin originates in Welsh poetry as *Myrddin Wyllt* ("Merlin the Wild"), a madman who lived in the Caledonian Forest after the Battle of Arfderydd.

**65. Owain** (OH-win) — "noble-born" or "youth." The Welsh form of *Ewan*, cognate with the Irish *Eoghan*. Borne by Owain Glyndŵr, the 15th-century Welsh leader who briefly achieved Welsh independence. The name's three-vowel structure gives it a melodic quality.

**66. Rhys** (REESS) — "enthusiasm," "ardor," or "passion." One of the most popular Welsh names, Rhys has maintained steady usage for over a century. Rhys ap Gruffydd was the 12th-century ruler who built Cardigan Castle and was described as "the greatest lord that ever wore a crown in Wales."

**67. Tewdwr** (thee-DOOR) — "divine father" or "father god." Derived from the Welsh *Tewdwr*, possibly connected to the Celtic deity *Teutates*. A rare but historically attested name.

**68. Trahaearn** (trah-HAY-arn) — "battle-bearer" or "warrior." A name borne by Trahaearn ap Caradog, a 11th-century king of Gwynedd who resisted Norman expansion. The name's hard consonant cluster gives it a distinctive phonetic profile.

**69. Traian** (tray-AHN) — The Welsh form of *Trajan*. A Roman name that entered Welsh tradition through the medieval Welsh chronicles, which recorded the Roman emperor's deeds.

**70. Ywain** (YOO-win) — The Welsh form of *Owain*, used in Arthurian literature as one of King Arthur's knights. The name appears in the French romance *Yvain, the Knight of the Lion* by Chrétien de Troyes, demonstrating the cross-cultural transmission of Celtic names.

### Breton, Cornish, and Manx Names

**71. Alan** (AL-an) — Possibly derived from the Breton *Alan*, meaning "handsome" or "rock." The name entered Breton onomastics in the early medieval period and spread through the Norman conquest to England and France. Alan Fitzhaymo was a Breton noble who accompanied William the Conqueror.

**72. Yann** (YAHN) — The Breton form of *John*. Distinct from the Irish and Scottish forms, *Yann* reflects the Brythonic phonological tradition. The Breton *Y* is pronounced as a short *eh* sound, closer to the French *je*.

**73. Jark** (JAR-k) — The Breton form of *Jack*, derived from *John*. A name that demonstrates the survival of Celtic forms even in names that entered English through Norman French.

**74. Malo** (MAH-loh) — Derived from the Breton saint *Saint Malo* (or *Malo*, *Maclou*), a 6th-century Welsh monk who founded the city of Saint-Malo in Brittany. The name means "chief" or "prince" in Breton.

**75. Marko** (MAR-koh) — A Breton form of *Mark*, used in modern Breton naming practice. The name reflects the revival of Breton language and culture in the 20th and 21st centuries.

**76. Kado** (KAH-doh) — A Cornish name meaning "battle." Derived from the Cornish *kadh*, cognate with the Welsh *cad* and the Irish *cath*. A name that preserves the ancient Celtic word for battle.

**77. Tremayne** (TREH-mane) — A Cornish surname-name derived from the Cornish *tre* (homestead) and *melyn* (yellow), possibly referring to a homestead near yellow flowers or a yellow-haired ancestor. Used as a first name in modern Cornish revival practice.

**78. Syllas** (SIL-as) — A Cornish name derived from the Cornish *silas* meaning "forest" or "wood." A nature name that reflects the Celtic tradition of connecting personal identity to the landscape.

**79. Cronyn** (KRON-in) — A Manx name derived from the Gaelic *Cronín*, meaning "little raven." The Manx language, a Goidelic language closely related to Irish and Scottish Gaelic, preserved this form through the island's unique cultural history.

**80. Kione** (KEE-oh-neh) — A Manx name meaning "head" or "chief." Derived from the Manx Gaelic *kione*, cognate with the Irish *ceann* and the Welsh *pen*. A name that encodes leadership and authority.

### Additional Names Across the Celtic Spectrum

**81. Arawn** (AR-awn) — A Welsh mythological name, Arawn is the king of Annwn (the Otherworld) in the *Math fab Mathonwy* branch of the *Mabinogion*. The name's meaning is uncertain but may relate to "wish" or "desire."

**82. Beli** (BEL-ee) — A Welsh name borne by *Beli Mawr* ("Beli the Great"), a legendary king of Britain in Welsh mythology. The name may derive from the Celtic god *Belenus*, associated with the sun and healing.

**83. Braid** (BRADE) — A Scottish name meaning "narrow" or "strait." Derived from the Gaelic *braigh*, referring to a narrow strip of land between water bodies. A topographic name that reflects the Celtic tradition of landscape-based naming.

**84. Cadoc** (KAD-og) — A Welsh saint's name, *Saint Cadoc* was a 6th-century abbot whose monastery at Llancarfan was one of the most important centers of learning in medieval Wales. The name may mean "battle" or "fight."

**85. Cadi** (KAD-ee) — A Welsh name meaning "battle." The feminine form *Cadfael* is more common, but *Cadi* has been used for boys in modern Welsh naming practice.

**86. Dyfnwal** (DIF-nwal) — A Welsh name meaning "deep ruler" or "profound leader." Derived from *dyfn* (deep) and *gwal* (ruler). A name with a solemn, weighty phonetic quality.

**87. Eilian** (AY-lee-an) — A Welsh name meaning "rock" or "flash of lightning." Possibly derived from the Welsh *eilian* meaning "little rock" or from the Latin *Elianus*. The name has a lyrical, flowing quality.

**88. Gwalchmai** (GWAHLK-hy-mai) — The Welsh form of *Gawain*, one of King Arthur's greatest knights. The name means "hawk of the plain" or "hawk of May," combining *gwalch* (hawk) and *ma* (plain) or *Mai* (the month of May). A name that encodes both nature and chivalric tradition.

**89. Idris** (ID-ris) — A Welsh name meaning "ardent lord" or "passionate leader." Derived from *idd* (ardor) and *ris* (lord). A name borne by Saint Idris, a 5th-century Welsh saint, and by Idris ap Gwylog, a legendary king of the Britons.

**90. Llyr** (LYR) — A Welsh name meaning "sea." Llyr is the father of Branwen, Bran, and Manawydan in the *Mabinogion*, a divine figure associated with the sea and the Otherworld. The name's single syllable and open vowel make it phonetically striking.

**91. Morgan** (MOR-gən) — A name shared across the Celtic world, *Morgan* derives from the Welsh *Morys* or the Brittonic *Morigena*, meaning "sea-born" or "born of the sea." Saint Morgan was a 6th-century Welsh bishop, and the name entered Arthurian legend as *Morgan le Fay*.

**92. Neriah** (neh-RAI-ah) — A Welsh name meaning "light of the Lord" or "divine light." A biblical name that entered Welsh tradition through the Welsh Bible translations of the 16th century.

**93. Peredur** (peh-REE-dor) — A Welsh name meaning "cultivator of land" or "farmer." A figure in Welsh mythology and Arthurian literature, Peredur appears in the Welsh triads and in Chrétien de Troyes' *Perceval*. The name's agricultural etymology connects to the Celtic tradition of naming based on occupation.

**94. Rhonwy** (RON-ee) — A Welsh name meaning "red king" or "red sovereign." Derived from *rhudd* (red) and *rhain* (king). A name that shares etymology with the Irish *Rory* but follows Welsh phonological patterns.

**95. Taliesin** (tal-EE-sin) — A Welsh name meaning "shining brow" or "bright forehead." Taliesin was a 6th-century Brythonic poet whose name became legendary in Welsh literary tradition. The name's association with poetic genius makes it a powerful choice.

**96. Tewdwr** (thee-DOOR) — Also appears in the Welsh tradition as a saint's name, *Saint Tewdwr* was a 5th-century Welsh prince and martyr. The name's dual appearance in Welsh and Cornish traditions demonstrates the cross-cultural flow of Celtic names.

**97. Uther** (YOO-ther) — A Welsh name meaning "chief" or "first." Most famously borne by Uther Pendragon, the father of King Arthur in Welsh and British legend. The name *Pendragon* means "chief dragon" or "head dragon," a title of extraordinary martial significance.

**98. Wulfric** (WUL-fric) — A Cornish name of Old English origin, *Wulfric* means "wolf power" or "wolf ruler." Used in modern Cornish revival practice as a bridge between the Cornish and Anglo-Saxon naming traditions.

**99. Yestin** (YESS-tin) — A Welsh name meaning "young" or "youthful." A modern Welsh name that draws on the Welsh word *iestyn*, meaning "young man" or "youth." The name's simplicity and clarity make it accessible.

**100. Ysbaddaden** (iss-ba-DAD-en) — A Welsh mythological name, *Ysbaddaden* is the giant father of Branwen in the *Mabinogion*. The name's meaning is uncertain but may relate to "exalted" or "lofty." A name of considerable mythological weight.

## Meaning & Etymology

The etymological landscape of Celtic names is remarkably rich because the Celtic language family represents one of the oldest continuously spoken language groups in Europe. The names in this collection draw from three distinct etymological strata:

**Pre-Celtic substrate:** Some names preserve vocabulary from languages spoken in Britain and Ireland before the arrival of Celtic speakers. The name *Avon* (meaning "river" in Brittonic) likely derives from a pre-Indo-European word for water. Similarly, place names like *Thames* and *Severn* preserve pre-Celtic hydronyms that may inform personal names.

**Proto-Celtic layer:** Many names can be traced back to reconstructed Proto-Celtic roots. The root *brosko-* (bright, shining) appears in names like *Brosius* and may underlie Welsh *Brychan*. The root *daru-* (oak) appears in names like *Daran* and *Darwin*. The root *lug-* (light, shine) appears in *Lugh*, *Llew*, and *Lucius*, connecting to the pan-Celtic god *Lugus*.

**Historic Celtic layer:** The best-documented layer consists of names attested in Insular Celtic languages (Old Irish, Old Welsh, Old Breton, etc.). These names carry transparent etymologies that can be traced through comparative linguistics. *Cian* (ancient), *Fionn* (fair), *Bran* (raven), *Rhys* (ardor) are all transparently etymologized.

The meaning of a Celtic name often operates on multiple levels simultaneously. *Aodh* means "fire" but also carries connotations of the Celtic fire god *Aodh* (or *Aengus*), who was associated with love, youth, and poetic inspiration. *Cernunnos* means "the horned one" but also invokes the entire theological complex of the horned god in Celtic polytheism. This multilayered meaning is a distinctive feature of Celtic onomastics that most modern naming traditions lack.

## Origin & Cultural Context

Celtic naming traditions emerged from a cultural context that was fundamentally different from the Roman and later Christian naming systems that would eventually dominate Europe. In pre-Christian Celtic society, names were not merely identifiers—they were performative acts that established the child's place in a network of kinship, tribal allegiance, and spiritual belonging.

**The Patronymic Tradition:** Celtic names were traditionally constructed using patronymics rather than surnames. A son of *Brian* would be *Brian mac* (son of Brian); a daughter would be *Brian ingen* (daughter of Brian). This system meant that names were inherently relational—they connected the individual to their ancestors. The modern use of fixed surnames (O'Brien, MacLeod, ap Llywelyn) represents a later development, but the patronymic logic persists in many Celtic names.

**The Saintly Innovation:** With the Christianization of the Celtic world (5th–7th centuries CE), a new naming pattern emerged: the adoption of saint names. Irish and Welsh monks adopted Greek and Hebrew biblical names ( *Pádraig*, *Colmán*, *Deiniol*) alongside traditional Celtic names. This created a dual naming system that persists today—families might name a child *Seán* (traditional) or *Pádraig* (Christian), each carrying different cultural associations.

**The Colonial Suppression:** The most significant disruption to Celtic naming traditions came through colonial policies. The English Crown's efforts to "civilize" Ireland and Wales involved systematic suppression of Gaelic and Welsh language and culture. The 1588 Act of Parliament in Ireland declared that Irish names were "barbarous and unsuitable," and the 18th-century Penal Laws actively discouraged Irish-language education. This led to a massive Anglicization of names that continues to shape the Celtic onomastic landscape.

**The Modern Revival:** The 20th and 21st centuries have seen a powerful revival of Celtic naming traditions, driven by cultural nationalism, linguistic revitalization movements, and a broader trend toward culturally distinctive naming. In Wales, the Welsh Language Act of 1993 and the establishment of the Welsh Language Commissioner created institutional support for Welsh names. In Ireland, the *Gaelscoil* movement (Irish-language schools) has produced a generation of parents fluent in or familiar with Irish naming conventions. In Scotland, the Gaelic Language (Scotland) Act 2005 has similarly supported the revival of Gaelic names.

## Pronunciation Guide

Celtic names present a particular challenge for parents who want to honor the original pronunciation while ensuring their child can navigate a predominantly English-speaking world. The following guide provides standard pronunciations, with notes on regional variation.

### Irish Pronunciation Conventions

Irish orthography follows a consistent phonological system based on the *leanas agus leathana* (slender and broad) rule:

- **Slender consonants** (followed by *e, i*) are palatalized—producing a *y*-like sound. *Seán* is pronounced *SHAYN*, not *SEHN*.
- **Broad consonants** (followed by *a, o, u*) are velarized—producing a deeper, more guttural sound. *Donn* is pronounced *DON*, not *DOHN*.

Key Irish sounds:
- **bh/fh** = *v* (as in *Seamus*: *SHAY-mus*)
- **ch** = guttural *h* (as in *Cillian*: *KIL-ee-an*, where the *ll* is a lateral fricative)
- **dh/gH** = *y* or *g* sound depending on context
- **í/é** = long *ee* and *ay* sounds

### Scottish Gaelic Pronunciation Conventions

Scottish Gaelic shares many features with Irish but has distinct phonological developments:

- **ch** = same guttural *h* as Irish (as in *Alasdair*: *AL-az-dar*)
- **f/h** = often pronounced as *f* or *v* depending on position
- **bh/vh** = *v* sound (as in *Bhòid*: *VOYD*)
- **ea/ai** = *ey* or *ay* sounds (as in *Eòghann*: *YOO-an*)

### Welsh Pronunciation Conventions

Welsh orthography is highly phonemic—each letter has a consistent sound:

- **dd** = voiced *th* (as in *Rhys*: *REESS*, where the *dh* in other names like *Gwadd* produces the voiced *th*)
- **ll** = voiceless lateral fricative (a sound that doesn't exist in English; approximate by blowing air around the sides of the tongue)
- **ch** = guttural *h* (as in *Llwyd*: *LUID*)
- **w** = *oo* sound (as in *Owain*: *OH-win*)
- **ng** = *ng* as in *sing* (not *n-g*)
- **rh** = *r* with aspiration (as in *Rhys*: the *r* is breathed rather than voiced)

### Breton and Cornish Pronunciation

- **Breton** uses French-influenced orthography with some distinct sounds: *zh* = *sh* (as in *Yann*: *YAHN*), *j* = *zh* (as in *Jark*: *ZHAR-k*), *c'h* = guttural *h* (as in *Kado*: *KAH-doh*)
- **Cornish** revival orthography varies, but standard forms use: *dh* = *th* (voiced), *gh* = *g* (hard), *k* = *k* (hard, used instead of *c* to avoid confusion with English)

## Popularity Trends

Celtic name popularity follows a complex, non-linear trajectory that reflects broader patterns of cultural identity, linguistic policy, and onomastic fashion.

### The First Wave (1890–1920)

The late 19th and early 20th centuries saw the first major wave of Celtic name adoption in English-speaking countries. Irish immigration to the United States (particularly during and after the Great Famine) brought names like *Patrick*, *Michael*, *James*, and *Daniel* into mainstream American usage. These names had been Anglicized from their Irish forms (*Pádraig*, *Seán*, *Séamus*, *Dáithí*), but their Celtic origins were transparent.

In Britain, the Victorian fascination with Celtic mythology and the Arthurian revival led to the adoption of names like *Lancelot*, *Gawain*, *Mordred*, and *Tristan*—though these are more Arthurian than strictly Celtic. The Celtic Revival in literature (Yeats, Lady Gregory, Synge) also contributed to a cultural appreciation for Irish and Welsh names.

### The Decline (1920–1970)

The mid-20th century saw a dramatic decline in Celtic name usage. The post-war period favored "international" names that were easy to pronounce across languages—*John*, *David*, *Michael*, *Robert*. Celtic names, particularly those with difficult phonemes (*Rhys*, *Cian*, *Llywelyn*), were increasingly seen as "ethnic" or "regional" rather than universally acceptable.

In Ireland itself, the period saw a shift toward English-name dominance in official contexts. The 1937 Irish Constitution established Irish as the first official language, but in practice, English remained the language of government, education, and commerce. Many Irish parents chose English names for their children to give them better economic prospects.

### The Resurgence (1990–Present)

The Celtic name resurgence began in the 1990s and has accelerated through the 2020s. Several factors have contributed:

**The Celtic Tiger (Ireland):** Ireland's economic boom from 1995 to 2007 coincided with a cultural renaissance. Irish-language media (*TG4*, established 1996), the success of Riverdance (1994), and the global popularity of Irish music (U2, The Cranberries, Enya) created a positive association with Irish culture and, by extension, Irish names.

**Welsh Nationalism:** The establishment of the National Assembly for Wales (1999) and the Welsh Language Act (1993) created institutional support for Welsh cultural identity. Names like *Rhys*, *Owain*, and *Cadan* gained popularity among Welsh parents seeking to express their national identity.

**Scottish Devolution:** The Scottish Parliament's establishment in 1999 similarly fueled interest in Scottish Gaelic names. *Alasdair*, *Callum*, *Lachlan*, and *Iain* became increasingly common.

**Global Celtic Identity:** For the diaspora—particularly in the United States, Canada, Australia, and New Zealand—Celtic names offer a way to connect with ancestral heritage. The 2020 census data shows that over 30 million Americans claim Irish ancestry, and over 15 million claim Scottish ancestry. These populations represent a vast reservoir of potential name adopters.

### Current Rankings (2025–2026)

According to the latest SSA and ONS data:
- **Ireland:** *Conor* (#3), *Jack* (#1), *James* (#2), *Daniel* (#4), *Sean* (#5)
- **Scotland:** *Jack* (#1), *Lewis* (#2), *James* (#3), *Noah* (#4), *Oliver* (#5)
- **Wales:** *Noah* (#1), *Oliver* (#2), *Jack* (#3), *Leo* (#4), *Arthur* (#5)
- **United States:** *Liam* (#1), *Noah* (#2), *Oliver* (#3), *Elijah* (#4), *William* (#5)

Notably, *Arthur* has entered the top 10 in Wales, and *Liam* (the anglicized form of *Leah* or possibly derived from *Uilliam*, the Irish form of *William*) has topped the US charts for several years. *Cian* ranks #214 in the US and #47 in Ireland. *Rhys* ranks #312 in the US and #28 in Wales.
