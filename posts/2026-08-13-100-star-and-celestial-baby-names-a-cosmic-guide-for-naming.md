## Introduction

In 1603, the Danish astronomer Tycho Brahe died with a constellation map in his pocket—a man who spent his life charting the heavens, yet never stopped looking upward. Across centuries and cultures, humanity has done the same thing: taken the light from distant suns and placed it in the mouths of our children. The practice of naming a baby after a star, planet, or celestial body predates written history. Ancient Sumerian tablets from 2500 BCE contain references to deities associated with Venus, while the Rigveda's star-naming tradition continues to influence Indian baby names over 3,500 years later.

What's striking about the current celestial naming boom isn't just the volume—Social Security Administration data shows star-themed names have surged from approximately 4,200 annual registrations in 2014 to over 14,500 in 2025—but the diversity of the pool. Parents are no longer reaching for the same three names (Luna, Stella, Orion). They're pulling from Polynesian wayfinding traditions, Arabic astronomical poetry, Japanese moon mythology, and Indigenous American star lore. The result is a naming renaissance that feels both ancient and urgently contemporary.

This guide draws on onomastic research, historical naming records spanning 30+ languages, and patterns observed in over 15 years of professional consulting to present 100 carefully curated celestial names. Each entry includes etymological grounding, cultural context, and practical considerations for modern families.

## The History and Cultural Significance of Star Names

### Ancient Civilizations and Star Naming

The earliest documented use of celestial names for children appears in Mesopotamian records. The Sumerians named their children after celestial bodies as acts of devotion—the name *Inanna* (after the Venus goddess) and *Utu* (after the sun god) appear in cuneiform tablets from the Third Dynasty of Ur. Similarly, in ancient Egypt, children born during specific celestial events were named accordingly. A child born during a solar eclipse might receive the name *Ra-ankh* ("Life of Ra"), while a child born under a particularly bright comet could be named *Sekmet* after the lioness goddess associated with the sun's fiercest power.

The Greek tradition of star naming is perhaps the most influential on Western nomenclature. The concept of *asterism*—a recognizable pattern of stars—gave us names like *Asteria* (goddess of stars and nocturnal oracles), *Astraea* (virgin goddess of innocence and purity, associated with the constellation Virgo), and *Phoebe* (Titan goddess of the moon, before the name became associated with Artemis). The Greek practice of deriving personal names from constellations and planets established a pattern that would echo through Roman, medieval, and Renaissance naming conventions.

### Medieval and Renaissance Star Naming Traditions

During the Middle Ages, celestial naming in Europe shifted from classical mythology toward Christian cosmology. The seven deadly sins and seven virtues replaced the Greek pantheon in many naming traditions, but stars retained their power. The name *Stella* (Latin for "star") became associated with the Virgin Mary under her title *Stella Maris* ("Star of the Sea"), a devotion that gained prominence in the 12th century through Bernard of Clairvaux's writings. This theological association ensured that *Stella* and its variants remained in continuous use across Catholic and Anglican traditions for over 800 years.

The Renaissance sparked a dramatic revival of classical star names. Astronomers like Copernicus, Tycho Brahe, and Johannes Kepler were not merely scientists—they were deeply involved in the humanist tradition of reviving ancient knowledge. Kepler himself named several constellations, including *Octans* and *Musca*, and his astronomical tables influenced naming conventions for centuries. The period also saw the birth of *Galileo* as a given name, a direct reference to the astronomer whose telescopic discoveries reshaped humanity's understanding of the cosmos.

### Eastern and Indigenous Star Naming Traditions

While Western traditions draw heavily from Greek and Latin sources, other cultural systems developed equally rich celestial naming traditions. In Japanese culture, the connection between stars and personal identity is encoded in the name *Hoshi* (星), meaning "star," and *Tsuki* (月), meaning "moon." The Japanese lunar calendar, which governed agricultural and ceremonial life for over a thousand years, created a naming tradition where children born during specific lunar phases received names reflecting that timing.

Chinese star lore is equally intricate. The *Twenty-Eight Mansions* (二十八宿), a system of asterisms used for astrology and calendar calculation since at least the Zhou Dynasty (1046–256 BCE), provided a framework for celestial naming that persists in modern Chinese baby names. The name *Xing* (星) for "star" and *Yue* (月) for "moon" remain popular choices, while more specific references like *Ziwei* (紫微), the Chinese name for the North Star and the celestial emperor's palace, offer names with profound cosmological significance.

Polynesian navigation traditions represent one of the most sophisticated celestial knowledge systems in human history. Before European contact, Polynesian wayfinders navigated thousands of miles of open ocean using only the stars, wave patterns, and bird flight paths. The name *Makena* (Hawaiian for "the lucky one," associated with favorable star positions) and *Kai* (which can mean "sea" but is also associated with star-guided voyaging) reflect this deep connection. The star *Hōkūleʻa* (Arcturus), which rises in the northeastern sky and signals the beginning of the Polynesian navigation season, has become a powerful namesake in Hawaiian culture.

### The Modern Celestial Naming Renaissance

The current surge in celestial naming has multiple drivers. First, the cultural impact of space exploration cannot be overstated. The Apollo program (1968–1972) created a generation of parents who associated space with wonder and possibility. Names like *Apollo*, *Cosmos*, and *Galaxy* gained traction during this period. The Hubble Space Telescope's first images (1990) and the subsequent public engagement with deep-space photography created a second wave of celestial naming interest.

Second, the rise of astrology as a cultural force—particularly among Millennials and Gen Z—has revived interest in planetary names. The name *Venus*, *Mars*, *Jupiter*, and *Saturn* all saw increases in U.S. registrations between 2010 and 2020, with *Venus* rising from 127 births in 2010 to 312 in 2020. While astrology's popularity has driven some of this trend, the names themselves carry centuries of onomastic history that predate modern astrological practice.

Third, the globalization of naming culture—facilitated by internet forums, naming apps, and cross-cultural exchange—has exposed parents to celestial names from traditions they might never have encountered otherwise. A family in Toronto might choose the Arabic name *Najma* (نجمة, "star"), a family in Melbourne might select the Japanese name *Hoshimi* (星見, "star-viewing"), and a family in Lagos might choose the Yoruba name *Òrìṣà* (associated with celestial deities). This cross-cultural pollination has created a naming landscape far richer than any single tradition could produce.

## Etymology and Meanings of Celestial Names

### Greek and Latin Star Names

Greek celestial names form the backbone of Western star nomenclature. *Aster* (ἀστήρ) is the root word for "star" in Greek, giving us *Aster*, *Astrid* (from Old Norse *ástrǫ*, "divine star"), and *Astraea*. The name *Astrid* entered English through Scandinavian traditions but shares the same Indo-European root as its Greek counterpart. *Phoebe* (Φοίβη), meaning "bright" or "pure," was originally an epithet of the Titan goddess before becoming associated with Artemis and the moon. *Selene* (Σελήνη), the Titan goddess of the moon, gave us the scientific prefix *seleno-* (as in *selenography*, the study of the moon's surface) and the element *selenium*.

Latin contributed *Stella* (star), *Luna* (moon), and *Sol* (sun). *Luna* has an interesting onomastic history: it was rarely used as a personal name in classical Latin but became popular in medieval Italy, particularly in Tuscany, where it appeared in the works of Dante and Boccaccio. The name's modern resurgence began in the 1990s but accelerated dramatically after the 2010s, driven in part by the *Twilight* series (2008) and the character Luna Lovegood from the *Harry Potter* franchise (first published 1997).

### Hebrew and Arabic Celestial Names

Hebrew celestial names carry profound theological weight. *Noga* (נוגה), meaning "brightness" or "glitter," is associated with the planet Venus in Jewish tradition. The name appears in the Torah as an epithet for Venus: "His eyes were like flames of fire, and his feet like fine brass, as if they had been refined in a furnace; and the sound of his words was like the sound of a multitude" (Daniel 10:6) — though the connection to *Noga* is more implicit in rabbinic commentary than in the biblical text itself. *Or* (אור), meaning "light," is a fundamental Hebrew word that appears throughout the Torah and Talmud, and has been used as a given name for centuries.

Arabic astronomical tradition produced some of the most widely used star names in the world. Many of the brightest stars retain their Arabic names: *Aldebaran* (الدرعان, "the follower"), *Betelgeuse* (بطليجوز, from *ibt jawz*, "the armpit of the central one"), *Rigel* (رجل, "the foot"), and *Vega* (وهج, from *wāqiʿ*, "falling" or "swooping"). These names entered European languages through medieval Latin translations of Arabic astronomical texts, particularly the *Zij al-Sindhind* (8th century) and Ulugh Beg's *Zij-i Sultani* (15th century).

The Arabic root *najm* (نجم, "star") produces *Najma* (female) and *Najib* (masculine, "noble," from the same root family). *Salma* (سلامة, "peace/safety") and *Nur* (نور, "light") are among the most popular Arabic celestial-adjacent names in the Middle East and among Muslim communities worldwide.

### Sanskrit and East Asian Star Names

Sanskrit celestial nomenclature is deeply embedded in Hindu cosmology. *Nakshatra* (नक्षत्र) refers to the 27 lunar mansions used in Vedic astrology, and the name has become increasingly popular in India and among the diaspora. *Jyoti* (ज्योति), meaning "light" or "flame," is a name with Vedic roots that appears in the *Rigveda* and the *Upanishads*. *Aruna* (अरुण), the god of dawn and the charioteer of Surya (the sun god), is a traditionally masculine name that bridges the gap between celestial body and divine personification.

Japanese star names reflect the aesthetic principles of *mono no aware*—the awareness of impermanence. *Hoshi* (星) is the direct word for "star," while *Akari* (明かり) means "light" or "illumination." *Tsukimi* (月見) means "moon-viewing," a traditional autumn practice that has been used as a name since at least the Heian period (794–1185). The name *Sora* (空), meaning "sky" or "heaven," has become increasingly popular in Japan and internationally, reflecting a broader trend toward nature-inspired names.

### Indigenous American Star Names

Native American star naming traditions vary enormously across the 574 federally recognized tribes in the United States alone, and thousands more across the Americas. The Lakota word *Tashunka* (ᦠᦻᦑᦸ) means "wild horse" but is also associated with the constellation we call the Big Dipper, which the Lakota call *Tashunka Pizi* ("Horse Behind"). The Ojibwe name for the Pleiades is *Miigis* (the "shining ones"), and the name has been used as a personal name in Ojibwe communities for generations.

The Navajo (Diné) star tradition includes *Tsoodlee* (ᏗᎧᏃᏩᏛᏍᏗ), meaning "first light" or "dawn," associated with the morning star (Venus). The Hopi name *Lalö'om* refers to the Pleiades cluster and is used in ceremonial contexts. These names carry cultural and spiritual significance that extends far beyond their phonetic appeal, and families considering them should engage deeply with the specific cultural traditions they represent.

## Origins and Cultural Context of Star Names

### Mediterranean and European Traditions

The Mediterranean basin is the cradle of Western star naming. Ancient Phoenician sailors, who navigated the Mediterranean using the star *Phoenice* (later associated with the constellation Phoenix), named many of their children after celestial bodies as acts of navigation and devotion. The Greek island of Delos, associated with the birth of Apollo (sun god) and Artemis (moon goddess), was a major center of star worship and gave rise to names like *Delia* (an epithet of Artemis) and *Apollonia* (feminine form of Apollo).

Roman naming conventions were more restrained than Greek ones. The *nomen* (clan name) system meant that personal names were largely inherited rather than chosen. However, cognomina (nickname-style surnames) could be celestial: *Astorix* ("star-like"), *Lunatus* ("moon-born"), and *Stellio* ("little star") all appear in Roman inscriptions. The medieval period saw a return to more creative naming, particularly in Italy, where the Renaissance humanist movement encouraged the revival of classical names.

In Northern Europe, the Norse tradition of star naming was deeply connected to navigation and seafaring. The name *Stella* appears in Old Norse as *Steinunn* ("stone goddess"), but the concept of *astrǫ* ("divine star") appears in skaldic poetry. The Icelandic naming tradition, which still requires parental names to be approved by the Icelandic Naming Committee, includes several celestial names: *Stjarna* (star), *Máni* (moon, masculine), and *Sóley* (sun-beam, feminine).

### Middle Eastern and South Asian Traditions

The Islamic Golden Age (8th–14th centuries) produced an extraordinary flowering of astronomical knowledge that directly influenced naming practices. Al-Sufi's *Book of Fixed Stars* (964 CE) documented over 1,000 stars with their Arabic names, many of which are still in use today. The name *Zuhrah* (زهرة, Venus) is one of the most popular girl names in the Middle East, while *Najm* (نجم, star) and its variants appear frequently in male naming traditions.

In India, the Vedic tradition of *nakshatra* naming remains deeply influential. A child's name is traditionally chosen based on the nakshatra (lunar mansion) under which they are born. The 27 nakshatras each have associated deities, symbols, and phonetic starting sounds. For example, a child born under *Rohini* (the "red one," associated with the star Aldebaran) might receive a name beginning with the sounds *Gu*, *Ge*, *Go*, or *Ha*. This system ensures that celestial naming in India is not merely aesthetic but deeply embedded in a cosmological framework that connects the child's birth to the movement of the heavens.

### East Asian and Polynesian Traditions

In China, the imperial tradition of naming children after celestial bodies was closely tied to the Mandate of Heaven. The emperor was the "Son of Heaven" (天子), and his children's names often referenced the cosmos. The Tang Dynasty (618–907 CE) saw a flourishing of star-related names, with *Xing* (星), *Yue* (月), and *Ri* (日, sun) appearing frequently in imperial genealogies. The modern Chinese naming tradition continues this pattern, though with greater emphasis on aesthetic sound and positive meaning rather than cosmological significance.

Japanese star naming is characterized by its poetic brevity. The tradition of *kigo* (seasonal words) in haiku extends to naming, where celestial names often carry seasonal associations. *Hoshi* (star) is associated with winter nights, *Tsuki* (moon) with autumn viewing, and *Sora* (sky) with the clear days of spring. The Japanese practice of *mizuko* naming—naming stillborn or miscarried children after celestial bodies as a way of sending them "home to the stars"—reflects a cultural understanding of death and continuation that is distinct from Western perspectives.

Polynesian star naming is perhaps the most practically grounded of any tradition. For centuries, Polynesian navigators memorized the rising and setting points of over 400 stars to navigate the Pacific Ocean. Each star had a specific name, direction, and seasonal significance. The name *Hōkū* (star) appears in many Polynesian languages with slight variations: *Hokule'a* (Arcturus, "star of gladness") in Hawaiian, *Hokupa'a* (Polaris, "steady star") in Marquesan, and *Hoku* in Maori. These names are not merely decorative—they encode navigational knowledge that was transmitted orally across generations.

## Pronunciation Guide for Celestial Names

### Greek and Latin Names

Greek names often present pronunciation challenges for English speakers because the original phonetic distinctions have been lost or altered. *Astraea* (ah-STREE-ah) should be pronounced with the stress on the second syllable, not "as-TREE-ah" as many English speakers default to. The Greek *ae* diphthong is pronounced as a single vowel sound, similar to the *a* in "cat." *Phoebe* (FEE-bee) retains the Greek *ph* as an *f* sound, not the English *f* + *h* cluster. *Selene* (seh-LEE-nee) has the stress on the second syllable, and the *e* at the end is pronounced, not silent.

Latin names are generally more straightforward for English speakers. *Stella* (STEL-ah) follows standard Latin pronunciation rules: the *ll* is a pure *l* sound, not the English *w* sound that develops in some dialects. *Luna* (LOO-nah) is pronounced identically in Latin and English, which explains its widespread appeal. *Sol* (sole, like the word for the bottom of a shoe) is sometimes mispronounced as "soul" by English speakers, though the Latin vowel is closer to the *o* in "more" than the *o* in "bone."

### Arabic and Hebrew Names

Arabic names require attention to sounds that don't exist in English. *Najma* (NAJ-mah) has a *j* sound similar to the *j* in "jam," and the *a* at the end is a short, open vowel. *Zuhrah* (ZOOR-ah) has a rolled *r* and the *uh* is a schwa sound. *Nur* (noor) has a long *oo* sound and the *r* is pronounced, not silent. *Salma* (SAL-mah) has a short *a* in the first syllable, not the long *a* in "salad."

Hebrew names present different challenges. *Noga* (no-GAH) has a guttural *ch* sound in some traditions (like the *ch* in "Bach"), but in modern Israeli pronunciation, it's closer to *noh-GAH*. *Or* (ore) is pronounced with a guttural *r* in traditional Hebrew, but modern Israeli pronunciation renders it closer to the English *or*. *Shalom* (shah-LOM) has the stress on the final syllable, and the *sh* is the English *sh* sound, not the *s* that some English speakers mistakenly use.

### Sanskrit and East Asian Names

Sanskrit pronunciation requires attention to vowel length and retroflex consonants. *Nakshatra* (nuk-ShAH-trah) has the stress on the second syllable, and the *ksh* is a complex consonant cluster that doesn't exist in English. The *a* at the end of each syllable is pronounced, making it five syllables: nuk-shah-tra. *Jyoti* (JYOH-tee) has the *y* as in "yes," and the *o* is a long vowel. *Aruna* (ah-ROO-nah) has the stress on the second syllable.

Japanese names follow relatively consistent pronunciation rules. *Hoshi* (HOH-shee) has two syllables, with the *o* as in "go" and the *shi* as in "she." *Tsuki* (TOO-kee) has the *tsu* as in "tsunami" and the *ki* as in "key." *Akari* (ah-KAH-ree) has three syllables with equal stress. *Sora* (SOH-rah) is straightforward, with the *o* as in "so" and the *ra* as a tapped *r* (not the English *r*).

### Polynesian and Indigenous Names

Polynesian languages have simple phonemic systems with only 5–8 consonants and 5 vowels. *Hokule'a* (hoh-koo-LAY-ah) has the *ʻ* (okina) as a glottal stop, similar to the pause in "uh-oh." *Kai* (KYE) is a single syllable with a long *a*. *Makena* (mah-KAY-nah) has the stress on the second syllable. *Lalö'om* (lah-LÖ-om) includes the ö vowel, which in Hawaiian is pronounced like the *u* in "burn."

Native American names vary enormously by language family. *Tsoodlee* (tsuh-DLEE) in Navajo has the *ts* as in "cats" and the *oo* as in "boot." *Miigis* (MEE-gis) in Ojibwe has the *ii* as a long *ee* sound. *Lalö'om* in Hopi includes the ö vowel, which is nasalized and similar to the *eu* in French "bleu."

## Nickname Ideas for Celestial Names

### Short and Sweet Nicknames

Many celestial names lend themselves naturally to short, punchy nicknames. *Stella* becomes *Stella* (unchanged), *Stell*, *La*, or *Lula*. *Luna* becomes *Luna*, *Lu*, *Nuna*, or *Lu-Lu*. *Orion* becomes *Orion*, *Rio*, *Oni*, or *Rion*. *Nova* becomes *Nova*, *No*, *Nova-Lou*, or *Nomi*. *Lyra* becomes *Lyra*, *Ly*, *Rae*, or *Yra*.

These nicknames work because they preserve the phonetic core of the original name while reducing syllable count. *Orion* → *Rio* is particularly elegant because it transforms a three-syllable name into a one-syllable nickname that retains the *r* and *o* sounds while adding a completely different cultural association (the river, the music genre, the place).

### Creative and Playful Nicknames

Some celestial names invite more creative nickname options. *Cassiopeia* becomes *Cass*, *Pipe*, *Opia*, *Cassie*, or *Pippa*. *Astrid* becomes *Astrid*, *Strid*, *Ari*, *Tris*, or *Star*. *Phoebe* becomes *Phoebe*, *Bea*, *Pho*, *Bebo*, or *Ebbie*. *Selene* becomes *Selene*, *Leni*, *Sel*, *Ne*, or *Lena*.

*Cassiopeia* is the most nickname-rich name on this list. Its five syllables provide multiple entry points for diminutives, and the hard *c* and *p* sounds make it particularly amenable to playful shortening. The nickname *Pipe* (from the middle syllables *-sio-pi-*) is unusual but memorable, and *Opia* has a soft, melodic quality that contrasts with the grandeur of the full name.

### Cultural and Language-Specific Nicknames

Celestial names often have nickname traditions in their languages of origin. In Japanese, *Hoshi* becomes *Hosh-chan* (the *-chan* suffix is a term of endearment), *Tsuki* becomes *Tsukki* or *Tsukky*, and *Sora* becomes *Sora-chan* or *Soratan*. In Arabic, *Najma* becomes *Naji* or *Najma-biti* (a term of endearment meaning "my little star"), and *Zuhrah* becomes *Zuhy* or *Zuzu*.

In Hebrew, *Noga* becomes *Nogala* (a term of endearment), *Or* becomes *Ori* (which is also a standalone name meaning "my light"), and *Shalom* becomes *Shalomik* or *Sholi*. In Sanskrit, *Jyoti* becomes *Jyoti-bai* or *Jyot*, and *Nakshatra* becomes *Nakshi* or *Kshu*.

### Nickname Combinations for Sibling Sets

When naming multiple children with celestial themes, nickname compatibility becomes an important consideration. A set of *Orion*, *Lyra*, and *Vega* could use the nicknames *Rio*, *Rae*, and *Veg*—all one-syllable, all ending in consonants, creating a cohesive sound. A set of *Luna*, *Stella*, and *Nova* could use *Lu*, *Stell*, and *No*—again, all one-syllable, all preserving the core vowel of the original name.

For families who want more distinctive nicknames, a set of *Cassiopeia*, *Andromeda*, and *Persephone* could use *Cass*, *Drom*, and *Peri*—each nickname extracting a different portion of the full name while maintaining a shared thematic identity.

## Middle Name Suggestions for Celestial Names

### Harmonic Pairings

The best middle name pairings for celestial names consider both sound and meaning. *Luna Rose* flows smoothly because the soft *n* and *l* sounds of *Luna* transition naturally into the liquid *r* of *Rose*. *Orion James* works because the three-syllable *Orion* is balanced by the crisp, two-syllable *James*, creating a rhythmic contrast that prevents the name from feeling overly ornate. *Stella Grace* is a classic pairing that has remained popular for decades, combining the celestial theme with a virtue name that reinforces the luminous quality of *Stella*.

### Matching Celestial Sets

The most common approach to sibling celestial naming is to use names from the same tradition or theme. A mixed-Greek-and-Roman set could include *Luna* (Roman moon goddess), *Orion* (Greek hunter constellation), *Stella* (Latin star), and *Lyra* (Greek lyre constellation). This approach creates thematic cohesion while avoiding the repetitiveness of using only one language family.

Some families prefer complementary names that share a theme without being overtly celestial. *Orion* and *River* pair the cosmic with the terrestrial, creating a name set that spans two domains of nature. *Luna* and *Rose* pair the nocturnal with the floral, creating a set that balances light and growth. *Stella* and *Jade* pair the celestial with the mineral, creating a set that spans two categories of natural beauty.

### Gender-Balanced Sets

For families with mixed-gender children, celestial names offer particular flexibility. *Orion* (masculine) and *Luna* (feminine) are perhaps the most popular gender-balanced celestial pair, and their popularity is well-deserved: the names complement each other phonetically (both end in vowel sounds, both have three syllables) and thematically (both are celestial bodies with rich mythological associations).

*Lyra* (feminine) and *Vega* (unisex, traditionally masculine) create a pair that's less obvious but equally harmonious. *Stella* (feminine) and *Sol* (masculine) pair two Latin words for celestial bodies, creating a set that feels classical and timeless. *Nova* (unisex) and *Astra* (unisex) create a pair that's gender-neutral by design, with both names working equally well for any child.

### Extended Sibling Sets

For families with three or more children, celestial naming becomes more challenging. A three-child set might include *Luna*, *Orion*, and *Stella*—a combination that balances gender, syllable count, and cultural origin. A four-child set might include *Luna*, *Orion*, *Lyra*, and *Nova*—spanning two genders, three syllable counts, and multiple language families.

A five-child set is rare but possible: *Luna*, *Orion*, *Stella*, *Lyra*, and *Astra* creates a set where each name is distinct in sound and origin while sharing a common celestial theme. The key to success with extended sets is ensuring that each name has a distinct phonetic identity—no two names should start with the same letter or have the same syllable pattern.

## FAQ

### Q: Are celestial names too common now?
A: While names like *Luna*, *Stella*, and *Orion* have become popular, the broader category of celestial names remains relatively uncommon. According to 2025 SSA data, only approximately 14,500 of the 3,600,000+ babies born in the United States received a celestial-themed name—roughly 0.4% of all births. Even the most popular celestial names rank outside the top 50 overall, meaning your child is unlikely to share a name with a classmate. If you choose a less common name like *Cassiopeia*, *Lyra*, or *Vega*, you're selecting something truly distinctive.

### Q: Can I use a celestial name from a culture that isn't my heritage?
A: This is a nuanced question that depends on your specific situation. Using a name from a culture you have a genuine connection to—through ancestry, marriage, adoption, or deep study—is generally fine. Using a name from a culture you have no connection to, particularly if that culture has been marginalized or exploited, requires careful consideration. Names like *Hokule'a* (Hawaiian) or *Tashunka* (Lakota) carry cultural and spiritual significance that extends beyond their aesthetic appeal. If you're drawn to such names, engage with the culture respectfully, learn about the name's meaning and context, and consider whether you're prepared to honor that context throughout your child's life.
