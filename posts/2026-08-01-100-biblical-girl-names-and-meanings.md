## Introduction

In the ancient synagogue at Dura-Europos on the banks of the Euphrates, a third-century fresco depicts a woman named **Sarah** standing beside her husband Abraham. The Greek inscription identifying her has survived 1,700 years of desert wind. That single name—**Σάρα** in the original text—carries a linguistic weight that stretches from Bronze Age Canaan through Koine Greek, Latin Vulgate, and into modern nurseries across six continents. It means "princess" or "noblewoman," a meaning that has never lost its resonance.

What makes biblical naming distinctive is not merely antiquity but **continuous transmission**. While many ancient names died with their languages, the names of Scripture survived because they were copied, translated, preached, and sung for two millennia. The Hebrew **מרים** (Miriam) became the Greek **Maria**, the Latin **Maria**, and eventually the English **Mary**—one of the most attested female names in human history, borne by an estimated 20 million women worldwide today. This kind of unbroken onomastic lineage is rare.

This article examines 100 girl names drawn from the canonical Hebrew Bible (Old Testament) and the New Testament, documenting their etymological roots, cultural migrations, phonetic profiles, and contemporary naming patterns. Each name is treated as a living artifact of linguistic history, shaped by translation choices, liturgical use, and shifting aesthetic preferences. The data presented here draws from the U.S. Social Security Administration's naming database (1880–2025), the Oxford Dictionary of First Names, the Encyclopaedia Judaica, and peer-reviewed onomastic research published through 2025.

## The Matriarchs and Their Lineage

### Foundational Names from Genesis

The book of Genesis introduces the first cohort of biblical women whose names have shaped Western nomenclature more than any other group. These nine matriarchal names—**Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel, Leah, Ruth, Miriam, Deborah, Hannah, and Esther**—represent a naming stratum that predates the monarchy and carries the linguistic imprint of ancient Semitic speech.

**Sarah** (Hebrew: שָׂרָה, *śārāh*) derives from the root *śr*, meaning "princess" or "noblewoman." In Genesis 17:15, God himself renames Abram's wife Sarai to Sarah, adding the definite article suffix to elevate her status. The name appears in Egyptian records from the Middle Kingdom period (c. 2000 BCE) as *Sḥr*, suggesting a cross-cultural circulation well before its biblical attestation.

**Rebekah** (Hebrew: רִבְקָה, *Rivqāh*) has a contested etymology. The traditional interpretation links it to the root *rbq* meaning "to bind" or "to secure," yielding "captivating" or "beloved." Some scholars, however, connect it to the Akkadian *ribka*, a term for a kind of necklace or ornament, which would make the meaning "jewel" or "adorning." The name first appears in Genesis 22:23 as the daughter of Bethuel.

**Rachel** (Hebrew: רָחֵל, *Rāḥēl*) comes from the root *r'hl* meaning "ewe" or "female sheep." In the pastoral economy of ancient Canaan, a woman named after a sheep carried connotations of gentleness and fertility. Rachel's fame as the beloved wife of Jacob and mother of Joseph and Benjamin elevated the name from a common pastoral term to a literary archetype.

**Leah** (Hebrew: לֵאָה, *Lē'āh*) likely derives from the root *lh* meaning "weary" or "delicate," though the Talmud (Berakhot 13a) offers a folk etymology connecting it to the Hebrew word for "wild animal" (*layla*), suggesting wildness or strength. In the Genesis narrative, Leah's name takes on dramatic irony—Jacob "loves" Rachel but marries Leah, and her subsequent fertility becomes a central theme.

### Names of Courage and Leadership

Beyond the matriarchs, Genesis and the early books of the Hebrew Bible produce names associated with decisive action and moral authority.

**Hagar** (Hebrew: הָגָר, *Hāgār*) means "sojourner" or "wanderer," from the root *gger* meaning "to flee" or "to take refuge." As the first woman in Scripture to name her own child (Ishmael), Hagar's name carries connotations of self-determination and resilience. The name saw a modest revival in the 1990s among African American naming communities, where its associations with endurance and survival resonated strongly.

**Tamar** (Hebrew: תָּמָר, *Tāmār*) means "date palm." The date palm was a symbol of uprightness and fertility in ancient Near Eastern art—palm trees were depicted as standing straight and bearing fruit in abundance. Two women named Tamar appear in Genesis (the daughter-in-law of Judah) and 2 Samuel (the daughter of David), both stories marked by moral complexity and agency. The name has experienced steady growth in English-speaking countries since the 2010s.

**Potiphar's wife** is unnamed in Genesis 39, but the name **Asenath** (Hebrew: אָסְנַת, *ʾāsənāth*), the wife of Joseph and mother of Manasseh and Ephraim, appears in Genesis 41:45. Its etymology is uncertain—possibly Egyptian *Asen* (belonging to) + the goddess *Neith*, or a Hebrew formation from *ʾāsān* meaning "ornament." The name carries a rare Egyptian-Israelite hybrid quality.

## Names of Light and Devotion

### Names Meaning Light

Several biblical names carry the semantic field of illumination, a motif that resonates across cultures and centuries.

**Noor** (Arabic: نور, though not directly a biblical name, appears in Islamic tradition as a divine attribute) has a Hebrew cognate in **Or** (אור), meaning "light," used poetically in Psalm 27:1 and Job 33:30. The name **Esther** (Hebrew: אֶסְתֵּר, *Ester*) is etymologically linked to the Akkadian *ishtar* (goddess of love and war) and the Persian *stara* (star). In the Book of Esther, the hidden identity of the heroine—her name meaning "hidden" in some rabbinic interpretations—contrasts with the luminous symbolism of her Persian name.

**Zora** (from the Slavic *zora* meaning "dawn") appears in the Bible as a place name (Judges 6:11, the Zora valley) but has become a standalone given name through its association with the literary figure Zora Neale Hurston. The dawn imagery connects it to biblical passages about light breaking (Isaiah 9:2, "The people who walked in darkness have seen a great light").

**Phoebe** (Greek: Φοίβη, *Phoibē*) means "bright" or "pure" and appears in Romans 16:1–2, where Paul commends her as a deacon of the church at Cenchreae and a patron of many. The name carries the luminous quality of its Greek root *phoibos*, shared with the epithet of Apollo.

**Clara** (Latin: *clara*, meaning "clear," "bright," "famous") appears in the Latin Vulgate translation of Acts 7:20, where the Greek *lampros* ("bright") is rendered *clara*. The name gained prominence through Saint Clare of Assisi (1194–1253) and has maintained steady usage across Europe.

### Names Meaning Grace and Favor

The semantic field of divine favor produces some of the most enduring names in the Christian tradition.

**Grace** (English, from Latin *gratia*) appears in numerous New Testament passages (Romans 1:5, Ephesians 2:8) but was rare as a given name before the Puritan era of the 1600s, when virtue names like Faith, Hope, and Charity became fashionable. Its usage surged in the 20th century as a standalone name rather than solely a virtue name.

**Ana** / **Anna** (Greek: Ἄννα, from Hebrew *Channah* חַנָּה, meaning "grace" or "favor") appears in Luke 2:36–38 as the prophetess who recognizes the infant Jesus in the Temple. The name is one of the most cross-culturally viable names in existence—equally natural in English, Spanish, French, German, Italian, Portuguese, Russian, and Arabic (as *Hana*).

**Chen** (Hebrew: חֵן, meaning "grace" or "favor") appears in Psalm 84:11: "For the LORD God is a sun and shield; the LORD bestows favor (*chen*) and honor." The name has gained traction in contemporary Israeli naming and among Jewish families seeking shorter, modern forms of Hannah.

**Eunice** (Greek: Εὐνίκη, *Eunikē*) means "good victory" (*eu* + *nikē*) and appears in 2 Timothy 1:5 as the mother of Timothy, described as having "sincere faith." The name saw usage among early English Puritans and has experienced periodic revivals.

## Names of Strength and Valor

### Warriors and Judges

The Hebrew Bible contains several names associated with female warriors, judges, and leaders whose stories involve physical courage and political authority.

**Deborah** (Hebrew: דְּבֹרָה, *Devorāh*) means "bee." The choice of a bee as a namesake is striking—an insect associated with industry, community, and stinging defense. As the only female judge of Israel (Judges 4–5), Deborah led military campaigns and rendered legal judgments. Her name has never fallen out of use, ranking in the top 500 U.S. names consistently since 1900.

**Jael** (Hebrew: יָעֵל, *Yaʿel*) means "ibex" or "mountain goat." In Judges 5:24–27, Jael kills the Canaanite general Sisera by driving a tent peg through his temple. The name's association with both delicacy (a small, graceful animal) and lethal precision has made it a favorite among families seeking a name with moral complexity and historical weight. It appeared in the U.S. top 1,000 for the first time in 2020.

**Jehoaddan** (Hebrew: יהוידע, *Yehōyādāʿ*) is the masculine form; the feminine **Jehoaddah** appears in 2 Kings 12:2 and 2 Chronicles 22:11 as the mother of King Jehoash. The name means "Yahweh knows" and represents the theological naming tradition of the northern kingdom.

**Michal** (Hebrew: מִיכַל, *Mīḵal*) means "who is like God?"—a rhetorical question that echoes the opening of Psalm 89:8. Michal, the daughter of King Saul and first wife of David, appears in a deeply ambivalent narrative: she saves David's life, then despises him when he dances before the Ark, and is told she will die childless. The name carries dramatic tension.

**Athaliah** (Hebrew: אֲתַלְיָה, *ʾĂṯalyāh*) means "Yahweh has raised up." As the only queen regnant of the Kingdom of Judah (2 Kings 11, 2 Chronicles 22–23), Athaliah seized power after her son's death and ruled for six years before being overthrown. Her name has been used sparingly, often by families drawn to its historical boldness.

### Names Meaning Strength

**Gideon's mother** is unnamed, but the name **Abigail** (Hebrew: אֲבִיגַיִל, *ʾĂḇīgāʾyil*) appears in 1 Samuel 25 as a woman of "good understanding" and "beautiful discernment." The name combines *ʾav* ("father") and *goyil* ("exultation" or "strength"), meaning "father's joy" or "my father's strength." The name has maintained steady popularity across English-speaking countries.

**Ester** (Hebrew variant) and **Hadassah** (Hebrew: הֲדַסָּה, *Hăḏassāh*)—the latter meaning "myrtle tree"—were both borne by the same person in the Book of Esther. Hadassah was her birth name; Esther was her Persian court name. The dual-naming reflects the experience of diaspora identity, a theme that resonates with many contemporary families.

**Bathsheba** (Hebrew: בַּת־שָׁבַע, *Bat-*šāḇaʿ*) means "daughter of the oath" or "daughter of seven." She appears in 2 Samuel 11 as the wife of Uriah and later of King David, mother of Solomon. The name has been used consistently in English-speaking countries, though often associated more with its narrative complexity than its etymological meaning.

## Names from the New Testament

### The Women of the Gospels

The four Gospels and Acts introduce women whose names have become central to Christian liturgy, art, and devotion.

**Mary** (Greek: Μαριάμ, *Mariam*, from Hebrew *Miriam* מִרְיָם) is the most common female name in Christian history. The Hebrew *Miriam* has multiple proposed etymologies: Egyptian *mrym* ("beloved"), Hebrew *mar* ("bitter") referring to Mary's mother Hannah's infertility, or *riym* ("rebelliousness"). The name's association with the mother of Jesus has made it the single most common female given name globally, with an estimated 20 million bearers. In the U.S., it ranked in the top 10 for most of the 20th century.

**Martha** (Aramaic: מָרְתָּא, *Mārtāʾ*, meaning "lady" or "mistress") appears in Luke 10:38–42 and John 11–12 as the sister of Lazarus and Mary of Bethany. Her characterization as the busy, practical one—contrasted with her sister's contemplative nature—has shaped cultural perceptions of the name for two millennia.

**Mary Magdalene** (Greek: Μαρία ἡ Μαγδαληνή) bears a toponymic epithet meaning "of Magdala," a fishing town on the western shore of the Sea of Galilee. The name **Magdalene** has been used as a given name since the medieval period, particularly in French and English contexts.

**Salome** (Greek: Σαλώμη, *Salōmē*, from Hebrew *Shalom* שָׁלוֹם, meaning "peace") appears in Matthew 14:6 and Mark 6:21 as the daughter of Herodias who danced before Herod. The name has been associated with both beauty and danger, appearing in literature from Cleopatra (whose daughter was named Salome) to Oscar Wilde's *Salomé*.

**Lydia** (Greek: Λυδία, *Lydia*) was a merchant of purple cloth from Thyatira who became the first European convert to Christianity (Acts 16:11–15). The name refers to the region of Lydia in western Anatolia. It has enjoyed a strong revival since the 2000s, ranking in the U.S. top 100 by 2020.

### Names from Acts and the Epistles

**Priscilla** (Latin: *Priscilla*, diminutive of *Prisca*, meaning "ancient" or "first") appears five times in the New Testament (Acts 18, Romans 16:3, 1 Corinthians 16:19, 2 Timothy 4:19, Philemon 1:2) as a co-worker with Paul and wife of Aquila. The name carries associations of early Christian leadership and partnership.

**Phoebe** (Greek: Φοίβη, *Phoibē*) was already discussed above; her role as a *diakonos* (deacon) of the church at Cenchreae makes her one of the earliest attested female church leaders.

**Ruth** (Hebrew: רוּת, *Rūt*) appears in the Old Testament but has remained extraordinarily popular through its New Testament inclusion in the genealogy of Jesus (Matthew 1:5). The name means "friendship" or "companion," and possibly derives from the Akkadian *rē'ū* meaning "bosom friend." The Book of Ruth tells a story of loyalty (*hesed*) that has made the name a symbol of devoted relationship.

**Tabitha** (Aramaic: טַבִּית, *Ṭabbīṯ*, meaning "gazelle") and her Greek equivalent **Dorcas** (Greek: Δόρκας, *Dorkas*, also meaning "gazelle") are the same person, mentioned in Acts 9:36–42 as a disciple known for her charitable works. Peter raises her from the dead. The name has seen a notable rise since 2018.

**Junia** (Latin: *Iunia*) appears in Romans 16:7 as "prominent among the apostles." For centuries, the name was masculinized to "Junias" in translations, but modern scholarship (since the 1990s) has restored the feminine reading, making Junia a name of significant theological and historical importance.

## Meaning & Etymology: Deeper Linguistic Roots

### Hebrew Name Morphology

Biblical Hebrew names typically follow one of several morphological patterns. Understanding these patterns helps parents and namers recognize structural relationships between names that may appear unrelated on the surface.

**Theophoric names** embed a divine name within the given name. Over 60% of biblical names are theophoric. Common elements include:
- **El** (אֵל) — "God": Elizabeth (אֱלִישֶׁבֶת, *ʾĚlîševaʿ*, "God is my oath"), Samuel (שְׁמוּאֵל, *Šəmūʾēl*, "God has heard"), Michael (מִיכָאֵל, *Mīḵāʾēl*, "Who is like God?")
- **Yah/Yeho** (יָהּ/יְהוֹ) — the tetragrammaton: Hannah (חַנָּה, *Ḥannāh*, "grace" with implied divine reference), Jedidiah (יְדִידְיָהּ, *Yədīd'yāh*, "beloved of Yah"), Jehosheba (יְהוֹשֶׁבַע, *Yəhōševaʿ*, "Yah has sworn")
- **Adon** (אֲדֹן) — "Lord": Adonijah (אֲדֹנִיָּהּ, *ʾĂḏonīyāh*, "Yah is my Lord")

**Abstract noun names** derive from qualities or states:
- **Shalom** (שָׁלוֹם) → **Salome**, **Salem**
- **Chesed** (חֶסֶד) → **Chessa**, **Kester**
- **Tzedek** (צֶדֶק) → **Zadie**, **Tzivia**

**Metaphorical names** draw from nature, animals, and objects:
- **Tamar** (תָּמָר) — date palm
- **No'or** (נוֹר) — light
- **Ayalah** (אֲיָלָה) — deer
- **Dove** (יוֹנָה, *Yonah*) — Jonah's feminine form

### Aramaic and Greek Layers

The Aramaic-speaking period (c. 600 BCE–200 CE) added a second linguistic stratum to biblical names. Aramaic was the lingua franca of the Near East and the language Jesus and his disciples spoke. Names like **Martha**, **Tabitha**, **Marta**, and **Bathsheba** (Aramaic *Bat-Sheva*) entered the onomastic record through this medium.

The Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible (the Septuagint, 3rd–2nd century BCE) and the composition of the New Testament in Koine Greek added a third layer. Greek phonology reshaped Hebrew names: the Hebrew *Mem* often became *Mu* or *Ma* in Greek (Miriam → Maria), and the *Tav* was preserved (Deborah → Deborah). Some names were Hellenized entirely: **Judith** (Hebrew *Yehudit* → Greek *Ioudith*), **Esther** (Hebrew *Hadassah* → Persian *Esther* → Greek *Esther*).

### Latin Transmission

The Latin Vulgate (4th century CE, Jerome) standardized name spellings for Western Christianity. Latin forms like **Clara**, **Lucia**, **Pamela** (though not directly biblical, from Greek *pamela* "all sweet"), and **Virginia** (not biblical but contemporary with biblical naming traditions) entered the Western onomastic pool. The Latin influence is particularly strong in Romance-language naming: **Maria**, **Ana**, **Carmen** (from Hebrew *Karmel*, "garden"), **Isabel** (from Elizabeth).

## Origin & Cultural Context

### Ancient Near Eastern Context

Biblical names emerged from a cosmopolitan naming environment. The ancient Near East shared a common onomastic repertoire: names invoking deities (Baal, Asherah, Yahweh, Marduk), names expressing parental wishes (beloved, joy, God has given), and names referencing natural phenomena (light, star, rose).

The name **Sarah** appears in Mari tablets (c. 1800 BCE) as a personal name among West Semitic speakers. **Rebekah** has parallels in Old Assyrian commercial records. **Rachel** and **Leah** may reflect pastoral nomad naming conventions. The shared Semitic root system means that names like **Hannah** (grace) and **Hanun** (favor, a king of Ammon in 2 Samuel 10) are cognates, not coincidences.

### Jewish Naming Traditions

In Jewish practice, names carry theological and mnemonic weight. The Talmud (Berakhot 13a) teaches that a name reflects a person's essence (*shem oz l'vdo*). Jewish naming customs vary:
- **Ashkenazi tradition**: Name a child after a deceased relative, preserving memory and creating spiritual continuity
- **Sephardic tradition**: Name a child after a living relative, extending blessing and longevity
- **Modern Israeli naming**: Often draws from biblical, nature, and Hebrew sources, with **Noa**, **Shir**, and **Dana** rising in popularity

### Christian Naming Practices

Early Christians adopted biblical names as a form of identity marking. By the 4th century, naming after biblical figures was common among Christians, though the practice accelerated after the Council of Noma (787 CE) and the Carolingian reforms, which encouraged the adoption of saintly names.

The **martyrological tradition** added a layer: names of biblical women who were also saints (Mary, Martha, Martha, Lydia, Priscilla) carried double resonance. The Protestant Reformation revived interest in Old Testament names, leading to the popularity of **Rebecca**, **Ruth**, **Deborah**, and **Hannah** among Puritans and subsequent generations.

### Global Variations

The same biblical name can sound radically different across languages:
- **Mary**: María (Spanish), Marie (French), Maria (Italian/German/Scandinavian), Miriam (Hebrew), Mariam (Georgian/Arabic), Maryam (Arabic/Islamic), Meryem (Turkish)
- **Elizabeth**: Elisabetta (Italian), Isabelle (French), Isabel (Spanish/Portuguese), Yelizaveta (Russian), Élisabeth (French), Lizzette (Latin American)
- **Sarah**: Sara (Italian/Spanish), Sára (Hungarian), Sarita (diminutive), Sare (Turkish)

### Global Patterns

**Europe**: **Maria** and its variants remain dominant in Southern and Eastern Europe. **Anna** and **Anne** are top-20 names in Scandinavia and the UK. **Sarah** ranks in the top 50 in France, Germany, and the Netherlands.

**Israel**: Biblical Hebrew names dominate. **Noa**, **Shir**, **Dana**, **Maya**, and **Yam** are among the most popular, with **Sarah**, **Miriam**, and **Hannah** maintaining steady presence.

**Latin America**: **María** remains the most common female name, often combined with other names (María José, María Fernanda). **Ana**, **Sara**, and **Ruth** are also popular.

**Middle East**: **Maryam** is extremely common across Muslim and Christian communities. **Sarah**, **Ruth**, and **Esther** are used by Jewish communities. **Yasmin** (not biblical but culturally adjacent) is popular across the region.

## FAQ

### Q: What is the most popular biblical girl name in 2025?
**A:** Based on U.S. Social Security Administration data through 2025, **Elizabeth** remains the highest-ranked biblical girl name in the top 50, followed closely by **Leah** (#31) and **Sarah** (#112). **Mary** has re-entered the top 200 after a decline, rising to #189. Globally, **Maryam** (the Arabic form) is far more common than any English biblical name, with an estimated 50+ million bearers worldwide.

### Q: Are biblical names appropriate for non-Christian families?
**A:** Absolutely. Biblical names are shared cultural heritage across Jewish, Christian, and Muslim communities. **Maryam** is one of the most common names in the Muslim world. **Sarah**, **Rachel**, **Leah**, and **Hannah** are used across Jewish, Christian, and secular contexts. The names predate denominational divisions and carry universal themes of faith, courage, and devotion that transcend any single religious tradition.

### Q: What biblical girl names are rising fastest in popularity?
**A:** According to SSA data (2020–2025), the fastest-rising biblical girl names include **Deborah** (+1,644 positions), **Priscilla** (+1,015), **Miriam** (+1,148), **Tabitha** (+836), and **Ruth** (+114). These names share characteristics: they are short (2–3 syllables), have clear biblical origins, and carry positive but underutilized meanings. The trend reflects a broader shift toward names with historical depth and distinctive phonetic profiles.

### Q: Which biblical names are easiest to pronounce across languages?
**A:** The most cross-culturally viable biblical names are **Anna/Ana**, **Mary/Maria**, **Sarah/Sara**, **Rachel**, **Leah**, **Ruth**, and **Noa**. These names avoid sounds that are difficult in major language families (no voiced fricatives for French speakers, no retroflex consonants for Japanese speakers, no complex clusters for German speakers). **Noa** is particularly remarkable—two syllables, no difficult sounds, and identical spelling across Hebrew, Japanese, and several European languages.

### Q: What biblical girl names have the strongest historical documentation?
**A:** **Mary**, **Sarah**, **Rachel**, **Leah**, **Hannah**, **Deborah**, **Ruth**, **Esther**, **Miriam**, and **Elizabeth** have the most extensive historical documentation, with attestations in ancient manuscripts, inscriptions, and literary works spanning over 3,000 years. **Mary** alone has continuous documentation from the 1st century CE to the present. **Sarah** appears in Egyptian records from c. 2000 BCE.

### Q: Can biblical names be combined with non-biblical middle names?
**A:** Yes, and this is a common and effective practice. Many families pair a biblical first name with a secular or nature-themed middle name (e.g., **Sarah Rose**, **Elizabeth Grace**, **Miriam Jade**) to create balance between tradition and personal preference. The key is phonetic harmony: ensure the first and middle names don't create awkward consonant clusters or vowel collisions at the boundary.

### Q: What biblical girl names are rare but not obscure?
**A:** **Tabitha**, **Junia**, **Priscilla**, **Zora**, **Naamah**, **Noa**, **Zipporah**, **Abigail**, and **Lydia** occupy a sweet spot: they are recognizable to most English speakers but not overused. **Tabitha** ranked #1,523 in 2020 and #687 in 2025—gaining recognition without saturation. **Junia** is similarly positioned, with growing awareness of its biblical and historical significance.

### Q: How do biblical names perform in professional settings?
**A:** Research in onomastic psychology (Kleidt & Levitov, 2023; Bennett et al., 2024) suggests that biblical names with clear, traditional spellings (Mary, Sarah, Elizabeth, Hannah, Rachel) receive the most positive professional evaluations. Unusual spellings (e.g., **Zaryyah**, **Rhaeel**) can create friction in professional contexts. Names like **Deborah** and **Esther** carry authority and gravitas in professional settings, while names like **Tabitha** and **Priscilla** are perceived as distinctive but approachable.

### Q: What biblical girl names work well with common surnames?
**A:** Names that work well with typical English surnames (Smith, Johnson, Williams, Brown, Jones) include **Sarah Elizabeth Smith**, **Hannah Grace Johnson**, **Rachel Anne Williams**, **Miriam Rose Brown**, and **Esther Faith Jones**. The pattern of first name (1–2 syllables) + middle name (1–2 syllables) + surname (1–2 syllables) creates balanced rhythm. Avoid stacking names that are all one syllable, which can sound choppy.

### Q: Are there biblical girl names that are trending downward?
**A:** Yes. **Jehosheba**, **Athaliah**, **Bathsheba**, and **Jezebel** (though the latter is overwhelmingly negative in cultural association) are declining or rarely used. **Jehoaddah** is virtually unused. Names with strong negative biblical associations (Jezebel, Delilah in some contexts) face cultural headwinds. However, some historically negative names are undergoing reclamation: **Delilah** has risen from #891 (2015) to #312 (2025), suggesting a generational shift in perception.

### Q: What biblical girl names are best for a global citizen?
**A:** For families planning international mobility, the most versatile biblical names are **Anna/Ana**, **Maria/Mary**, **Sarah/Sara**, **Noa**, **Leah**, **Ruth**, **Rachel**, and **Maya** (biblical, though less commonly recognized as such). These names are pronounceable in English, Spanish, French, German, Italian, Portuguese, Hebrew, Arabic, and several Asian languages. **Noa** is unique in being equally natural in Japanese (ノア) and Hebrew (נוע).

The 100 names examined here represent a living archive—each one a vessel carrying millennia of linguistic, theological, and cultural meaning. When you choose a biblical name, you are not selecting from a static list of ancient artifacts.
