## Introduction

In 410 AD, when Roman legions withdrew from Britain, the stone inscriptions carved into standing monuments across what is now southern Wales and western England tell a surprising story about identity. The names etched into those stones—Voteporix, Cogidubnus, Catigern—belonged to a population that was simultaneously Romanized and stubbornly Celtic. Within two centuries, as Latin receded and Anglo-Saxon settlements pushed westward, a linguistic reclamation began. Celtic nomenclature didn't disappear; it retreated into the highlands and islands, where it would be preserved by monastic scribes in Ireland and Wales, eventually surfacing centuries later in forms we recognize today.

This survival story is precisely what makes Celtic boy names compelling for contemporary parents. These aren't invented traditions or romanticized fabrications. They are living linguistic artifacts—names that survived the collapse of empires, the imposition of foreign languages, and centuries of cultural suppression, only to resurge in the early twenty-first century. The onomastic data bears this out: according to U.S. Social Security Administration records, seven names of Celtic origin appeared in the top 200 boy names for 2023, with Finn climbing from outside the top 500 in 1990 to #56 by 2023—a trajectory that mirrors a broader cultural shift toward names with perceived historical depth and cross-cultural usability.

The Celtic linguistic sphere encompasses six distinct branches, each with its own phonological signature and naming tradition. Irish and Scottish Gaelic (Goidelic languages) favor slender consonants and vowel combinations that produce a lyrical, open quality. Welsh, Breton, and Cornish (Brythonic languages) preserve the p-Celtic consonant shifts and complex initial mutations that distinguish them from their Goidelic cousins. Manx, nearly extinct until a revival movement in the twentieth century, occupies a unique position between the two branches. Together, they offer a naming reservoir far broader than the limited set of names most parents encounter in mainstream baby name books.

What follows is a curated survey of one hundred names drawn from across the Celtic world. The selections are organized by linguistic and cultural origin, with attention paid to etymological accuracy, documented usage patterns, and contemporary viability. Every name included has a verifiable historical or linguistic pedigree—no speculative or invented forms.

## Irish Boy Names

### Classic Irish Names with Deep Roots

Ireland's naming tradition, preserved most completely in medieval manuscripts and ecclesiastical records, offers one of the richest corpora of Celtic boy names. Many of these names entered English-language usage through Irish immigration waves, particularly during the nineteenth century and again in the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries.

**Aidan** (AY-dən) — "The little fire one." Derived from the Old Irish *Aodhán*, a diminutive of *Aodh*, meaning "fire." Saint Aidan of Lindisfarne (c. 610–651) carried this name across the Irish Sea to Northumbria, establishing the monastery on Holy Island and playing a pivotal role in the Christianization of Anglo-Saxon England. The name's trajectory—from a seventh-century hagiographical figure to a mainstream American boy name (#237 in 2023 SSA data)—demonstrates the remarkable adaptability of Celtic nomenclature across cultural boundaries.

**Cillian** (KIL-ee-ən) — "Church" or "battle." The etymology is debated among scholars. The most widely accepted derivation traces it to the Old Irish *Cillín*, meaning "little church," referencing Saint Cillian of Northumbria. An alternative theory links it to the Celtic root *cil* meaning "bright-headed" or "battle." The double-L spelling variant, popularized by actor Cillian Murphy, has gained traction in English-speaking countries, while the single-L *Killian* remains more common in Ireland itself.

**Conall** (KON-əl) — "Strong wolf." A name of considerable heroic weight in Irish mythology. Conall Gulban, a fifth-century king of Ulster and son of Niall of the Nine Hostages, is a foundational figure in the Ulster Cycle. The name appears repeatedly in early Irish genealogies, and its phonetic structure—two syllables, hard consonants, a final schwa—makes it remarkably accessible to non-Irish speakers while retaining an unmistakably Celtic character.

**Darragh** (DAR-ə) — "Oak." The Irish *darach* denotes the oak tree, a symbol of strength, endurance, and sacred significance in Celtic cosmology. Oak groves were among the few natural features explicitly associated with druidic practice in classical accounts. The name's simplicity—two syllables, straightforward phonology—has contributed to its steady presence in Irish naming registries.

**Declan** (DEK-lən) — "Full of goodness" or "follower of Patrick." The etymology is uncertain. One derivation links it to the Old Irish *Declan*, possibly from *de* (good) and *clann* (children, followers). Another associates it with Saint Declan of Ardmore, an early missionary who predated Saint Patrick in Ireland. The name experienced a dramatic popularity surge in the United States, rising from outside the top 1,000 in 1990 to #228 by 2023—a textbook example of the "Celtic revival" in American naming patterns.

### Contemporary Irish Favorites

**Aiden** (AY-dən) — Often treated as a distinct name in modern usage, though etymologically it is a variant of Aidan. The *-en* spelling, popularized in the late twentieth century, gives the name a slightly different visual identity while preserving the "little fire" meaning. Aiden reached #78 on the 2023 SSA list, making it one of the most widely used Celtic-origin names in English-speaking countries.

**Cian** (KEE-ən) — "Ancient" or "enduring." One of the most phonetically elegant names in the Irish corpus: a single strong consonant, a long vowel, and a soft closing. The name belonged to Cian mac Tairrdelbach, a ninth-century king of Munster, and appears in the *Lebor Gabála Érenn* as the father of the legendary Lugh. Despite its brevity and melodic quality, Cian remained relatively uncommon in English-speaking countries until the twenty-first century, when it climbed to #387 in 2023—a trajectory that suggests growing familiarity with Irish orthography among non-Irish parents.

**Conor** (KON-ər) — "Lover of hounds" or "keen." From the Irish *Conchobhar*, a name of enormous significance in Irish literature. Conchobar mac Nessa was the legendary king of Ulster and the central figure in the *Táin Bó Cúailnge* (the Cattle Raid of Cooley), the longest and most elaborate narrative in the Ulster Cycle. The anglicized form Conor has been in steady use in English-speaking countries since the mid-twentieth century.

**Darragh** (DAR-ə) — See above.

**Finn** (FIN) — "Fair" or "white." From the Irish *Fionn*, most famously borne by Fionn mac Cumhaill (Finn McCool), the legendary warrior-poet of the Fianna. The name's monosyllabic structure, clean phonology, and cross-cultural usability have made it one of the fastest-climbing Celtic names in recent decades. Its presence on the SSA top 100 since 2015 reflects a broader trend toward short, internationally pronounceable Celtic names.

**Kieran** (KEER-ən) — "Little dark one." From the Irish *Ciáran*, itself possibly derived from *ciar* meaning "dark" or "black." Saint Kieran of Ossory (c. 490–544) founded several monasteries in County Offaly, and the name's hagiographical pedigree has kept it in continuous use in Ireland. The anglicized spelling Kieran (versus the Irish *Ciáran*) has facilitated its adoption in English-speaking contexts.

**Liam** (LEE-əm) — "Strong-willed warrior" or "protector." A shortened form of the Irish *Uilliam*, the Irish adaptation of William. Despite its Germanic root, Liam has become so thoroughly Irish in its cultural association that it is routinely classified as a Celtic name in onomastic surveys. It held the #1 position on the U.S. SSA boy names list for eight consecutive years (2016–2023), making it the most popular baby name of the twenty-first century's second decade regardless of origin.

**Oisín** (OSH-een) — "Little deer." From the Irish *Óisín*, the name of the most famous poet of the Fianna, son of Fionn mac Cumhaill. In Irish mythology, Oisín travels to Tír na nÓg (the Land of Youth) and returns after what he perceives as a brief visit to find three centuries have passed. The name's literary resonance and delicate phonetic quality—two syllables, a soft opening, a long vowel—make it a distinctive choice for parents seeking a name with mythological depth. The anglicized form *Owen* shares an unrelated etymology (Welsh *Ewene*, from *Ieuan*) but is sometimes conflated with Oisín due to phonetic similarity.

**Rory** (RAW-ree) — "Red king." From the Irish *Ruaidhrí*, a compound of *ruadh* (red) and *rí* (king). Multiple Irish kings bore this name, including Ruaidrí Ua Conchobair, the last High King of Ireland before the Norman invasion. The name's cheerful phonetics and accessible spelling have made it a staple of Irish-American naming culture since the mid-twentieth century.

**Shane** (SHAYN) — "God is gracious." The Irish form of John, derived from the Hebrew *Yohanan* via Latin *Iohannes* and Anglo-Norman * Jehan*. While its ultimate origin is Semitic rather than Celtic, Shane has been so thoroughly integrated into Irish naming tradition over eight centuries that it functions as a Celtic name in contemporary usage. It peaked in the U.S. top 100 during the 1980s and 1990s and remains a recognizable Irish association.

**Tadhg** (TJ-ig or TAVE-ig) — "Poet" or "philosopher." An Old Irish name of considerable antiquity, borne by numerous medieval Irish kings and scholars. The pronunciation remains a point of contention: the traditional Irish pronunciation approximates "TJ-ig" (with the *dh* silent), while some English-speaking families pronounce it "TAVE-ig." The name's rarity outside Ireland—#891 in the 2023 SSA data—makes it a distinctly Irish choice for parents seeking a name with deep cultural roots and minimal Anglophone competition.

## Scottish Gaelic Boy Names

### Ancient Scottish Names

Scotland's Gaelic naming tradition shares significant overlap with Irish nomenclature, reflecting the common linguistic and cultural origins of the two traditions. The Dál Riata kingdom, which spanned eastern Scotland and northeastern Ireland from the sixth to ninth centuries, operated as a bilingual Gaelic political entity that facilitated the exchange of names, saints' cults, and literary traditions across the North Channel.

**Alasdair** (AL-əz-dər) — The Scottish Gaelic form of Alexander, meaning "defender of the people." The name has been borne by numerous Scottish nobles and Jacobite figures, including Alasdair Mac Colla, a seventeenth-century military commander. The *-sair* ending is a distinctive Scottish Gaelic phonological marker that signals the name's regional origin to speakers familiar with the language.

**Calum** (KAL-əm) — The Scottish Gaelic form of Columba, meaning "dove." Saint Columba (521–597) founded the monastery on Iona in 563, establishing a religious and intellectual center that exerted enormous influence across northern Britain and beyond. The name Calum has remained in continuous Scottish use, particularly in the Highlands and Islands, where Gaelic naming traditions have persisted most strongly.

**Donnchad** (DON-ək) — "Brown-haired warrior" or "world ruler." From the Irish/Scottish Gaelic *Donnchadh*, a compound of *donn* (brown, dark) and *cath* (battle). Multiple Scottish kings bore related forms of this name, and the anglicized *Duncan* entered English usage through the same root. The Gaelic form Donnchad retains a phonological authenticity that the anglicized variant loses.

**Ewan** (YOO-ən) — A Scottish Gaelic rendering of Giovanni, ultimately from the Hebrew *Yohanan*. While its etymological chain is Semitic-Latin-Gaelic-English, Ewan has been so thoroughly absorbed into Scottish naming culture that it functions as a Scottish name in contemporary usage. The name experienced a revival in the late twentieth century, partly due to the fame of actor Ewan McGregor.

**Fergus** (FUR-gəs) — "Man of strength" or "force of man." From the Scottish Gaelic *Fergus*, itself derived from the Common Celtic root *verg-*, meaning "vigor" or "strength." Fergus mac Eirc was a legendary king of Dál Riata, and the name appears throughout Scottish genealogical records. Its two sharp syllables and strong consonant onset make it phonetically memorable and cross-culturally accessible.

**Lachlan** (LAK-lən) — "Land of the lakes" or "from Lapland." From the Old Norse *Langafjǫrðr*, brought to Scotland by Viking settlers who established control over the western islands and coastlines. The name's Norse-Gaelic hybrid origin reflects the complex cultural stratification of medieval Scotland. Lachlan has seen steady usage in Scotland and growing popularity in English-speaking countries more broadly.

**Malcolm** (MAL-kəm) — "Devotee of Saint Columba." From the Scottish Gaelic *Maol Coluim*, meaning "follower" or "devotee" of Columba. Nine Scottish kings bore this name, including Malcolm III (c. 1031–1093), whose reign and death in battle against the English are recorded in detail by contemporary chroniclers. The name's royal pedigree and clean phonology have sustained its usage across centuries.

**Roddy** (RAH-dee) — A diminutive of Roderick, from the Gaelic *Ruaidhrí* (same root as Rory). The Scottish Gaelic form *Rudhraigh* produces the diminutive *Rudhraigheag*, which anglicized to Roddy. The name carries a lighter, more informal register than its full form and has been used in Scotland since at least the medieval period.

**Stuart** (STYOOR-t) — From the Old French *estuard*, meaning "steward." While its etymology is Franco-Norman rather than Celtic, the Stuart dynasty's association with Scotland—particularly the reigns of Mary, Queen of Scots, and the later British monarchs—has made the name culturally Scottish in the popular imagination. The name's continued popularity in Scotland and among the Scottish diaspora reflects this deep cultural entanglement.

### Modern Scottish Choices

**Angus** (ANG-gəs) — From the Scottish Gaelic *Aonghus*, meaning "one choice" or "unique strength." Aonghus mac Dubhshlaine was a thirteenth-century Lord of Islay, and the name's mythological resonance derives from Aengus Óg, the Celtic god of youth and love. The name's two syllables and clear consonant structure have made it a durable presence in Scottish naming registries.

**Callum** (KAL-əm) — Variant spelling of Calum. The double-L spelling is more common in English-speaking contexts, while the single-L *Calum* is preferred in Gaelic-language settings. Both forms are etymologically identical.

**Duncan** (DUHN-kən) — Anglicized form of Donnchad. The name's association with Shakespeare's *Macbeth* (where Duncan is the virtuous king murdered by his cousin) has given it a literary dimension that extends beyond its Gaelic origins. The name remains popular in Scotland and among Scottish diaspora communities.

**Finlay** (FIN-lə) — "Fair warrior" or "white hero." From the Scottish Gaelic *Fionnlaidh*, a compound of *fionn* (fair, white) and *latha* (bright, clear). The name's heroic connotations and clean phonology have sustained its usage in Scotland for centuries.

**Iain** (AYN) — The Scottish Gaelic form of John. Pronounced with a single syllable, the name's Gaelic spelling signals its cultural origin more explicitly than the anglicized *Ian*, though both share the same etymology. Iain has been a staple of Scottish naming since the medieval period.

**Lorne** (LORNE) — From the Gaelic *Lunnainn*, the name of the Firth of Lorn and the associated region in western Scotland. The name's toponymic origin places it in the tradition of Scottish names derived from landscape features—a pattern that recurs throughout Celtic onomastics.

**Murdo** (MER-doh) — From the Scottish Gaelic *Murchadh*, meaning "sea warrior." A compound of *muir* (sea) and *cath* (battle). The name's maritime connotations reflect the seafaring culture of the western Scottish islands, where Gaelic naming traditions have persisted most vividly.

**Ross** (RAHS) — From the Gaelic *Ros*, meaning "promontory" or "peninsula." A toponymic surname turned given name, Ross originates in the northern Scottish region of Ross-shire. The name's brevity and geographic specificity give it a distinctive character within Scottish nomenclature.

## Welsh Boy Names

### Traditional Welsh Names

Welsh naming patterns differ significantly from their Irish and Scottish Gaelic counterparts. The Welsh language, a Brythonic Celtic tongue, preserves the p-Celtic consonant shifts that distinguish it from the q-Celtic Goidelic languages. Welsh names often feature initial consonant mutations, complex consonant clusters, and a preference for nature-derived and patronymic naming structures.

**Aled** (AL-eth) — "Bright" or "radiant." A name of considerable antiquity in Welsh tradition, borne by Saint Aled (also known as Alloid), a sixth-century bishop. The name's single syllable and soft ending make it phonetically accessible to non-Welsh speakers while retaining its Welsh character.

**Bran** (BRAN) — "Raven" or "crow." From the Welsh *bran*, a word that also appears in the Irish *bran* and the Common Celtic *brano-*. In Welsh mythology, Branwen ferch Llŷr is a central figure in the *Mabinogion*, and the name Bran carries strong associations with the ravens of Tower Hill—a tradition that connects Welsh mythology to British royal custom. The monosyllabic form is strikingly direct.

**Cadwallader** (kad-WAL-ə-der) — "Battle leader" or "leader of hosts." From the Welsh *Cadwvallawn*, a compound of *cad* (battle) and *gwallt* (hair, head). Cadwalladr ap Cadfan was a seventh-century king of Gwynedd, and the name's martial connotations have made it a recurring choice in Welsh history. The anglicized form *Cadwalader* appears in Shakespeare's *King Lear*.

**Carys** (KAR-iss) — "Love." While traditionally a girl's name in Wales, Carys has occasionally been used for boys in contemporary Welsh naming practice. The name's direct semantic meaning—"love"—places it in the tradition of virtue names, a category that is more common in Welsh onomastics than in Irish or Scottish Gaelic naming.

**Gareth** (GAR-eth) — One of the most widely known Welsh names in the English-speaking world, Gareth is a figure from the Arthurian tradition, the nephew of King Arthur and one of the Knights of the Round Table. The name's literary pedigree and clean phonology have made it a durable choice. Its appearance in Thomas Malory's *Le Morte d'Arthur* and earlier Welsh sources like *Perceval* demonstrates the name's deep roots in Celtic literary tradition.

**Gwydion** (GWI-dhee-on) — A name of enormous significance in Welsh mythology. Gwydion is a magician and trickster figure in the *Mabinogion*, the uncle and adoptive father of Lleu Llaw Gyffes. His name may derive from the Celtic root *gwyd-* meaning "illusion" or "magic." The name's three syllables and distinctive initial GW- cluster make it a phonologically complex but rewarding choice for parents willing to commit to a name with deep mythological roots.

**Iolo** (EE-loh) — A diminutive of Iorwerth, meaning "born leader" or "authentic ruler." Iolo Morganwg (1747–1826), the real name of Edward Williams, was a Welsh poet and antiquarian who played a crucial role in the Welsh cultural revival of the eighteenth century. He is credited with popularizing many eisteddfodic traditions and with shaping the modern romantic conception of Welsh identity. The name Iolo carries this cultural weight.

**Llewelyn** (LOO-ə-lin) — From the Welsh *Llywelyn*, a name borne by several princes of Gwynedd, most notably Llywelyn ap Gruffudd (Llywelyn the Last), the last native Prince of Wales before the English conquest. The name's meaning is debated—possibly "leader" or "lion-like"—but its historical resonance is undeniable. The anglicized short form *Llew* is increasingly used as a standalone name.

**Owain** (OH-ayn) — A Welsh form of the Latin *Iovianus*, derived from *Jove* (the Roman god Jupiter). Owain is a major figure in Welsh mythology—Owain mab Urien is the protagonist of the Middle Welsh romance *Owain, or the Lady of the Fountain*, one of the Three Welsh Romances of the *Mabinogion*. The name's Arthurian associations and clean phonology have made it popular across the Celtic world and beyond.

**Rhys** (REES) — "Enthusiasm" or "passion." From the Welsh *Rhys*, a name borne by multiple figures in Welsh history, most notably Rhys ap Gruffudd (The Lord Rhys), a twelfth-century ruler of Deheubarth who was one of the most powerful Welsh leaders before the Norman conquest. The name's single syllable, clear pronunciation, and historical weight make it one of the most viable Welsh names for international use.

**Taliesin** (tal-EE-sin) — "Bright face" or "shining brow." Taliesin is the name of the most famous poet of the Welsh tradition, a sixth-century figure whose attributed poems form one of the oldest bodies of vernacular literature in Western Europe. The name's literary significance is comparable to that of Homer in Greek tradition or Ossian in Irish tradition. Using this name signals a serious commitment to Celtic cultural heritage.

**Torin** (TOR-in) — "Chief" or "leader." From the Irish *Toirdealbhach*, but adopted into Welsh naming practice through the shared Celtic linguistic heritage. The name's meaning and phonetic structure make it a bridge between Irish and Welsh naming traditions.

**Urien** (YOO-ree-en) — A sixth-century king of Rheged, a Brythonic kingdom in what is now Cumbria and southern Scotland. Urien is a significant figure in Welsh poetry and mythology, the father of Owain and a contemporary of the poet Taliesin. The name's historical specificity and Brythonic origin make it a distinctive choice within the Welsh naming corpus.

### Contemporary Welsh Names

**Bronwen** (BRON-ən) — Traditionally a girl's name meaning "white breast" or "fair breast," Bronwen has occasionally been used for boys in contemporary Welsh practice. The name's association with Saint Bronwen and its melodic quality make it a notable example of Welsh naming flexibility.

**Cian** (KEE-ən) — While primarily an Irish name, Cian is also used in Wales, reflecting the cross-Celtic exchange of nomenclature that has characterized the British Isles for millennia. The name's shared Celtic root makes it naturally transmissible between the languages.

**Dylan** (DIL-ən) — "Son of the sea" or "born from the ocean." From the Welsh *Dylan ail Ton*, a mythological figure associated with the sea in Welsh poetry. Dylan is the son of the goddess *Arianrhod* in the *Mabinogion*, and his name appears in some of the oldest Welsh poetic fragments. The name's popularity in English-speaking countries (#78 in the 2023 SSA data) reflects the successful cross-cultural migration of Welsh nomenclature.

**Emrys** (EM-riss) — The Welsh form of Ambrose, meaning "immortal." Emrys is best known as the Welsh name for Merlin—the *Merlinus* of Geoffrey of Monmouth's *Historia Regum Britanniae* is identified as Emrys in Welsh tradition. The name's mythological association and clean two-syllable structure make it a compelling choice.

**Gethin** (GEH-thin) — "Dark" or "swarthy." From the Welsh *gethyn*, a descriptor that functioned as a byname and eventually a given name. The name's semantic directness—simply meaning "dark"—reflects a naming tradition that valued descriptive clarity.

**Gwen** (GUEN) — Traditionally a girl's name meaning "white" or "fair," Gwen is occasionally used for boys in Welsh practice. The name's brevity and clear meaning make it a notable example of Welsh naming flexibility.

**Hywel** (HOO-wel) — From the Welsh *Hywel*, meaning "eloquent" or "gracious." Hywel Dda (Hywel the Good, c. 880–950) was a king of Deheubarth who unified much of Wales and is best known for compiling the Welsh legal codes that bore his name. The name's legal and governmental associations give it a distinctive civic dimension.

**Idris** (ID-riss) — "Ardent lord" or "passionate ruler." From the Welsh *Idris*, a name borne by a sixth-century king of Ergyng and by Idris Gawr, a legendary giant in Welsh tradition. The name's two syllables and clear phonology make it accessible while retaining its Welsh character.

**Ieuan** (YOO-ahn) — The Welsh form of John. Pronounced with a distinctive Welsh vowel quality, the name's spelling signals its cultural origin more explicitly than the anglicized *John* or Scottish *Iain*. Ieuan has remained a steady presence in Welsh naming registries.

**Mabon** (MAB-on) — From the Welsh *mapon*, meaning "son" or "young man." Mabon ap Modron is a figure in Welsh mythology, the son of the goddess Modron and a companion of King Arthur. The name's divine association and mythological depth make it a distinctive choice.

**Myfanwy** (miv-AN-vey) — Traditionally a girl's name meaning "my beloved," Myfanwy is occasionally used for boys in contemporary Welsh practice. The name's literary association with the Welsh poet Gwynfor Evans and its romantic meaning make it a notable example of Welsh naming traditions.

**Taliesin** (tal-EE-sin) — See above.

**Tudor** (TOO-dər) — From the Welsh *Tyddyr*, meaning "farmer" or "settler." The Tudor dynasty, which ruled England and Wales from 1485 to 1603, bears this name, though the family's Welsh origins predate their English accession. The name's royal associations and Welsh etymology give it a unique historical dimension.

**Gwenllian** (gwen-LLEE-ən) — A girl's name, but included here for completeness. Gwenllian ferch Gruffudd was a twelfth-century princess of Deheubarth who led a Welsh rebellion against Norman rule. Her name represents the Welsh tradition of warrior women.

## Breton, Cornish & Manx Boy Names

### Breton Names

Brittany (Breizh in Breton) was settled by Celtic speakers who migrated from Britain between the fifth and seventh centuries, likely fleeing Anglo-Saxon expansion. Breton names share significant overlap with Welsh nomenclature, reflecting this common Brythonic origin.

**Yann** (YAHN) — The Breton form of John. Pronounced with a single syllable and a distinctive Breton vowel quality, Yann is one of the most common names in Brittany. The name's persistence in a region where Breton language revival movements have been active since the 1950s makes it a symbol of cultural continuity.

**Mael** (MAHL) — "Chief" or "prince." From the Breton *mael*, a title that appears in numerous Breton place names and personal names. Mael I of Brittany was a ninth-century ruler, and the name's aristocratic connotations have sustained its use in Breton naming practice.

**Riwallon** (ree-WAH-lon) — "Little king" or "royal." From the Breton *ri* (king) and *gwallon* (little). The name's compound structure reflects a Breton naming tradition that valued descriptive precision. Riwallon is a relatively rare name outside Brittany, making it a distinctive choice for parents seeking a truly regional Celtic name.

**Yves** (EEV) — "Yew tree." From the Breton *ivinen*, the yew tree being a symbol of immortality and sacred significance in Celtic tradition. Saint Yves (1253–1303), the patron saint of Brittany, was a canon lawyer and bishop known for his justice and charity. The name's single syllable and saintly association make it a durable Breton choice.

### Cornish Names

Cornwall's Celtic linguistic tradition, a southern extension of the Brythonic branch, was nearly extinguished by the eighteenth century but has experienced a revival since the 1970s. Cornish names are often indistinguishable from Welsh names due to their shared linguistic origin, but several names have acquired distinct Cornish associations.

**Trevor** (TREV-ər) — From the Cornish *tres avor*, meaning "large farmstead" or "big homestead." The name's toponymic origin places it in the tradition of Celtic names derived from landscape features. Trevor entered English usage through Cornish migration to Wales and England during the medieval period.

**Angan** (AHNG-ən) — A Cornish name meaning "bell." The name's semantic connection to church bells reflects the deep association between Celtic Christianity and monastic settlement in Cornwall. Angan is rare outside Cornwall, making it a distinctly regional choice.

**Kernow** (KER-noh) — The Cornish word for Cornwall itself. Using a place name as a given name is a well-established tradition in Celtic onomastics, and Kernow represents the most direct possible connection to Cornish cultural identity. The name is extremely rare but symbolically potent.

**Lysander** (li-SAN-dər) — While not etymologically Cornish (it is Greek, meaning "liberator"), Lysander has been adopted into Cornish naming practice through the region's broader Celtic revival. The name's classical associations align with the Cornish romantic movement's interest in ancient Mediterranean cultures.

### Manx Names

The Manx language, a Goidelic Celtic language closely related to Irish and Scottish Gaelic, was declared extinct by UNESCO in 2009 before a vigorous revival movement restored it to classroom and community use. Manx names are often identical to or very close to their Irish and Scottish Gaelic counterparts.

**Gill** (GIL) — From the Manx *gille*, meaning "lad" or "young man." A descriptive byname that became a given name, Gill reflects the Manx tradition of short, functional names. The name's simplicity and directness make it accessible to non-Manx speakers.

**Kione** (KEY-nee) — "Head" or "top." From the Manx *kione*, a word that appears in numerous Manx place names. The name's toponymic associations connect it to the Manx landscape and its Celtic linguistic heritage.

**Ned** (NED) — A Manx diminutive of Edward, but with a distinctive regional usage pattern. The name's brevity and Manx association make it a small but notable entry in the Manx onomastic corpus.

**Peadar** (PAY-dər) — The Manx form of Peter. Pronounced with a distinctive Manx vowel quality, Peadar reflects the Goidelic linguistic heritage of the Isle of Man. ## Mythological & Legendary Celtic Names

### Names from Irish Mythology

Irish mythology preserves one of the richest corpora of personal names in the Celtic world. The figures of the Mythological Cycle, the Ulster Cycle, and the Fenian Cycle provide a vast reservoir of names that have been used, adapted, and reimagined across centuries.

**Aengus** (AYNG-gəs) — The full form of Aengus Óg, the Celtic god of youth, love, and poetic inspiration. Aengus is a figure of enormous significance in Irish mythology, and his name—possibly meaning "choice" or "one desire"—carries the weight of divine association. The anglicized form *Angus* is far more common, but Aengus offers a more mythologically direct connection.

**Bres** (BRESH) — A king of the Tuatha Dé Danann, the supernatural race that dominates the Mythological Cycle of Irish literature. Bres's name means "beauty" or "splendor," and his story—deposition, exile, and eventual restoration—reflects the complex political theology of early Irish mythology. The name's brevity and mythological weight make it a striking choice.

**Cermait** (ker-MIT) — "Fair-bodied." Cermait mac Maele was a figure in the Ulster Cycle, a companion of Conchobar mac Nessa and the son of the god Maponos. The name's semantic meaning and mythological associations give it a layered quality that rewards careful consideration.

**Lugh** (LOO) — The god of skill, light, and kingship, Lugh is one of the most important figures in the Celtic pantheon. His name, possibly derived from the Celtic root *lug-*, meaning "light" or "oath," is associated with the festival of Lughnasadh (August 1st). The monosyllabic form is stark and powerful, and its use as a given name connects the bearer directly to the Celtic divine tradition.

**Macha** (MAK-ə) — While traditionally a girl's name, Macha is included here for completeness. Macha is a complex figure in Irish mythology—a goddess associated with horses, sovereignty, and the founding of Armagh. Her story, particularly the curse she placed on the Ulster men, is one of the most dramatic narratives in the Ulster Cycle.

**Niall** (NEEL) — "Champion" or "cloud." Niall Noígíallach (Niall of the Nine Hostages) is the legendary ancestor of several Irish dynasties, including the O'Neills and O'Connors. His historical existence is debated, but his mythological significance is undeniable. The name Niall has remained in continuous Irish use and has entered broader English-speaking usage.

**Suibhne** (SWIV-nee) — "Swan-like" or "graceful." Suibhne Geilt was a figure in Irish mythology who was cursed to live as a wild man of the woods, transformed into a bird-like creature. The name's poetic meaning and mythological narrative give it a distinctive literary quality.

### Names from Welsh Mythology

**Branwen** (BRAN-wen) — Traditionally a girl's name, Branwen is the sister of Bran the Blessed and the central figure in one of the Four Branches of the *Mabinogion*. Her story—a tragic tale of marriage, exile, and war—makes her one of the most emotionally complex figures in Welsh mythology.

**Gwaithfek** (GWATH-fek) — "Battle at the ford." Gwaithfek was a figure in Welsh heroic poetry, and his name reflects the Welsh tradition of combat-themed naming. The name's compound structure and martial connotations place it in the tradition of Welsh warrior names.

**Gwrhyr** (GUR-hir) — "Great warrior" or "tall warrior." Gwrhyr Wcthceneut was a figure in Welsh mythology, a speaker of languages who served as an intermediary in the *Massacre of the Sons of Terbe*. The name's descriptive compound structure is characteristic of Welsh onomastic tradition.

**Pryderi** (PRID-er-ee) — "Anxiety" or "care." Pryderi fab Rhydderch is a central figure in the Second and Third Branches of the *Mabinogion*, the son of Rhydderch Hael and the father of Glendyrl. His name's semantic meaning—anxiety—adds a psychological dimension to his mythological role.

**Teyrnon** (tay-RNON) — "Noble lord" or "chief man." Teyrnon Twrf Liant was a figure in the Third Branch of the *Mabinogion*, the foster father of Pryderi. The name's aristocratic connotations and mythological association make it a distinctive Welsh choice.

## Nature-Inspired Celtic Names

### Botanical Names

Celtic naming traditions have a long association with the natural world. Trees, animals, and landscape features provided a rich source of names that reflected the intimate relationship between Celtic speakers and their environment.

**Alder** (AL-dər) — While not exclusively Celtic, the alder tree has significant associations with Celtic woodland lore. The name Alder entered English usage through Celtic place names and has been adopted into contemporary nature-inspired naming.

**Ash** (ASH) — The ash tree (*fraxinus*) holds particular significance in Celtic tradition. The Ogham alphabet, the early medieval Celtic writing system, includes a letter for the ash tree (*ailm*), associated with wisdom and protection. The name Ash connects the bearer to this deep linguistic and botanical tradition.

**Birch** (BERCH) — The birch tree (*betula*) is associated with purification and new beginnings in Celtic tradition. The name Birch, while more common as a surname, has been adopted as a given name in contemporary Celtic-inspired naming practice.

**Bramble** (BRAM-bəl) — A nature name that reflects the Celtic tradition of deriving names from the surrounding landscape. Bramble's thorny quality and seasonal persistence make it a distinctive botanical choice.

**Cedar** (SEE-dər) — While not originally Celtic, cedar has been adopted into Celtic-inspired naming through its association with sacred groves and ancient trees. The name's durability and cross-cultural resonance make it a viable nature name.

**Druid** (DROO-id) — From the Latin *druida*, the Greek *drūidās*, and the Gaulish *druwidās*, meaning "wise man" or "seer." The druids were the learned class of Celtic society, responsible for religious practice, legal knowledge, and oral tradition. Using this name is a direct reference to the Celtic intellectual tradition.

**Fern** (FERN) — A nature name with strong Celtic associations. Ferns were among the first plants to colonize Celtic landscapes after the last Ice Age, and their persistent greenery made them symbols of endurance. The name Fern is simple, clean, and botanically grounded.

**Hazel** (HAZ-əl) — While traditionally a girl's name, Hazel has been used for boys in Celtic-inspired naming practice. The hazel tree was associated with wisdom in Celtic tradition—the nine wells of wisdom in Irish mythology were said to be fed by the nuts of the sacred hazel tree.

**Ivy** (IV-ee) — Traditionally a girl's name, Ivy has been used for boys in contemporary Celtic practice. The ivy vine's evergreen nature and climbing habit made it a symbol of fidelity and endurance in Celtic tradition.

**Rowan** (ROH-ən) — "Little redhead" or "rowan tree." The rowan tree (*Sorbus aucuparia*) was one of the most sacred trees in Celtic tradition, associated with protection, inspiration, and the Otherworld. The name Rowan's popularity (#328 in 2023 SSA data) reflects the broader trend toward nature-inspired Celtic names.

**Thistle** (TIS-əl) — Scotland's national flower, the thistle, has been a symbol of Scottish resistance and resilience since the eleventh century. The name Thistle, while rare, connects the bearer to this potent national symbol.

**Yew** (YOO) — The yew tree (*Taxus baccata*) was one of the most sacred trees in Celtic tradition, associated with immortality, death, and rebirth. Yew groves were commonly found in Celtic sacred sites, and the wood was used for crafting bows and ritual objects. The name Yew is stark, monosyllabic, and deeply symbolic.

### Animal Names

**Bear** (BEAR) — The bear was a powerful symbol in Celtic mythology, associated with the goddess Artio and the warrior class. The name Bear, while more common in English-speaking countries generally, has strong Celtic associations through the bear cults of ancient Europe.

**Boar** (BOR) — The wild boar was a symbol of martial valor in Celtic tradition. Boar imagery appears on Celtic armor, jewelry, and coinage, and the boar hunt is a recurring motif in Celtic literature. The name Boar is direct and unapologetically martial.

**Fox** (FOKS) — The fox was associated with cunning and trickery in Celtic tradition, qualities embodied by the mythological figure of Lugh and the trickster god maponos. The name Fox connects to this complex symbolic tradition.

**Hawk** (HAWK) — The hawk was a symbol of vision and swift action in Celtic tradition. Hawk imagery appears in Celtic art and literature, and the bird's association with the Otherworld makes it a meaningful nature name.

**Otter** (OT-ər) — The otter was a sacred animal in Celtic tradition, associated with water deities and transformation. The name Otter, while unusual, connects to a deep Celtic zoological symbolism.

**Stag** (STAG) — The stag was one of the most important symbols in Celtic mythology, associated with masculinity, sovereignty, and the Otherworld. The name Stag connects to this rich symbolic tradition and carries a powerful natural image.

## Modern Celtic Revival Names

### Names Gaining Contemporary Momentum

The late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries have seen a significant revival of Celtic naming traditions, driven by several factors: the globalization of Celtic cultural identity, the influence of Celtic music and literature, and a broader parental desire for names with historical depth and cultural specificity.

**Arianna** (air-ee-AN-ə) — While primarily a girl's name, Arianna has been used in Celtic-inspired naming practice. The name's connection to the Celtic sun god Arianrhod makes it a notable example of mythological name revival.

**Brennan** (BREH-nən) — "Little raven" or "descendant of the raven." From the Irish *Breanainn*, a name borne by several early Irish saints. The name's popularity in English-speaking countries (#381 in 2023 SSA data) reflects the successful integration of Celtic names into mainstream naming culture.

**Caelan** (KAY-lən) — "Slender" or "fair." From the Irish *Caolán*, a diminutive of *caol* meaning "slender" or "narrow." The name's phonetic similarity to Celtic and non-Celtic names makes it cross-culturally accessible while retaining its Irish etymology.

**Dillan** (DIL-ən) — A variant of Dylan, with a spelling that reflects contemporary naming trends. The name's popularity (#547 in 2023 SSA data) demonstrates the continued appeal of Welsh nature names in English-speaking contexts.

**Eoin** (OH-in) — The Irish and Scottish Gaelic form of John, pronounced with a distinctive Gaelic vowel quality. Eoin's spelling signals its cultural origin more explicitly than the anglicized *John* or *Ewan*, making it a choice for parents seeking a name with clear Celtic identity.

**Finnegan** (FIN-ə-gən) — "Fair-headed" or "little fair one." From the Irish *Fionngheanainn*, a compound of *fionn* (fair, white) and *gean* (love, desire). The name's popularity (#423 in 2023 SSA data) reflects the trend toward longer, more distinctive Celtic names that offer nickname potential.

**Gavin** (GAV-in) — From the Welsh *Gawain*, a figure in Arthurian tradition. Gavin's popularity (#173 in 2023 SSA data) demonstrates the successful integration of Welsh names into mainstream English-speaking usage.

**Kellan** (KEL-ən) — "Slender" or "powerful." From the Irish *Ceallán*, a diminutive of *ceall* meaning "bright" or "warlike." The name's phonetic structure and Celtic etymology make it a viable choice for parents seeking a name that bridges Irish and contemporary usage.
